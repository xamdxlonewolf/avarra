# The Fair Hand

> **The fortune-and-contract faith of [[Strandoren]].** It has no catechism and distrusts anyone who offers one. What follows is assembled the way Settlers, the people who keep this faith, assemble doctrine: from working books, settled margins, and clauses copied often enough to become prayer.

In everyday speech the name is **the Fair Hand**. Their own written name is **Leddoren** (LEDD-or-en), from *ledd* and *oren*, "the witnessed account." Along the Ledger Coast the slang is **the Settling**, and the Motherfaith calls them **the Bargainers**. A Bound, a person who chose a grant and named the Terms of it, will say they have **a closed book** once that year's Terms are paid.

## Open three books

Open a quay-factor's book, a factor working the quay, and the line reads: *A grant without a term is a debt whose number the giver keeps.*

Open a Bound mason's book and the line reads: *Asked. Answered. Owed through rain-month. Closed.*

Open a Kept daughter's book, beneath a blank colour-line, the line where a colour would have been entered, and it reads: *Nothing granted. Nothing owed. My mother still thinks this means nothing happened.*

No one entry speaks for the Fair Hand, and together they are closer than an official creed would be.

### What can be settled

This is a settling-religion, and it is not a salvation-religion and not a gift-religion. No one is being rescued, and no one is being *given* something they cannot refuse, because a gift you cannot put down is an imposition. The founding ethic is **fair terms**: know what you owe, pay it, and walk.

What makes you of the Fair Hand is the orthopraxy of the book, which means you *keep accounts honestly*, the Tithe and the contract and the witness, and not that you deny the Mother in the street. Many Settlers still bring a child to the Tree, and they attend as witnesses to a signing.

On the divine character, the faith splits over whether anyone sits across the table as a counterparty, or whether no one does. That split is written out in the facing margin below, and both branches agree the *table* is real. The Leaf-Mother, if she exists, is a giver who will not show her terms, which, to a Settler, is the one unforgivable clause.

The mark of a Settler is a small personal **book**, and it is not a census, because a census is Threnmaieth's sin. In the book a Settler writes colour, Tithe, and what they are owed. The Bound did not invent this book; they are simply the ones whose book is also a contract.

### What cannot be settled

The Fair Hand holds that nothing is given, and that the Leaf-Fall is a *striking of terms*: the colour is the grant, the Tithe is the price, and the town's need is the other side of the deal. "Tend what you're given" is how a creditor teaches you to be grateful for the interest.

The Bound are how the Fair Hand proves the model: a Bound *chose*, named a grant, named Terms, and named a Pledge. The Fair Hand says this is not a special horror — it is the same grammar as every becoming, written large enough to read. The Motherfaith's fear of the Bound is, to them, embarrassment: the Bound make the ledger visible.

The Kept, on this reading, declined or were not offered: no bargain was taken, nothing is owed, and nothing is owed *to* them, which the faith holds as a cooler dignity than spared-or-slighted. On Lestrand it rhymes with calling the Kept *the free* ([[Naming in the Turning]]). The Fair Hand did not invent that slight; they gave it a theology.

The Struck, they say, signed later, in a storm, because extremity is when people accept bad terms. A drowning, an oath, a death: of course the book opens then. The Struck are not suspects; they are people who bargained without a clerk. The faith's kindness is to *settle the year fairly* after the fact, with a hall or a warrant, a vouching-as-novation in which someone vouches and the civic year is taken up in place of the bargain that had no clerk. A [[Bound]]'s *Terms* are the exception: those belong to the Counterparty, the other party named in the Bound's own bargain, and not to a clerk. The cruelty is the same motion: a Struck who cannot settle fades, and the quay steps around them.

What they mean by grace is a closed book, and it is not forgiveness and not a mother's lap. The sentence they trust is the one they can say in a breath: you paid, you are even, walk.

## Marks you can see

The signing-watch is what Settlers make of the Leaf-Fall. They attend as *witnesses*, not as petitioners, and they do not speak the Tender's blessing, the words the Motherfaith speaks over the child. They speak, if they speak:

> *"Grant and term, seen and spoken. The book is open. Keep it fair."*

Public settling is the Tithe paid in a way a neighbour can see, and the act is *clearing*, not confession. A Bound's oath-day is the high holy day: the Terms are recited, and the year's book is closed or marked owing. The Motherfaith calls this ostentation, and the Fair Hand calls it the only honest worship.

Quay-blessings are short, worn, and said in an eroded tongue, the phrase rubbed down by use: *fair wind, fair term.* People say them over a hull, a contract, a marriage. The sacred and the commercial use the same sentence, which is the point.

The personal book holds the colour, the Tithe, the debts, and what the year owes you. It is private, and it is not the [[Naming in the Turning|Threnhael]], the crown's roll of what people became. A Settler will die to keep a crown-clerk out of it. That refusal is the Fair Hand's one shared fury with every other faith on that subject.

## The hands that keep other books

The working house is [[The Book-Hands]], and their office is easier to find than their authority.

**Book-hands**, also called **Settlers**, are notaries who are also theologians. They witness signings, they teach children how to keep a book, and they settle a Struck's *civic* year with a hall or a warrant. They recite a [[Bound]]'s Terms on oath-day, and they do **not** rewrite those Terms, because that clause belongs to the Counterparty and not to a clerk. On Lestrand they are often the same people as the commercial clerks, so the faith *is* the profession, worn lightly.

The Fair Hand has no seat and many tables: guild-rooms and quay-shrines rather than a mother-church, and a "high house" in a rich port is just the oldest book-room. There is no membership roll, because a roll would be a Threnhael, and two book-hands may refuse each other's settlements and still borrow ink.

## The argument in the facing margin

Settlers do not form two branches, because they write over one another in the same book.

> **Recto:** Something sits across the table — a mind, a power, a hand that is fair rather than mothering. The Bound prove that an answer has an address.
>
> **Verso, in another ink:** A book does not require a reader on the far side. Rain settles downhill without a creditor.
>
> **Lower margin:** Then whom did you pay?
>
> **Trimmed edge:** Paid is paid.

What unites the inks is practical fury at a hidden term. That is why Settlers cannot join the Motherfaith even when they share a festival: "the Mother does not reach twice by chance" is, to them, a clause with no number on it.

## A page still in use

The page still in use is a preamble copied from a Bound's working contract, the form-words a Lestrand book-hand uses when the grant is ordinary and the Terms are not.

> *I was not given. I asked, and I was answered, and I named what I would pay. If a mother had wanted me she would have sent a leaf, not a clause. I prefer the clause. I can read it.*

It is a Bound's pride, a little cold, and it is the Fair Hand's whole case against gift-doctrine. The Bound who prefers the clause is sincere, because they have felt the cost and named it a price.

**Links (GM).** [[Is the Leaf-Mother Real]] · [[The Other Hands]].

They are wrong about the verb and right about the ache. Per the keystone, Conditions are real *gifts*, and no bargain was struck at the Leaf-Fall. The Fair Hand misreads a cost as a price. They have correctly felt that giving is not free, which is her constraint. Do not play them as devil-worship or as capitalism-cosplay, because they are as sincere as the Motherfaith.

The Counterparty is real for the Bound, and not for every becoming. Per [[The Other Hands]], *Orledd* answers sworn asks, and the face a Bound names, whether demon, vast, or unknown, is a mask. The Fair Hand is *right* about that door and *wrong* that the Leaf-Fall is a signing. Do not let this faith retcon the Mother's Gifts into bargains, and do not play Orledd as a devil.

The Ledger Coast's injustice is wearing vestments here. Calling the Kept "the free," and calling the fading "a book that would not close," sacralizes the market-fade from [[Polity Archetypes]]. A Tithe-poor Settler who cannot close the book is still a person the rich city steps around.

The personal book and the Threnhael come from the same impulse, the wish to write the becoming down, and from opposite ethics, mine against the crown's. A campaign that steals, forges, or subpoenas a Settler's book is stealing a soul in *their* language. That is useful at the table, and it is easy to play as mere paperwork if you aren't careful.
