# The Child Who Counted Stones

> **A road-song with numbers left out of it.** People sing it. They do not recite it, and no two miles of the road keep the same gaps. The count carries distance and graves in the same breath, and it does not stop to tell you which line is a mile and which line is a person.

## The counting song

The leader sings a number, and the line of walkers answers with whatever that number bought.

> **One** for water.<br>
> **Two** for shade.<br>
> **Three** for the woman who knew the ford.<br>
> **Four** for bread.<br>
> **Six** for the boy whose ribbon stayed.

The first time they hear it, children object that five is missing, and the line answers **Keep your breath.**

The song goes on. A number may buy a dry bed, or a ferryman's lie, or a shoe that had to be stitched twice, or a name that nobody at the next stone knows. Some of the verses are useful for getting along the road, some of them are the names of people, and most of the verses never tell you which of those they are.

When the line reaches a mile-shrine, the leader skips the number that is cut into the stone. At an unmarked grave, the leader sings one number twice. If somebody challenges that, the leader says **Keep your breath** and keeps walking.

The origin has no number in the song, and neither does the Leaf-Fall, the day a child is brought to a Tree. The line arrives, stands, and begins the song again on the road home:

> **One** for water.<br>
> **Two** for shade.<br>
> **Four** for bread.<br>
> **Five** for the ribbon we left on the way.

Children object that five was the number missing before, and this time the line answers **Then carry it.**

## How the song is used

A practical leader sings the verses about water and shelter in order to pace the day, so the song is also a way of knowing how far the line has come. A bereaved household puts a name into the song once, and if that verse is still being sung after three walks, the name has become part of the geography. Crews on a quay count the tides instead of the stones, and they leave long silences in the places where an inland line would be waiting for a number. Children in a Hands-town, a town that already Turns its own at a local Tree, use the song for skipping games. People who are still walking dislike hearing it played that way, until they notice which numbers the children have left out.

## GM Notes (the buried truth)

*(Stripped on player-safe export. Keyed to the keystone via `reveals: [keystone-adjacent]`.)*

- **The paradox sits in the numbers that are missing.** The road wants a count it can trust, and mourning is what breaks that count and then repairs it. A name can become a direction you walk by without stopping to be a name.
- **There is no official colour for the ribbons.** A copper-green rag on a mile-shrine is not a Taken-In. If a warden reads those ribbons as if they were leaf-colours, that warden is laying a Hands-era habit over a Walking-era rag. That misreading is useful when you want an NPC to be wrong. Do not make it into doctrine.
- **Sing it while people are moving.** Change a verse when the party passes a stone that has been marked. Let an NPC refuse to explain a missing number until the line has made camp. The song should cost the singers their breath.
