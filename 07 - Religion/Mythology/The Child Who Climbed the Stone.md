# The Child Who Climbed the Stone

> **A square-game that people later mistake for a fable.** The learned title is *The Child Who Climbed the Stone*. Children call it **Here and Far**. It has no fixed narrator, no doctrinal endings, and no Long-Lived witness, no person of that long life who can swear they saw the first afternoon. A town can play it only if an old mile-stone, one of the markers that counted the road, still stands where a local Tree, the town's Turning Tree, later made a square.

## Here and Far

One child stands on the mile-stone and is **Here**, and everyone else waits at the edge of the square and is **Far**. Far calls **How many days?**, and Here must answer **None. The Tree is here.**

Far then takes one paving-stone forward and names something that is still being carried from the road. The things they are allowed to name are the old cargo:

> A bed kept empty.<br>
> A cup set before dawn.<br>
> A ribbon gone white.<br>
> A child sent to the neighbour.

Here may answer only **Already here.** If Here laughs, or repeats a thing that has already been named, or steps down off the stone, the nearest child takes the stone.

The game is meant to end when Far reaches the marker. It rarely gets that far, because the list of what people are still carrying grows faster than the square is wide. Children borrow grievances from the adults, invent cargo that nobody could have brought, and argue over whether *a grave* and *a name* are one thing or two. Shopkeepers walk through the line, carts break it up, and the child on the stone keeps shouting **Already here** at people who are plainly on their way somewhere else.

There is one rule the adults never teach, and every square already knows it. When a real walking household, people who are still carrying their lives on the road, crosses the game, play stops. Here gets down, the children move the marker aside so the packs can pass, and afterward they put the marker back, sometimes into the same socket it came out of.

## What survives outside the square

A Road-hand, passing along the road, calls it courtesy when the children move the marker, and a paving clerk, who keeps the stones of the square, calls it vandalism when the children put the marker back. In a town with no waiting neighbours, no one still owed a journey to a Tree, the children run out of true things and start naming the weather. Past the ford, where the game has no stone at its centre, **Here** stands wherever the canopy can be seen, and nobody agrees whether that placement is mockery. Adults only tell the game as a story once they have forgotten how to lose it.

## GM Notes (the buried truth)

*(Stripped on player-safe export. Keyed to the keystone via `reveals: [keystone-adjacent]`.)*

- **The paradox is played, not explained.** The marker is obsolete enough to be in a cart's way, and current enough that the game stops when walkers arrive. Moving the marker and putting it back are both ordinary things people do. They are not two morals competing for the square.
- **Use it inside a scene.** Let a chase cut through the line, let a player character be handed Here, or let a walking family arrive while the party is still listening. The square will supply the voices, and you will not need an NPC to recite a doctrine.
- **There is no official ending.** The thing that interrupts the game is the ending. Do not add a verdict from the Mother, from the Seat, or from the GM.
