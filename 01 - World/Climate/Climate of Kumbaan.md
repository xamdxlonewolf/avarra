# Climate of Kumbaan

Kumbaan is mild, wet, and cloudy, and it turns bright by night when the storm-wall opens a view of the moon. The storm-wall is the wind, the current, the reef, and the surf working together at the edge of the island. It is not weather happening somewhere past the last beach. It is the climate that shapes every shore, terrace, peat cut, roof, hall, and story of arrival.

There is no Tree here, and no graft crossed. Dead wood is not a Tree, and a wrecked mainland pot has no ecology beyond rot, fuel if anyone will burn it, and the discomfort of knowing why it was carried.

## At the table

A week on the isle is mild rain under low cloud, one bright moonlit night, reef thunder that arrives without any local wind, and then a wall-squall that closes the shallows and soaks the peat stacks. People eat barley broth, oats, beans, peas, cabbage, turnips, onions, and goat milk or cheese, and the shore adds mussels, limpets, fish, and seaweed, with berries, mushrooms, and hazel nuts in season. What comes into a scene is a terrace wall slumping, unseen hands bringing wet baskets in, shellfish sickness after a storm, a peat fire smoking through a day-room, a wreck adding guests to a hall, or goats loose above the turf. The unseen hands are the close help of the isle: they can hold a basket, lift a light net, fetch a line, gather limpets within reach, and carry a load above the wash. They do not swim the wall, haul a wreck alone, or make a poisoned bed safe.

## Moons are dates; weather is what happened

Yumboes, the people of the isle, keep twelve named moons. They do not align them to the mainland's Deep, Seed-month, or Turning-month. Islanders date a rain, a birth, a debt, or a second sitting by the moon, and they describe the season by its work: ditches full, terraces open, shallows closed, peat drying, goats brought in. A mainlander can call a week springlike or autumnal and be understood. They cannot convert Kumbaan's calendar by picking whichever mainland month has similar rain.

Long wet stretches bring mild rain, full springs, saturated terraces, and damp halls, and nobody promises that every wet moon belongs to the same place in a mainland season. Clearer stretches give several workable days, drying peat, field repair, and safer shallows, and they still do not promise a scheduled path through the wall. Bright-night stretches are when the cloud breaks after dusk and surface work, fishing, and visits increase, which does not mean a named moon always brings a clear sky. Hard wall weather is reef thunder, salt wind, squalls that reach inland, and wrecks that are possible, and the violence of the wall does not mean a crossing has opened or closed in any way you could write down ahead of time.

## The storm-wall

At the edge, wind, current, reef, and surf work together. Inside the island the same weather arrives diminished and still persistent: cloud, rain, salt carried farther inland than a guest expects, and a low thunder that can keep going under a hill sky that looks calm. A wall-week starts as a long swell and a cloud bank, with a strong current, while shore-sitters watch and the terrace workers clear the drains before the rain arrives. Then the reef thunders and the rain comes, the shallows close, fishers bring the gear up above the tide, and peat and hay go under cover. A bright night can open the cloud inland while the wall is still flashing, and that is when Yumboes work the surface. A mainland guest may mistake the beauty for safety. A wall-squall carries wind as far as the Paps, the hills above the shore, with salt in the rain, and terrace faces slump, roof turf lifts, and goats go looking for gaps in the stone walls. When the rain eases, the surf stays confused, and shellfish beds and wreck-lines can be reached if people are careful, though the crossing is no more bookable than it was before.

The wall will not give anyone a time to cross. A quiet shore does not mean the outer reef is passable, a violent night can put a living wreck on the sand, and neither supplies a timetable.

Shore-sitters, the people who wait that beach, do not keep rescue boats and do not swim the wall. They decide alive after the sea has already made its decision.

## Shores and shallows

The inhabited shore is beach, rock shelf, reef-cut pool, salt grass, and paths that climb toward the hills, and there are no mainland quays. Mussels, limpets, cockles, and the other shellfish are gathered at tides people already know are safe. Fish are caught in the shallows when the wall weather allows it. Seaweed is eaten, dried, used in broth, and carried uphill to manure the terraces. Eggs and shore greens supplement the basket. Wreck stores are windfalls and risks at the same time: spoiled grain, strange sickness, damaged casks, useful rope.

The shallows are generous only to people who know them. Storms shift the sand, break the markers, foul the shellfish, and leave pools cut off from clean water. Unseen hands can do the close work of a shore, the basket and the net and the limpets within reach, and they still cannot swim the wall, haul a wreck alone, or make a poisoned bed safe.

The first safe low water after three wet days means every hall wants shellfish and weed at once. A wreck-line puts rope, timber, bodies, grain, and disease on the same tide. When shellfish make one hearth sick, the hall closes that known bed or accepts a leaner table. A mainland object that washes up is material first, and it becomes a story only after somebody carries it uphill.

### Food from the edge

The basket from the edge is shellfish taken at a known safe tide, fish from the shallows when the wall allows, seaweed for the pot and for the terraces, eggs and shore greens, and whatever a wreck has left that is still fit to argue over.

## Paps, terraces, and grazing

The hills, which people call the Paps, carry pasture and terrace-gardens on the surface. Halls, hearths, stores, and day-rooms lie under the turf. Rain runs down every mistake in a terrace wall. Oats and barley are the cool, wet-tolerant staples, and the grain stores well, until lodging, wind, or a harvest rain spoils the heads before anyone can dry them. Peas and beans are food that dries for the halls and belongs in the terrace rotation, and a wet planting loses them to saturated roots and slugs. Cabbage and turnips are the hardy food through the weeks when the shallows are closed, and they rot if the soil or the store is waterlogged. Onions and greens grow in small beds near the halls and get used often, and a salt rain burns the leaves. Hay and rough pasture are the winter and storm fodder for goats and sheep, and a wet cut heats or moulds once it is under cover. Hazel and berry edges give food, rods, fuel, and a hedge to break the wind, and a gale strips the fruit, while overgathering leaves no cache for winter.

Terraces are what keep good soil from becoming shore silt, so their upkeep is food work, flood work, and neighbour work at the same time. A failed wall can bury one garden and feed the seaweed with the soil of three families.

Goats are common around [[Ndenjoo]] and the other halls. They give milk, cheese, meat, hide, and dung, they keep the brush down, and they have a talent for undoing a terrace repair the moment it is finished. Small sheep provide wool and milk where the pasture holds, chickens and geese fit the sheltered yards, and dogs guard and herd. Large draught animals are uncommon in country this steep and this narrow. People, goats, small carts on the good paths, and unseen hands doing light close work carry most of the loads.

## Peat, springs, and life below

Peat is cut from wet ground, dried whenever cloud and wind permit, and stored above the damp. It heats a hall without anyone needing a large woodland. It also fills a poorly vented room with smoke, and a stack that never dried is useless.

Hill-springs and caught rain are the drinking water. The good order is the one halls already keep: drinking water sits above the washing, waste goes downhill from the springs, terrace drains run away from the hall roofs, peat and bedding stay off the wet floor, and the day-rooms are aired when the weather and the sleeping both allow it.

The nocturnal day reverses a mainland guest's instincts, so bright night is the time for work, visiting, repair, fishing, and feast, and daylight is for sleep in the cool rooms. A hammer at noon can be as inconsiderate as shouting through a mainland midnight.

Steady rain inside a hill is drips found by touch, dishes moved, and unseen hands holding bowls under the leaks. A bright night means doors open, bedding aired, and the terrace and shore work actually done. A hard wall wind means the turf gets inspected, the smoke is pushed in ways no one predicted, and the animals are brought close. Several calm wet days in a row mean the peat refuses to dry, and mould and winter lung become the worry.

## Forage without invention

A terrace edge offers nettles, sorrel, escaped peas, onions, and berries, and that edge usually belongs to the hall that built the wall. A hazel hedge offers nuts, mushrooms, and fallen twigs, and the winter cache and the windbreak matter more than any one meal taken out of them. A wet hill offers mushrooms, watercress at a spring that is sound, and peat, which is fuel rather than food, and the limits are the water quality and the footing. The shore offers shellfish, seaweed, fish, and eggs, limited by the tide, the wall weather, who already holds that stretch, and whether the bed has fouled. A wreck-line offers rope, timber, sealed stores, and sometimes livestock, along with spoilage, injury, strangers' claims, and sickness.

Unseen hands make close gathering safer and faster, and they do not create abundance. A great table after a poor harvest means every hearth contributed, the stores were opened, the guests arrived with food, or the meal is thinner than the serving makes it look.

## Disease ecology of an island hall

Kumbaan's isolation prevents the ordinary constant import of mainland sickness and makes each wreck a sharper contact. A wreck cough or a fever arrives with wet, exhausted survivors in a closed hall. The response is to warm them, feed them, and then give them one hearth-space and air the bedding separately. What is scarce is a specialist who would recognize a mainland illness. Flux comes from a fouled spring, runoff into the catchment, or simply too many guests, and the hall closes one draw, boils the water, repairs the drains, and carries water from another spring, if it still has dry fuel and enough vessels. Shellfish sickness comes off a bed the storm fouled, or a bed that was unfamiliar. People stop taking from that shore and fall back on grain and goat stores, and the lack is replacement protein while the shallows stay closed.

Winter lung is peat smoke, damp bedding, and crowded day-rooms. Halls clear the vents, dry the bedding on bright nights, and move the sleepers, and what they need is dry weather and an empty room. Wound-rot follows a reef cut, a terrace collapse, or an animal injury. The care is clean water, a drain, a dressing, and rest. A Stilled surgeon is scarce, and so is clean light work in a hall that is already crowded. The [[The Stilled|Stilled]] are people whose upkeep is exertion, and enforced rest in a day-room can let the Grey settle in them, which is a stillness in the body and not the name of the old mainland epidemic. Marsh ague comes with the biting insects near wet peat ground. People move the beds, cover the water, and drain beside the dwellings, and the labour has to happen in the same short drying weather the fields also need.

The Grey Summer is a mainland date, not a Kumbaan epidemic, though a wreck-survivor may bring the name and the fear. Islanders judge the cough in front of them.

There is no local Leaf-Fall, the giving that happens at a Tree, to supply Given Stilled, and the [[Returned]] are only ever Struck, never Given at that Leaf-Fall. Either Condition is rare here. A Condition is a change a person lives with, and on this isle the usual work is done by ordinary healers, hosts, unseen hands, clean water, separation, and time. Hospitality requires care, and it does not require putting every wet stranger in the same sleeping room.

## Tithe-ecology without a Tree

Kumbaan supplies no Given path, because there is no local Leaf-Fall, and Struck are rare. A visitor, or a Yumboe returning from the mainland, may still carry a Condition acquired over there. The table knows how to keep a changed guest or a changed islander whole without turning the Condition into a foreign office.

A [[The Taken-In|Taken-In]] person, who has to stay near living plants, finds terraces, pasture, hedges, and potted greens, and can still be cut off by a wreck quarantine or a long stay in a stone shore-shelter. A Stilled person finds exertion in terrace repair, peat cutting, climbing, dancing, and hauling, and an injury that enforces day-room rest can let the Grey settle, with surgical training scarce besides. A [[Far-Voiced]] person, who needs somewhere to put the voice, finds the feast, the work-song, the shore warning, and the open night. A Cry in a sleeping hill at noon is a serious problem between neighbours. A Returned person's Purpose may be served by the aftermath of a rescue, by tending the wreck-dead, or by a search they chose. The island does not assign that Purpose. A person who needs passage in order to act toward it may be unable to act at all.

A [[Two-Bodied]] visitor, or a returning islander, can range, herd, climb, fly, or swim in a real animal self, and the tight halls and the storm-closed paths on the surface constrain how long that body can be used. An [[The Answered|Answered]] person, whose upkeep is earnest craft in a material, finds terrace stone, peat fire, shore water, and wall wind. The material is present, and a workshop and the teachers may not be. A [[Long-Lived]] newcomer can learn things here that are genuinely new, and months later, treating Yumboe life as an endless curiosity is neither learning nor courtesy.

Green, exercise, voice, work, and company are furnished as household facts when they can be. Novelty is not a crop, and islanders are not provisions for a visitor's Tithe.

## Beasts as livelihood

Goats provide milk, cheese, meat, hide, dung, and the browse of rough slopes. Small sheep provide wool, milk, meat, and manure on the gentler pasture. Chickens and geese provide eggs and meat close to the halls. Dogs herd, guard the stores, and find a path in low cloud. Cats keep a grain store from becoming a mouse store. Bees use the sheltered clear stretches, and honey and wax are valued because wet cloudy days limit how often they can fly. The fish and shellfish of the shallows are a food system, not adversaries.

Unseen hands work beside these animals, holding gates and baskets, and they do not milk a valley unseen while everyone feasts.

## The one thing the wall does not explain

On some nights later remembered as a thinning, peat smoke from the hill vents lies seaward while the grass and the rain move inland. Shore-sitters notice after the wreck or the sail. They do not use it to call one beforehand.

Bad vents, warm turf, crossed gusts, and the way memory works after danger explain the smoke well enough for any single hall. The smoke remains an observation, not a crossing calendar.

## GM Notes

No Tree means no Tree: there is no graft, no hidden root, no storm-grown Hand, and no dead pot that wakes. Kumbaan ecology is terrace, peat, shallows, halls, livestock, and unseen-handed labour.

The smoke is the one thing this note does not explain. People remember it after the fact, and they remember it badly, so it cannot schedule a crossing or show a safe route.

Player text can observe that there is no Tree and no Given system. It does not explain the nature of the limit, and it does not expose a hidden household. That is the keystone discipline on this page.

"Near-none" on the gradient is the reach column, meaning there is no Giving system. It is not a hedge that a Tree might be here. Player geography matches the categorical lock: no Tree, and never a graft. The log of that correction is [[Contradictions]] C-06.
