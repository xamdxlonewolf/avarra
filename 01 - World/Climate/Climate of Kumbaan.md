# Climate of Kumbaan

Kumbaan is mild, wet, cloudy, and bright by night when the wall opens a view of the moon. The storm-wall is not weather beyond the island. It is the climate that shapes every shore, terrace, peat cut, roof, hall, and story of arrival.

There is no Tree here. No graft crossed. Dead wood is not a Tree, and a wrecked mainland pot has no ecology beyond rot, fuel if anyone will burn it, and the discomfort of knowing why it was carried.

## At the table

| Ask | Use this |
|---|---|
| **Weather this week** | Mild rain under low cloud; one bright moonlit night; reef thunder without local wind; then a wall-squall that closes the shallows and soaks the peat stacks. |
| **What you can eat** | Barley broth, oats, beans, peas, cabbage, turnips, onions, goat milk or cheese; mussels, limpets, fish, seaweed, berries, mushrooms, and hazel nuts in season. |
| **What can enter a scene** | A terrace wall slumping; unseen hands bringing wet baskets in; shellfish sickness after a storm; a peat fire smoking through a day-room; a wreck adding mouths to a hall; goats loose above the turf. |

## Moons are dates; weather is what happened

Yumboes keep twelve named moons. They do not align them to the mainland's Deep, Seed-month, or Turning-month. Islanders date a rain, birth, debt, or second sitting by the moon and describe the season by its work: ditches full, terraces open, shallows closed, peat drying, goats brought in.

| Part of the island year | What often happens | What nobody promises |
|---|---|---|
| Long wet stretches | Mild rain, full springs, saturated terraces, damp halls | That every wet moon belongs to the same place in a mainland season |
| Clearer stretches | Several workable days, drying peat, field repair, safer shallows | A scheduled path through the wall |
| Bright-night stretches | Cloud breaks after dusk; surface work, fishing, and visits increase | That a named moon always brings clear sky |
| Hard wall weather | Reef thunder, salt wind, squalls reaching inland, wrecks possible | That wall violence means a crossing has opened or closed in any predictable way |

A mainlander can call a week springlike or autumnal and be understood. They cannot convert Kumbaan's calendar by picking whichever mainland month has similar rain.

## The storm-wall

At the edge, wind, current, reef, and surf work together. Inside, the same weather arrives diminished but persistent: cloud, rain, salt carried farther than expected, and a low thunder that may continue under a calm hill sky.

### A wall-week

| Watch | At the reef | Inland consequence |
|---|---|---|
| First | Long swell, cloud bank, strong current | Shore-sitters watch. Terrace workers clear drains before rain arrives. |
| Second | Reef thunder and rain | Shallows close. Fishers bring gear above the tide. Peat and hay go under cover. |
| Bright night | Cloud opens inland while the wall still flashes | Yumboes work the surface. A mainland guest may mistake beauty for safety. |
| Wall-squall | Wind reaches the Paps, salt in the rain | Terrace faces slump; roof turf lifts; goats seek gaps in stone walls. |
| Clearing | Rain eases, surf remains confused | Shellfish beds and wreck-lines can be reached cautiously. The crossing is no more bookable than before. |

The wall is a dial for a crossing, not a ferry bell. A quiet shore does not mean the outer reef is passable. A violent night can put a living wreck on the sand. Neither supplies a timetable.

Shore-sitters do not keep rescue boats and do not swim the wall. They decide alive after the sea has already made its decision.

## Shores and shallows

The inhabited shore is beach, rock shelf, reef-cut pool, salt grass, and paths climbing toward the hills. There are no mainland quays.

### Food from the edge

- Mussels, limpets, cockles, and other shellfish are gathered at known safe tides.
- Fish are caught in the shallows when wall weather allows.
- Seaweed is eaten, dried, used in broth, and carried uphill for terrace manure.
- Eggs and shore greens supplement the basket.
- Wreck stores are windfalls and risks: spoiled grain, strange sickness, damaged casks, useful rope.

The shallows are generous only to people who know them. Storms shift sand, break markers, foul shellfish, and leave pools cut off from clean water.

Unseen hands can hold a basket, lift a light net, fetch line, gather limpets within reach, and carry a load above the wash. They do not swim the wall, haul a wreck alone, or make a poisoned bed safe.

### A shore scene

| Ordinary event | Pressure |
|---|---|
| First safe low water after three wet days | Every hall wants shellfish and weed at once |
| A wreck-line appears | Rope, timber, bodies, grain, and disease share the same tide |
| Shellfish make one hearth sick | Close a known bed or risk a leaner table |
| A mainland object washes up | It is material first and a story only after somebody carries it uphill |

## Paps, terraces, and grazing

The hills carry pasture and terrace-gardens on the surface; halls, hearths, stores, and day-rooms lie under turf. Rain runs down every mistake in a terrace wall.

### What grows

| Crop or use | Why it fits | Bad-week failure |
|---|---|---|
| Oats and barley | Cool, wet-tolerant staples; grain stores well | Lodging, wind, or a harvest rain spoils heads before drying |
| Peas and beans | Food dried for halls; useful in terrace rotation | Saturated roots and slugs take a wet planting |
| Cabbage and turnips | Hardy food through closed-shallows weeks | Rot in waterlogged soil or stores |
| Onions and greens | Small beds near halls; frequent use | Salt rain burns leaves |
| Hay and rough pasture | Winter and storm fodder for goats and sheep | A wet cut heats or moulds under cover |
| Hazel and berry edges | Food, rods, fuel, and hedge shelter | Gale strips fruit; overgathering leaves no winter cache |

Terraces prevent good soil from becoming shore silt. Their upkeep is therefore food work, flood work, and neighbour work at once. A failed wall can bury one garden and feed seaweed with the soil of three families.

Goats are common around [[Ndenjoo]] and other halls: milk, cheese, meat, hide, dung, brush control, and a talent for undoing terrace repair. Small sheep provide wool and milk where pasture holds. Chickens and geese fit sheltered yards. Dogs guard and herd.

Large draught animals are uncommon in steep, narrow terrace country. People, goats, small carts on good paths, and unseen hands doing light close work carry most loads.

## Peat, springs, and life below

Peat is cut from wet ground, dried whenever cloud and wind permit, and stored above the damp. It heats halls without requiring large woodland. It also fills a poorly vented room with smoke and makes a wet stack useless.

Hill-springs and caught rain provide drinking water. The good order is familiar:

1. drinking water above washing;
2. waste downhill from springs;
3. terrace drains away from hall roofs;
4. peat and bedding kept off wet floor;
5. day-rooms aired when weather and sleep allow.

The nocturnal day reverses a mainland guest's instincts. Bright night is work, visiting, repair, fishing, and feast. Daylight is sleep in cool rooms. A hammer at noon can be as inconsiderate as shouting through a mainland midnight.

### Hall weather

| Outside | Inside the hill |
|---|---|
| Steady rain | Drips found by touch, dishes moved, unseen hands holding bowls under leaks |
| Bright night | Doors open, bedding aired, terrace and shore work done |
| Hard wall wind | Turf inspected, smoke pushed unpredictably, animals brought close |
| Several calm wet days | Peat refuses to dry; mould and winter lung become the worry |

## Forage without invention

| Where | Common finds | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Terrace edge | Nettles, sorrel, escaped peas, onions, berries | Usually belongs to the hall that built the wall |
| Hazel hedge | Nuts, mushrooms, fallen twigs | Winter cache and windbreak matter more than one meal |
| Wet hill | Mushrooms, watercress at a sound spring, peat | Water quality and footing; peat is fuel, not food |
| Shore | Shellfish, seaweed, fish, eggs | Tide, wall weather, ownership, and fouling |
| Wreck-line | Rope, timber, sealed stores, sometimes livestock | Spoilage, injury, strangers' claims, and sickness |

Unseen hands make close gathering safer and faster. They do not create abundance. A great table after a poor harvest means every hearth contributed, stores were opened, guests arrived with food, or the meal is thinner than its serving makes it look.

## Disease ecology of an island hall

Kumbaan's isolation prevents ordinary constant import and makes each wreck a sharper contact.

| Sickness | Route | Hall response | What is scarce |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wreck cough or fever | Wet exhausted survivors in a closed hall | Warm, feed, then give one hearth-space and air bedding separately | A specialist who recognizes a mainland illness |
| Flux | Fouled spring, runoff into catchment, too many guests | Close one draw, boil water, repair drains, carry from another spring | Dry fuel and enough vessels |
| Shellfish sickness | Storm-fouled or unfamiliar bed | Stop taking from that shore, use grain and goat stores | Replacement protein while shallows remain closed |
| Winter lung | Peat smoke, damp bedding, crowded day-rooms | Clear vents, dry bedding on bright nights, move sleepers | Dry weather and empty room |
| Wound-rot | Reef cuts, terrace collapse, animal injury | Wash with clean water, drain, dress, rest | A Stilled surgeon; clean light work in a crowded hall |
| Marsh ague | Biting insects near wet peat ground | Move beds, cover water, drain beside dwellings | Labour during the same short drying weather needed for fields |

The Grey Summer is a mainland date, not a Kumbaan epidemic. A wreck-survivor may bring the name and fear. Islanders judge the cough in front of them.

There is no local Leaf-Fall to supply Given Stilled, and Returned are only ever Struck. Either Condition is rare here. Usually ordinary healers, hosts, unseen hands, clean water, separation, and time do the work. Hospitality requires care; it does not require putting every wet stranger in the same sleeping room.

## Tithe-ecology without a Tree

Kumbaan supplies no Given path: there is no local Leaf-Fall. Struck are rare. A visitor or returning Yumboe may still carry a Condition acquired on the mainland. The table knows how to keep a changed guest or islander whole without turning the Condition into a foreign office.

| Condition present | Island fit | Friction |
|---|---|---|
| [[The Taken-In]] | Terraces, pasture, hedges, and potted greens provide living plants | A wreck quarantine or long stay in a stone shore-shelter can still cut them off |
| [[The Stilled]] | Terrace repair, peat cutting, climbing, dancing, and hauling provide exertion | Injury and enforced day-room rest can let Grey settle; surgical training is scarce |
| [[Far-Voiced]] | Feast, work-song, shore warning, and open night provide outlets | A Cry in a sleeping hill at noon is a serious neighbour problem |
| [[Returned]] | Rescue aftermath, tending wreck-dead, and a chosen search may serve Purpose | The island does not assign their Purpose; a person seeking passage may be unable to act toward it |
| [[Two-Bodied]] | A visitor or returning islander can range, herd, climb, fly, or swim in a real animal self | Tight halls and storm-closed surface paths constrain time in that body |
| [[The Answered]] | Terrace stone, peat fire, shore water, and wall wind offer earnest work | The material is present; a workshop and teachers may not be |
| [[Long-Lived]] | The island offers genuine new knowledge to a newcomer | Months later, treating Yumboe life as an endless curiosity is neither learning nor courtesy |

Green, exercise, voice, work, and company are furnished as household facts when they can be. Novelty is not a crop, and islanders are not provisions for a visitor's Tithe.

## Beasts as livelihood

- **Goats** provide milk, cheese, meat, hide, dung, and browse rough slopes.
- **Small sheep** provide wool, milk, meat, and manure on gentler pasture.
- **Chickens and geese** provide eggs and meat close to halls.
- **Dogs** herd, guard stores, and find a path in low cloud.
- **Cats** keep grain stores from becoming mouse stores.
- **Bees** use sheltered clear stretches; honey and wax are valued because wet cloudy days limit flight.
- **Shallow fish and shellfish** are food systems, not adversaries.

Unseen hands work beside these animals. They hold gates and baskets. They do not milk a valley unseen while everyone feasts.

## The one thing the wall does not explain

On some nights later remembered as a thinning, peat smoke from the hill vents lies seaward while the grass and rain move inland. Shore-sitters notice after the wreck or sail. They do not use it to call one beforehand.

Bad vents, warm turf, crossed gusts, and memory after danger explain it well enough for any single hall. The smoke remains an observation, not a crossing calendar.

## GM Notes

- **No Tree means no Tree.** No graft, no hidden root, no storm-grown Hand, no dead pot that wakes. Kumbaan ecology is terrace, peat, shallows, halls, livestock, and unseen-handed labour.
- **The smoke is the region's single inscrutable.** It is retrospective and unreliable. It cannot schedule a crossing or identify a safe route.
- **Keystone discipline:** player text can observe no Tree and no Given system. It does not explain the nature of the limit or expose a hidden household.
- **Reach-label vs Tree-count.** "Near-none" on the gradient is the *reach* column (no Giving system). It is not a hedge that a Tree might be here. Player geography now matches the categorical lock: no Tree, never a graft. Log: [[Contradictions]] C-06.
