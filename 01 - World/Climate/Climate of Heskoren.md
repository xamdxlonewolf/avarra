# Climate of Heskoren

Heskoren's single broad band is the Sundered upland: cool coast, rough moor, wooded vale, high spine, and long river country, with the weather changing as you move between them. Winters run harder than on the Old World pair, Maiethorn and Strandoren, and the kind season between frosts is shorter. A clear day is not background; it is when roofs, hay, carts, witnesses, and graft-tenders all move at once.

The continent is not empty wilderness. It is a patchwork of worked pockets around surviving Trees, the living grafts a town has managed to keep, and older hearths that predate them, separated by country too wet, steep, cold, or simply distant to hold densely.

## At the table

A week here is usually cold rain in a vale, sleet on a ridge, one brilliant dry day, and then a hard frost, and on the west coast a long swell can keep the next hull outside while everyone on shore spends the clear hours at once. The food is oat mash, rye bread, peas, cabbage, turnips, hard cheese, and smoked fish, with berries, hazel nuts, nettles, sorrel, and mushrooms when the season allows them. A first-meal bread at [[The First Bowl]] is a little sour. What walks into a scene is the road and the weather together: a Long Mile cart taking shelter, hay cut before the next rain, a wind-burned young graft, a camp fever at Eolvaeth, a pony mired between the mile-shrines that mark the road, a neighbour cohort with one dry day left, or the bowl set out at [[The First Bowl]].

## Reading the short kind season

Heskoren still uses the mainland months. The same date covers several seasons because elevation and exposure matter more here than a clerk's expectation. A coast can be opening its wet fields while a ridge is still holding snow, and both of them will call the month by the same name.

| Months in common speech | Coast and lower rivers | Vales and live-front pockets | Moor and high spine |
|---|---|---|---|
| **the Deep · Hearth-month · the Thaw / Liren** | Cold rain, sleet, gales, river ice in sheltered bends | Snow and frozen mud; roofs and fodder under pressure; thaw arrives unevenly | Deep snow, scoured ridges, paths hidden; Liren can bring avalanche and bog together |
| **Seed-month · Hale-month · Turning-month / Eolthael** | Wet fields open first; roads remain soft; coast hulls return | Sowing, lambing, roof repair, then crowded neighbour roads | Snow persists in shade; passes open late; a Hale-month frost can reset spring |
| **Gift-month · Ledger-month · Harvest** | Best fishing and coasting; hay and grain race the rain | Gardens, oats, rye, roots, berries, and the brief surplus season | Grazing and repair; nights sharpen by Ledger-month |
| **the Tally · the Wait · Dark-month** | Storms increase; fish and peat stores become public concern | Harvest ends fast; animals come close; first frost exposes weak roofs | Snow closes tracks from the top down; the next mile-shrine becomes uncertain |

## The frontier coast

The coast faces the West Water back toward [[Strandoren]]. It is the way in for iron, salt, craft goods, specialists, letters, and living grafts, and the way out for timber, wool, hides, fish, and raw staples. Where the salt wind is broken, the ground grows oats, rye, barley, cabbage, turnips, peas, and hay, and sheep and cattle take the rough pasture. Fish, mussels, limpets, seaweed, and eels supplement the grain, and peat and managed woodland are what people burn.

Storms make ordinary shortages stack, and a hull may carry a stiller, a Stilled traveller whose upkeep is hard use of the body and who can do the surgery, together with a page a Long-Lived has not read. A Long-Lived is a person who has to keep meeting something they did not already know, so that unread page is part of how they stay well, and a winter store cannot issue novelty the way it issues grain. The same hull may also carry seed grain and a Road-hand's living pot, the earth a young graft is still alive in. Losing it is not one kind of loss.

### A week of coast weather

The week tends to open under low cloud and a workable surf, spend its one clear cold day on every roof and net, and then shut the next hull out on a west rain and a rising swell. When the wind falls, the river can still be high enough that ships land before the carts can leave, and food, graft earth, and passengers end up competing for the same dry room.

| Day-shape | Weather | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | Low cloud, steady wind, workable surf | Fish comes in. Cargo waits on whether the inland road has dried. |
| Middle | One clear cold day | Every roof, net, cart, and drying rack is in use. A Stilled visitor gets three requests before breakfast. |
| Turn | West rain and rising swell | The next hull stands off. The quay runs out of covered space. |
| After | Wind falls; river remains high | Ships can land before carts can leave. Food, graft earth, and passengers compete for dry storage. |

## Vales and river pockets

Sheltered vales carry the continent's deeper fields. Rye, oats, barley, peas, cabbage, turnips, and hay grow on the bottoms and the slopes that stay kind, and apples take the warm slopes, which is why an apple is a vale pride and not a thing the whole continent expects. Cattle and oxen fit the broad bottoms, sheep and goats take the climb, and pigs use the woodland mast under rights the people there already hold.

A flood week is silt and, often enough, flux, the sickness that follows water that waste has reached. The useful response is clean drinking water, drainage, waste kept separate from the drink, and food. A Tide-Answered person can improve the flow, and a Root-Answered person can cut a drain, because that is the earnest craft their Tithe already is. Neither makes fouled water safe by declaration.

### A week of vale weather

Winter turns snow to rain and then freezes it again at night, so the pressure sits on roofs, fodder, firewood, and the path to a living evergreen. Spring can put the river up under a sky that is clear where you are standing, and the ford, the seed already in the low soil, the waste pits, and the neighbour road all feel it. Summer may give three dry days and then a heavy rain onto hay that is only half stacked, berries that are ripe, and a ditch through a camp-street. Autumn can bring the first hard frost before the harvest is finished, with roots still in the ground, apples still on the boughs, and a Tree-tender choosing what to cover.

## Moor and spine

The high country is wind, peat, short grass, heather, rock, and snow holding late in the shade. This is not Maiethorn's Rain-Wall. It has no Lirorn thaw-levy and no Shelf-gate. What it does have is a dawn that can be frost or sleet with a strong crosswind, a midday when the sun reaches one slope and everyone has to decide whether to cross before the released snow refreezes, an evening when cloud lowers and the next ridge vanishes so that a known cairn matters more than speed, and a night of hard freeze or cold rain in which packs, boots, and animal bedding decide the morning.

Lower moors graze sheep, goats, and hardy ponies, and oats and barley grow in the sheltered folds. Bilberries, sorrel, mushrooms, and stream fish supplement the stores when the season allows. Peat is the fuel, and wet ground is a trap for cart wheels, hooves, and cellars.

Tengu and Fox-of-the-Snows hearths are denser on the heights because of terrain and history, not because any ridge is a Kind-state, a government of one born people. Mixed hearths use the same winter stores.

## Live-front Tree pockets

A Heskoren town often begins where a mortal graft survived, a living cutting carried in earth and planted, and keeping it alive is ordinary work that does not stop. The planting needs living earth kept alive through the Long Mile, the long road between these pockets, whose exact times stay on [[Named Ground]], and a young union, the graft's join with the new ground, needs shelter from wind and from grazing. Wet ground has to drain without drying the roots, snow has to be lifted off the vulnerable boughs, and bark damage has to be cleaned before rot enters. A dry summer still needs a share of the cistern, even on a continent this wet.

[[Harrow's Green]] is the fortunate version: a Hand mature enough to Turn, which is what people call a graft that can hold a cohort's standing, with neighbour hamlets still using its week. [[Eolvaeth]] is the waiting version, an older graft that never became certainty. Both are Heskoren ecology, and neither proves worth.

### How a weather loss becomes a generation

Wind or root-rot sickens a young Tree, and the wardens cannot safely hold that year's standing there, the gathering that had to happen under that wood. The cohort then has to reach another sound Tree during the same Turning-Week, and a flood, a stretch of mud, or a closed ford spends part of the week before they arrive. Those who miss are unTurned, and those who arrive still need a witness to walk home. The town must tend what it still has, replace it, or wait another generation for wood that can Turn. This is why the map follows surviving Trees. It is also why roads, guest-right, and weather knowledge are part of Tree care, because a soaked cohort still needs a roof, a road, and someone who knows whether the ford will be there in the morning.

## Food and forage

### What households plant

Oats tolerate the cool wet ground and feed both people and animals, and rye is what makes bread where the soil is poorer. Barley fits the shorter season and goes to brewing. Peas enrich a field and dry well enough to carry. Cabbage and turnips are what stand between a household and the end of winter. Hay is not human food and still decides whether milk, wool, and traction survive to spring. Apples grow only in sheltered vales and are therefore a local pride, not a continent-wide default.

### What travellers can gather

Spring gives nettles and sorrel near water that is sound, and eggs where taking them is permitted. Summer gives berries, mushrooms after rain, fish, and shellfish on the coast. Autumn gives hazel nuts, late berries, mushrooms, and fallen apples near old steadings. Winter gives very little beyond safe shore food and whatever was stored while the year still allowed it.

The frontier is land-rich, not automatically food-rich. A wet summer can grow grass and ruin hay. A wide forest can belong to a winter mast-right, the right to the nuts and mast the pigs and the household were already counting on. Forage should reveal who uses the ground, not erase the need for roads.

## Disease and care

The sicknesses that actually arrive are the ones a crowded Turning-month, a flooded well, a long cold room, a wet wound, a bog-edge camp, or a dirty cut on a Tree will produce. Camp fever comes out of camp-streets, crowded gift-halls, and road-houses, and the practice is to air the bedding, separate the hearths, and carry food to the sick. The [[Far-Voiced]], people who need a safe place to let the voice out, carry the warning. The [[Returned]] tend plague and the dead, which is work a Purpose can include and which a town's fear does not get to assign. A Stilled visitor becomes precious because a [[The Stilled|Stilled]] person can hold a crisis and, when the training is there, do the surgery, and there are few of them.

Flux follows a flooded well, a river camp, or waste sitting uphill of the water, and the work is to close the source, boil if the fuel allows, and move the waste. The [[The Taken-In|Taken-In]], who have to keep near living plants, are the ones who read the ground, and the Answered improve the drains and the heat. Winter lung comes from peat smoke, wet rooms, and the long cold season. People clear the smoke, dry the bedding, and open the rooms on the one fair day. The Stilled hold the crisis, and ordinary healers do the long tending.

Wound-rot follows an axe cut, an animal injury, wet feet, or a long road. The care is to wash, drain, dress, and rest, with a Stilled surgeon when one can be reached and the Returned where the work has become deadly. Marsh ague sits in bog-edge camps and standing summer water, and the answer is to move the beds, cover the jars, and drain near the houses, which is ditch-crew work, Taken-In work, and Root- and Tide-Answered work where those people are present. Root blight enters through wind wounds, standing water, and dirty cutting tools, so people isolate the wood, drain it, clean the tools, and remove the dead tissue. Tree-wardens and the Taken-In do that work, and there is no guaranteed cure.

The [[The Grey Summer|Grey Summer]] happened on the Old Crossing, not here. Heskoren inherited the caution through delayed hulls, travellers, and stories: isolate a coughing berth, and do not let one witness carry a fever through every neighbour's week. A local outbreak may be deadly without becoming a second named Grey.

There are fewer Stilled surgeons and fewer specialist infirmaries than in the Old World pair, so a travelling stiller is a summer's luck. Returned do the plague and body work people still need, and neither group should become an excuse to skip ordinary care or the journey to reach them.

## Tithe-ecology at thin reach

A Condition is a change a person lives with, and the Tithe is the upkeep that change keeps requiring. On this frontier the upkeep is often a neighbour's field, a gift-hall hour, a place by the fire, or permission to range. It is warm and real, and it also runs out. The Taken-In fit a country where living land is abundant, until winter, quarantine, bog loss, or a dead planting cuts them off from living green. The Stilled find herding, cutting, repair, surgery, and road work to spend the body on, and the hard edge is that training and surgical support are scarce, so an injury can enforce a dangerous stillness. The Far-Voiced have warning posts, worship, work-song, and open country, until an isolated posting or a sleeping hall that is already full leaves nowhere safe to vent. The Returned are needed for recovery, plague, mines, flood, and the tending of the dead, and a useful danger may still not be that person's Purpose. Frontier need does not own them.

The [[Two-Bodied]] make ranging, scouting, herding, courier work, and rescue into livelihoods, and a town may start treating a family's range as if it were public property. Confinement spends the other self. The [[The Answered|Answered]] are how drains, walls, kilns, ferries, roofs, and firebreaks get earnest craft where few guilds exist to send a second pair of hands. After the work, an idle Answered person can be stranded. A [[Long-Lived]] person can be taught by every new valley, custom, graft, and traveller, and isolation turns that discovery into repetition. Novelty cannot be issued from a winter store.

## Beasts as livelihood

Hardy ponies and mules carry Long Mile loads where a cart fails, and oxen work the broader vales and the timber roads. Sheep and goats turn rough slopes into wool, milk, meat, hide, and manure. Cattle use the sheltered grazing and make the winter hay a public concern. Pigs take the woodland mast near an established hearth. Chickens and geese fit a small yard or a camp-street. Dogs herd, guard, and find people when the weather has already gone bad. Bees survive in the sheltered pockets, and a failed blossom is read before the grain harvest, because the flowers tell on the year earlier than the grain does.

A Two-Bodied courier in a pony, hawk, wolf, otter, or other real animal body is still a person, not one of these animals pressed into use. Their other self can make a route possible and still argue against it.

## The one thing the frost does not explain

After the first hard snow around some young Heskoren Trees, a narrow melt runs back along the road by which the graft first arrived. It can lie on the shaded side while the open square stays white. People call it the old-road melt only when they need to give directions.

Packed earth, drainage, buried stone, or a tender's morning walk can explain any one example. No explanation accounts for enough of them to end the conversation. It does not point toward the next town and does not promise a Tree will take there.

## GM Notes

The sour first-meal bread at The First Bowl stays unexplained, so do not give it a cause.

What the player-facing pages can show is distance and history expressed through horticulture: young, sick, or scarce grafts, long carrying chains, and wind, soil, water, and care that arrived late. The real edge of that pattern stays unconfirmed.

The old-road melt is the one habit this note does not explain. It shows where the wood came from, and it does not show where wood should go, so do not turn it into a compass, a prophecy, or a voice.

The Kind-hearth discipline stays as it is: foxes in the woods and the snow, crows on the ridges, seals on the coasts. Terrain can make a hearth dense, and it never makes a Kind-government.
