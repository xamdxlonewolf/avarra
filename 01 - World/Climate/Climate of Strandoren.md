# Climate of Strandoren

Strandoren is maritime country: an indented coast, a tidal estuary, wet lowland, and a west face that watches open water. The sea softens the cold and refuses predictability in return. A week can begin with three working tides, lose two days to fog, and end with every crane tied down.

The coast and the [[Named Ground|Chart-run]], the interior river that brings another district's weather downstream to the estuary, carry food, people, living grafts, disease, and news through the same narrow mouths. High reach and mature Trees do not make the shore ecologically safe. They make it busy, because so much of what the country lives on has to pass those mouths in the same week.

## At the table

A week is salt mist and mild rain, one clear day for unloading, and estuary water rising after rain that fell inland. On the Night Shore, the open face on the West Water, a west front gives hard rain, a false lull, and a second wind. People eat rye or wheat bread, beans, cabbage, apples, and eel or herring, with mussels and cockles when the tide is safe, and oat pottage when the fleets stay tied. What enters a scene is a late Chart-run barge, brine in a rented leaf-lot, a coughing hull held outside the quay, a fish market closed by red water, or a Far-Voiced warning with no ship behind it. A leaf-lot is a rented plot of living green in a stone town. The [[Far-Voiced]] are people who have to put the voice somewhere, and a warning from one of them can arrive while the hull it refers to never does.

## Reading the year

The shore-lands and the Chart-run, the Night Shore, and the Old Crossing face share the mainland months and do not share a week. Through the Deep, Hearth-month, and the Thaw, also called Liren, the shore-lands get cold rain, an occasional frost, muddy fields, and river floods, while the Night Shore takes long gales, wet cold, high surf, and few days anyone will call reliable for sailing, and the Old Crossing face gets cold drizzle and working tides between squalls. Seed-month, Hale-month, and Turning-month, also called Eolthael, are early sowing on drained ground, blossom, warm rain, and long fair evenings inland of the salt. The Night Shore goes from fog to hard bright days and then to sudden weather out of the west. Hale-month storms decide who departs the Crossing, and Turning-Week fills every berth and every road.

Gift-month, Ledger-month, and Harvest are hay, grain, fruit, flax, and the busiest river traffic, the best coasting weather the Night Shore gets and still interrupted by fronts, and a Crossing on which fish, passengers, and market cargo compete for the same holds. The Tally, the Wait, and Dark-month close the harvest wet, put fog on the estuary, and bring the first frost to the fields. Gales return on the Night Shore, and the missing hulls become the season's public count. Rain and wind shorten the Crossing before winter closes the inland roads.

Hard frost is less reliable than in Maiethorn's core. That makes winter wet rather than kind: rot, mud, smoke, and bad bedding do more harm than deep snow.

## Shore-lands

Most settlements lie within reach of tide or estuary. Saltwater is the road, the food, the thing that carries waste, and the threat. Inland rain can flood a quay under a clear local sky, because the Chart-run brings another district's weather downstream. A week opens in mist and light rain on a gentle tide, so the fishing boats work, the carts reach the quay, and the washing does not dry. A bright day with a steady breeze puts every crane, drying rack, and hayfield to work at once, and labour and berths are both scarce. Warm rain inland, or a squall on the coast, lifts the Chart-run, sends people to sandbag the low warehouses, and pushes silt into the berths on the fresh water. Afterward the salt air is clear over water that is still standing, and the grain may be dry in the field and trapped behind a flooded road.

Drainage is wealth, which is why the wealthy river fields hold ditches that Root- and Tide-Answered craft maintains alongside ordinary labour. Poorer ground floods longer, and the advance is skilled work, not command of rain. The [[The Answered|Answered]] are people whose upkeep is earnest craft in a material, root and soil for one and the water for the other, and the ditch is that craft.

Seaweed is gathered for food in a lean week and for manure where custom permits. It can enrich a field and salt-burn a young green if it is piled carelessly.

### What grows

The ground that feeds a shore household is wheat and rye for the best bread, oats and barley where it is wet or poor, peas and beans, cabbage and turnips for the winter, flax for rope and cloth and linen, apples and pears behind a windbreak, hay for the cattle and the sheep and the horses and the oxen, and the marsh edge for reeds, grazing, eels, and biting insects.

### What can be gathered

Herring, eel, mussel, cockle, limpet, seaweed, marsh greens, berries, hazel nuts, nettles, sorrel, and mushrooms cover the ordinary range of what a person can gather. Safe gathering depends on the tide, on who already owns the ground, and on the quality of the water. A clean-looking shore after a storm can hold fouled shellfish. A generous mussel bed can already belong to a quay hearth. A [[The Taken-In|Taken-In]] person, who has to keep near living plants and who reads ground more readily than most, may read an estuary better than the people beside them. A Condition is the change a person lives with, and the Tithe is the upkeep that change requires, and no Condition makes spoiled water wholesome by wishing.

## The Old Crossing face

The eastern harbours face [[Maiethorn]] across the oldest, busiest water in the world. The distance is short enough to tempt bad decisions and long enough for one Hale-month storm to close the Given door, the week in which a ten-year-old can still be Given a change under a sound Tree.

A high thin cloud, falling air, and gulls gone inland are read in the harbour as a blow coming before the next tide, and the family's problem is whether to leave early, lose the cargo, or wait with the ten-year-old. Fog that holds after noon can mean the wind is light while the navigation is not, and a Far-Voiced person can be heard and still cannot show the shoal. Warm rain and a backing wind can make a fast crossing possible and a rough landing likely, and the berth and the inland road may not be open in the same hour. A sudden clear after the squall opens the harbour all at once, so pilgrims, grain, fish, and graft earth compete for the same labour.

Far-Voiced are infrastructure here, because the warning and the berth-call carry through weather. They do not steer ships or make a safe channel. Tide-Answered people improve the work on a hull and read the flow. They do not cancel a gale.

Turning-Week crowds the lodging, the wells, the green lots, and the release-houses, the halls where a Far-Voiced person can let the voice out. The prosperous coast can furnish all four and still price a poor family out of them.

## The Chart-run plain

The river interior feeds the ports. Its weather arrives twice: first in the field, and then, days later, as water at the estuary. Saturated soil in spring delays the seed and the carts, and the price of stored grain rises before anyone knows the yield. Warm rain in summer swells the crops and the river, so the barges move well until debris reaches the lower run. Harvest can be three dry days and then rain, which means every household cuts at once, the sheaves go wet, and the mills crowd. Winter is mud and banks eroding. The roads fail while the river stays navigable, and the power on the plain shifts toward the people who own the boats.

Oxen work the heavy soils, horses and mules move the lighter loads, and barges carry the bulk. [[Two-Bodied]] people, who have another self in a real animal body, scout the banks, carry messages, herd, or work the water in a form that suits the job. An animal self is not a replacement for a barge crew with hands.

## The Night Shore

The western face meets the open West Water. Here the word weather often means a hull that is overdue beyond the last forecast anyone trusted. The week begins with a long low swell and a wind steady from the west, so the nets come in, roofers are hired, and the late ships are named and not yet feared. Then a dark front and hard rain stop the coasting, the Far-Voiced warning posts work, and stores and people crowd under cover. A false lull eases the rain while the pressure stays wrong, one factor tries to sail, and families argue over whether the lull counts. The second wind comes harder, with a crossed sea, and a berth breaks, a roof goes, or wreckage arrives with no crew in it. Clearing brings a sharp light and a line of debris, and the Chart-crews and the rightful owners meet at whatever the water brought.

Fish, oats, rye, sheep, and hardy orchard fruit support the shore. Peat supplements driftwood and whatever fuel is imported. Salt meadows graze sheep and cattle when a storm surge has not covered them.

People keep duplicate stores, shore watches, salvage rights, widows' shares, and factors who never count a cargo as arrived before the line is tied. That is how a shore lives with hulls that go missing. The mood comes after the stores do.

## Mortal Trees at saltwater

Strandoren's Trees are mature and many, especially on the wealthy coasts, and their hazards are ordinary and persistent. Salt spray and storm surge burn the leaves and the soil, and waterlogged roots invite rot even in a land that calls rain prosperity. Gale damage opens bark to blight, quay expansion compacts the living ground and narrows the room the roots had, and polluted runoff follows the streets downhill toward the old squares.

Towns answer with raised greens, fresh-water cisterns, shelter walls, drains, and rules about what may be stored uphill of a Tree. These are civic works, built by ordinary hands, with Answered craft where it can be had.

A mature graft that has survived for generations becomes a financial fact. Houses lend against the property nearby, guilds schedule Turning-Week around the wood, and the leaf-lots cluster in the same protected drainage. One storm can therefore threaten sacred wood, Tithe provision, and rents together.

## Food and Tithe ecology

A river farm lives on grain, beans, roots, hay, and cattle. Taken-In green and Two-Bodied work are close to free there, until a flood isolates the household and drowns the living green. A fishing quay lives on fish, shellfish, and imported grain. Far-Voiced work, Tide-Answered work, Returned salvage, and the physical work of the Stilled all fit that quay, until a tied fleet removes both the income and the outlet and saltwater gets into the lots. The [[The Stilled|Stilled]] are people who have to keep the body working, and the [[Returned]] are people whose chosen Purpose can include the deadly useful work and is not assigned by the town that needs it.

A stone port lives on imported food, paid wells, and rented green. The markets in lots, halls, archives, and licensed medicine run deep, and the poor can stay hungry for green or for an outlet while food is still reaching the richer streets. A Night Shore town lives on fish, sheep, oats, and stored cargo. Warning posts, repair, and ranging fit it, until a missing hull carries off the food, the novelty, the witnesses, and a person's Purpose at once.

Urban Taken-In are a working minority, on the orchards, the river-gardens, the quay greens, and the rented leaf-lots. They were Given here, which means the change came at a Tree, or they wintered here, or they work here, or they cannot afford to leave. Brine in a lot is therefore a public utility failure, not decorative damage.

[[Long-Lived]] people thrive on the flow of testimony, scholarship, and knowledge of the cargo, until a house controls which pages or which travellers they are allowed to see. A Long-Lived person has to keep learning something genuinely new, so novelty can arrive by ship, and it is not a commodity made genuine by stamping it "new."

The Stilled have the continent's best licensed surgery and enough physical work to clear the Grey, the stillness that settles in the body when the work stops. The danger comes after an injury, a quarantine, or a dismissal, when the body that was ordered to rest is also the body that has to keep moving.

Returned people can align a Purpose with a plague ward, with the recovery of the drowned, or with wreck work. The job remains deadly and useful. It does not become every Returned person's Purpose by civic demand.

## Disease on connected water

The same network that makes Strandoren rich makes infection fast. A packed hold shows itself as coughing in one berth, wet bedding, and fever by night. The ordinary response is to tie the hull, air the people ashore by hearth, and mark who stood near. The Tithe complication is that quarantine cuts people off from green, from range, from a Purpose, and from paid work. A fouled well shows as several streets down with flux after a flood. People close the rope, boil the water, draw it somewhere else, and dig the waste lower. Fuel and clean water are priced, and a city lot still needs a drink.

Bad shellfish is one market sick after a tide that was calm and red, or simply foul. That shore is closed, the catch is discarded, and the town uses its stores. A food trade that is tied up removes the livelihoods of the Far-Voiced and the Two-Bodied who lived by moving it. Wound-rot is rope burns, fish cuts, and crane crushes. People wash, dress, and operate when an operation is needed. A licensed Stilled surgeon may be available only to a household that holds a charter. Marsh ague is a fever that keeps returning near standing water. The beds are drained around, the jars are covered, and the camp is moved. The poorest live on the low ground, which is the ground drained last.

[[The Grey Summer]] in Cut-year 171 is the named epidemic: a cough out of a hold, then the streets and the quay-gangs, with no respect for Given, Struck, or Kept, whether a person had been Given at a Tree, Struck later, or Kept without that giving. Ships were tied, tenders died, and the notes outlived the debtors. A later fever is not a second Grey Summer. People call it "Grey" when they are frightened, and clerks decide whether the comparison changes a rate.

Stilled surgeons hold the harm and operate, Returned people do the plague and the work of the dead, Far-Voiced people warn without carrying their bodies from street to street, and Tide- and Ember-Answered people improve the water and the boiling. None of them replaces clean bedding, separation, food, or trust.

## Beasts as livelihood

Oxen pull the ploughs and the heavy quay carts that come off the Chart-run. Horses and mules carry the lighter goods between the river landings and the indented coasts. Sheep graze the salt meadow and the higher dry ground, and the wool travels even when the meat has spoiled. Cattle use the wet meadows and provide milk, hide, manure, and traction. Pigs feed on woodland mast inland and on quay scraps, under ownership that is strict about both. Geese and chickens fit a small port yard, and people notice quickly when they sicken. Dogs herd, guard, and work the boats, cats protect the grain stores, and bees serve the orchard country and supply wax to the halls and the houses.

These animals enter a scene as property, food, labour, affection, or loss. If the coast needs teeth, the place to go is [[Dangers of the Turning]], rather than inventing a sea-monster to explain the fog.

## The one thing the tide does not explain

Some Turning-month mornings, salt lies on the roots of mature coastal Trees above any storm line. [[Orentel]]'s Tree on the rise is the best-known case. The tide does not reach it.

Tree-tenders wash the roots with fresh water, and hall-keepers schedule around the work. Factors file the damaged salt below the rise and decline to file this. No explanation is required for the salt to delay a stand or start an argument.

## GM Notes

High reach is not high faith. In the player-facing text, mature and abundant Trees follow early grafting carried by sea, and they follow wealth, drainage, and care. Commercial belief does not weaken the wood, and healthy wood does not prove a doctrine.

Salt on the roots is Orentel's habit, and Orentel does not explain it. Keep the salt practical and local, and do not let it forecast a colour, speak a name, or become a second Grey Summer.

Strandoren is vulnerable because people and goods move quickly, and the mechanism is that connection. The sea is not carrying a curse, and the player-facing page should not acquire one.
