# The Haldar

> The long-integrated people who are *genuinely content* — the schoolroom-date conquest, the living proof that a march becomes a province becomes, near enough, home. Proud, distinct, stubborn, and quietly certain the Empire is the best bargain their hills ever got. They are the case for the defence, and they mean it.

## Overview

The Haldar are the hill-clans of [[The Halden]] — herders, miners, and terrace-farmers of hard upland country, conquered so long ago that the conquest is a date in a book, not a wound in a family. They will tell you at length how different they are from the soft basin-folk of the Aurenne, in the Empire's common tongue, over Empire-taxed ale, having just walked out of an imperial court where they won. Their integration is total *and* affectionate, and there is no contradiction in that they can see.

If the Kessadi are the Empire's hardest case, the Haldar are its easiest — and the campaign needs them exactly as much. They are what "content" honestly looks like: not brainwashed, not cowed, just people whose grandparents' grandparents did the math and found it good.

## What they kept, what they lost

**Kept:** their dialect (Haldic, spoken at home and in song, never in a contract), their hill-gods, their festivals, their clan structure, their pride, and their walls — the old hill-forts still stand, kept for festival and vanity. The Empire touched none of it.

**Lost:** the raiding. Before the Empire, the clans fought each other — cattle-raids, feuds, and the lean-year killings when a failed harvest at altitude meant a family starved or a family stole. The Empire ended the raiding by roping the uplands into the network: when the Haldar harvest fails, grain comes upriver and *nobody dies.* They gave up their wars and their feud-law. Ask a Haldar elder what the Empire took and she will genuinely struggle to name a thing she misses.

## How they feel (mostly warm, not uniform)

- **The old** love the Empire with the specific love of people who remember, through their grandparents, a harder world. To them it is *the thing that ended the raiding* — and that is not abstract. Every clan has a lean-year story.
- **The young** are more Haldar-proud and less Empire-grateful, precisely *because* the peace is all they've known. They never saw the before, so the Empire is not a rescuer — it's just the air, and the air is easy to resent. They lean into the dialect, the old dress, the hill-gods, as identity rather than inheritance.
- **The class split runs the other way from Kessad:** the Haldar send *more* clerks, surveyors, and officers into imperial service per head than almost anywhere. For a sharp hill-child with no land, the Empire is the only ladder tall enough to matter — and the ones who climb it come home in imperial grey and are both admired and quietly resented.

> The grievance the Empire should fear is **not here yet — but you can watch the ground for it.** A generation that inherited the peace and paid for none of it is a generation that can be told the peace was never really a gift. The Haldar are content; contentment is not permanent; and the rebellion (task 1c) knows a proud young Haldar makes a better recruit than a grateful old one.

## Customs & belief

- **The hill-gods** are small, local, and *placed* — this spring, that pass, the god-of-the-third-terrace — not a pantheon so much as a census of the landscape. You do not worship the hill-gods; you *keep* them, the way you keep a wall. The Empire's holidays are now cheerfully hosted alongside the local ones at the same old shrines, and no Haldar finds this odd.
- **Reckoning your line.** A Haldar introduces themselves by clan and by the count of named ancestors they can recite — a good line runs seven, nine, eleven deep. It is memory as status, and it long predates the Empire's obsessively accurate [[The Shape of the Empire|Rolls]] — which the Haldar regard, not incorrectly, as the Empire keeping *their* kind of memory for the whole world.
- **The kept walls.** Every hill-town's fortifications are obsolete, decorative, and immaculately maintained. Once a year the clan "mans the walls" in festival — an armed watch against an enemy that the Empire made extinct. They do not quite remember what they are watching for. They keep the watch anyway.

## GM Notes

`visibility: gm` — walled off; this note is player-safe.

- The Haldar are the **design-contract anchor**: the living argument that the Empire is worth defending. Never let them curdle into "sellouts" or "the deluded." Their contentment is *earned and rational.* If the table starts rooting purely for the rebellion, the Haldar are the correction.
- **The kept walls / the watch against a forgotten enemy** is a *very* faint, deniable rhyme with the frontier that went quiet ([[The Outmark]], the "dam against the dark" half of [[The Cost]]). Almost every old culture has a ritual watch against a forgotten foe; play it as folk custom, nothing more, for a long time. It is a seed, not a clue.
- The "reckoning your line" custom sets up the eventual weirdness that the [[Climate & Terrain|land was measured before there was anyone to measure it]] — a people who prize deep ancestral memory butting against a memory older than any line. Do not force it.
