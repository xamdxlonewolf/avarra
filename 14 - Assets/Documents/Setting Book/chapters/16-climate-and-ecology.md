# Climate, Ecology, and Travel

This chapter names the water and the stone, says how a week feels in each band of weather, and says how long a walk takes. Say the everyday name. A devout speaker keeps the *th* in the land-names around the water. On a dock, that *th* wears off. Clerks enjoy the long old form. Dates here are in Cut-years, counted from the First Cut, the first time living wood was successfully cut and carried. The Ages chapter keeps that count.

The year is familiar enough to farm by and strange enough to argue over. Maiethorn and Strandoren, the Old World pair, keep four seasons and the twelve shared month-names. The Rain-Wall, the range down the middle of Maiethorn, can make that same month a flood on one face and a dry well on the other. Heskoren gets a shorter kindness between frosts. Kumbaan keeps its own moons and lives inside the storm-wall. A month tells people what work should be possible. A season tells them what the land actually did. A late frost can put winter into Seed-month, and three clear weeks can put the harvest into Ledger-month. Clerks still date the note by the month. Farmers date the year by what survived.

## Named water, range, and rivers

| Everyday | What it is | Other name |
|---|---|---|
| **Old Crossing** | Water between Maiethorn and Strandoren | *the crossing*; *the waiting-water* |
| **West Water** | Sea between Strandoren and Heskoren | Night Shore's name. Hush clause. |
| **Rain-Wall** | Maiethorn's range. Wet one side, dry the other. | *Thaw-Wall*; *the Divide* |
| **Noon Pass** | Old high road. Lost its stone, Cut-year 233. | *noon-pass* |
| **Shelf-gate** | The pass after the Break | *the new one.* Break-rate. |
| **Core-thaw** | Maiethlir's river | clerks: *snowmelt* |
| **Well-wash** | Seasonal water, Rain-Shadow | dry most years |
| **Rise-water** | Harrow's stream to the ford | the Seat says *the ford* |
| **Chart-run** | Strandoren's river, out at Orentel | inland tickets that reached salt |

**the Old Crossing.** This is the oldest trade route in the world. It is narrow enough that a fair-weather hull makes it in a handful of days, and busy enough that a late storm in Hale-month, when the land is supposed to be sound, can hold a family past Turning-Week. Miss that week, and the child stays UnTurned, having reached no Tree in the year they turned ten. The Hinge Shore is Maiethorn's face on this water. Orentel is Strandoren's face. The Salt Walk, the old way a Shore-lands child reached a Turning, was this water first and the Near Mile inland after that, the walk on Maiethorn to Thaeloren.

People call Maiethorn and Strandoren the Old World pair because the water is old and close, and because it made the two continents into one argument. They do not call the water "inner." What happened on it has dates. The Grey Summer, in Cut-year 171, was a fever that tied the quays. It began as a cough in packed holds, it killed across the Given, the Struck, and the Kept, and it left grafts unwatered when their tenders fell. It was a fever. The Stilled are people whose upkeep is exertion, and the Grey they work back out is the stillness that fills the body when the work stops. The Grey Summer was neither that stillness nor a punishment laid on anyone's Condition. The Hinge Hush, in Cut-year 304, is the peace still charged as the **Hush-rate**. From Cut-year 299 to 304 the two shores fought over who set the price. The hush is a rate, a clause for the far water, and a refusal to name a winner.

**the West Water.** This sea is wider than the Crossing. It is the water-leg of the Long Mile, the long way on from Strandoren toward a Tree on Heskoren, and that road takes weeks. A pot of living cuttings has a harder berth here than on the Old Crossing. Netstrand quotes the far crossing on this water, and Braetu is the harbour where that quote is written. Heskoren's frontier coast, its east coast, faces the same sea back toward Strandoren. Heskoren's west coast, the slate-shore, faces the last capes, and beyond them the storm-wall.

The Hush's west-water clause lives on this sea, and it says who may do what once a hull leaves the Crossing. Netstrand quotes the far crossing. Saelvaeth may take a pot of cuttings without a waiting-due, the charge Vaethorn would have written before the wood could move, while Vaethorn may bless a landing and may not levy it, and Ornled collects a beach-fee at Sanbreo when a hull wants the beach. Vaelhesk is not on the clause, because a first meal set for a guest is not a landing-due. Kumbaan is not on this water either. Sailors tell of a pot that went into the storm and was wrecked, and no graft took. No house on this water sells a passage to the isle.

**the Rain-Wall.** This is a highland spine down the middle of Maiethorn. Weather on the west face is wet and runs through four seasons. The leeward east is the Rain-Shadow, which is why Saelthael sows at wells, and why Lirorn can levy a thaw as if the melt were a tax. Fox-of-the-Snows and Tengu hearths sit densest on the high snow. That is terrain, families on the ground the weather made, not a flag and not a nation of one born people. Heskoren's highland spines are a different weather. They are not this range.

Two passes cross the spine. The **Noon Pass** is the old high road. In the Thaw-Break of Cut-year 233 a water-line was left over its ribbon-notch, and the road up to the pass was gone for two summers. Children still rub that line for luck, and ridge-folk rub it because the water was real. The toll does not tell a lowlander which rubbing is the prayer. The **Shelf-gate** is the road that was left once the old stone sat under that line. Narol of the Pass took the thaw-toll when the old holder's stone was under the water, and Narol is dead, so the household that inherited the toll still levies it. Threnmaieth wanted the new pass counted, and Lirorn charged them for the water anyway. The Shelf-gate is the road people use now.

**Four rivers.** The **Core-thaw** is snowmelt off the Rain-Wall's west face, running toward the Old Crossing. Maiethlir sat down where the water had slowed enough to hold a Turning Tree, a day's living-earth from the Motherwood, which is as far as a cutting could be carried in a day and still be planted. The town is on the river road, not on Orenbren's Near Mile. Clerks file the spring rise as snowmelt. The river runs louder the week before Leaf-Fall, and Maiethlir is where that louder week is known. Watchers do not file it. Rithim, the Speaker, raises their voice over the water, and the town will not say why the week is louder. In a high Liren, when the thaw is already up, the lodging-road meets a near branch of the same river. The line "Eolthael, the summer the ford ran high" is this water in a bad year, and it is not a fifth river. Below Maiethlir the same water is the Down-Bank, the days a traveller is already going to spend on the way to the Hinge Shore. It is not a second river. In the Thaw-Break the Core-thaw took a month of melt in seven days.

The **Well-wash** is a seasonal run east of the range. In a kind Liren it is a river, and in a cruel one it is a line of silt. Towns cluster at wells because this water cannot be a civic certainty. **Larbril** is where the west road meets that wash, short of the pass. **Ornsael** drinks a well, not the wash, farther into the dry, with open country between it and Larbril, and the wash is why the well matters. The town sits inland. Between a river-week and a silt-week, the day-wash shows for an afternoon, water enough to look like a camp. A traveller from the core sees a silver in the dust and treats it as a ford, and by morning it is a stain. Saelthael already knows this, which is why the towns sit at wells. Ornsael's problem is a well dropping, not that shine. The Dry Stair climbs above a different well-town, and it is not a river-shrine. Sometimes a bright thread of water runs uphill of the last rain, lasts an hour, and is gone. Well-keepers say the rock remembers a wet year, and travellers from the core say the map is wrong. Nobody on the bank will say which of those is true.

The **Rise-water** is a low stream off Harrow's rise. Downstream it becomes the hamlets' ford, the crossing of Brenod, Vaelun, and Ornath. The Seat still says *the ford*, because the Seat will not learn those three names. In clear weather the hamlets can see Harrow's canopy from the water, a dark on the far slope. There is no stone on the waiting side, and the cup still goes on the ford-rock. **Natai**, the march-town, stands on other ground, west of Harrow's Green, between Sanbreo and that grove, and not on this ford.

The **Chart-run** is Strandoren's interior river-plain, running out to Orentel's estuary. The coasts took their Trees by sea. The inland country waited on a cart and on this river together. Lunbra sits on the wealthy stretch, and that is where the roll is kept. Food sold on the Salt Quay comes from this plain, from the catch of the Crossing, and from whatever the hinge pays to have brought across.

## Climate bands

A week in each band feels like this. Choose the band, read the month, and then let the weather turn once. A whole week of identical weather should feel notable. Weather is usually an inconvenience before it is a danger: the queue at the dry shed, the cart sharing a rock, the neighbour whose cistern failed. Turning-Week stays the week around the High Solstice. A storm, a fever, a blocked pass, or a late hull does not move it.

| Band | Where | Weather |
|---|---|---|
| Crossing-shore | both faces of the Old Crossing | mild, wet-temperate; a Hale-month storm can decide a Salt Walk |
| Mother-core | Maiethorn west of the Rain-Wall | four true seasons in old sacred country; winters that make the High Solstice mean something |
| Rain-Wall / Thaw-Wall | Lirorn | snow, shelves, and a thaw the towns keep as a civic year |
| Rain-Shadow | Saelthael, east of the range | dry hills, wells, and either the Well-wash or nothing |
| Shore-lands | Strandoren coasts and the Chart-run | maritime, indented, the sea used as the highway |
| Night Shore | Netstrand | open ocean, west-weather, watches kept for hulls that do not arrive |
| Sundered upland | Heskoren | cooler, wilder, harder winters; settled country is a map of the Trees that survived |
| Storm-wall / Kumbaan | the Sundering Isle | mild and wet under cloud, bright nights, and the wall itself is the climate |

On the Crossing-shore the week opens under low cloud and mild rain, and the turn is a clear tide-day or a fast squall, which in Hale-month is the squall. Berths close before the roads do, so a Salt Walk family can lose Turning-Week without ever being far inland. The Mother-core opens on weather you would know as four seasons, and the turn is a river rising, a bright drying wind, or a cold night that does not belong to its month. Mills keep moving, while the grafts, the orchards, and the crowded greens all want the same drink.

The Rain-Wall opens with snow on the high shelves and melt or cold rain below, and the turn locks the path or unlocks all of it at once. In Liren the Shelf-gate may already be a lake before a traveller reaches the toll. The Rain-Shadow opens clear, cool, and dusty, and the turn is a hard shower, a blow of dust, or no change at all, so the wells fail before the roads do. The Well-wash may run for a day and still leave no drink a town can count on.

The Shore-lands open in salt mist and a working tide, and the turn is sun enough to unload, estuary rain enough to flood, or a gale along the coast. A leaf-lot, the rented plot of living green a stone town keeps for people who have to rest among plants, can be burned by brine and drenched by fresh water in the same month. The Night Shore opens on an open-ocean wind, and the front arrives as hard rain, a sudden lull, and then a second wind, until missing hulls become a kind of local weather. A Far-Voiced warning, from a person who has to put the feeling of the voice somewhere, can cross when the hull cannot.

The Sundered upland opens as cold rain below, sleet on the ridges, and sun in one vale while the next is still in cloud, so a healthy pocket can be bringing in a harvest while the next valley loses its roof, its road, or its young graft. On Kumbaan the inside of the island is mild and wet under cloud, and the edge is reef-thunder. The turn is a bright night within the wall, or a squall that reaches inland as steady rain. The crossing remains a chance, and it is never a timetable.

## Maiethorn

Maiethorn holds four of the eight bands, and a traveller can go from salt drizzle to old forest, cross a pass that floods like a lake, and reach a well-town measuring dust, without leaving the Motherland. The deep difference is water. Towns in the core drain it, Lirorn waits to levy the thaw the range lets go, Ornsael divides what the wells hold, and every living graft wants a share.

West of the Rain-Wall the country keeps four true seasons, and the winters are real, which is why the High Solstice means something. A core week is two wet days, one bright break, and then a river on the rise. Grafts take in the old sacred country and in the Motherwood because generations have built drains, cistern turns, and a rule about keeping animals off the roots. The core's food is bread, peas, cabbage, and orchard fruit, with pork or river fish when the week allows. In the core, the greens a Tithe asks for, the living plants a Taken-In person has to keep near, are commons and garden lots. Stilled infirmaries and Returned plague-work cluster in the rich core because that is where the chairs and the endowments are. The Mill-hold is the caution in that plenty: the mill-race stayed full, and the town's Tree stood in the overflow. Stored grain did not water it.

On the range, last year's snow is this year's civic year. A Wall week freezes at night and melts by noon. Lirorn levies the thaw, the pass, and the high road, because the weather has made that road scarce. A thaw-week can turn the Shelf-gate into a lake. The Thaw-Break was that arrangement failing at once, a month of melt in seven days, and the west road stayed closed for two summers. People still remember which notch holds the water-line. The lower shelves eat barley, roots, and hard cheese. Hay is wealth, because the animals of winter eat the summer that was cut.

East of the Wall the week is dry wind until one hard shower fails to fill the well. The food is millet or barley, lentils, onions, and goat cheese if the herd came in. A Taken-In Tithe's living green is cheap on a terrace and expensive if the well is dropping, because the same bucket is the food, the plants, and the Tree's drink. Two-Bodied people, who have another self in a real animal body, range the dry hills at night. Returned work sits downwind of the drink. People on the pass tell of a travelling stiller, a Stilled person whose upkeep is hard use of the body and who can hold a wound. The dry country has no stillers' college. At Ornsael the well-rope is knotted at last year's water-line, and this year's knot is wet even when the water stands below it. Ornsael will not say why.

On the Old Crossing face the weather is the berth. The week is mild rain, with a squall in Hale-month that decides a Salt Walk. Far-Voiced warnings are how a dock hears weather it cannot yet see. They do not steer the ship. Selkie hearths are common on the quays. The Hinge Shore says what the hull is carrying before anyone across the water prices it. The food on that shore is herring, mussels, and rye bread.

## Strandoren

Strandoren is maritime country. A week can begin with three working tides, lose two days to fog, and end with every crane tied down. The sea softens the cold and refuses to be predicted. Hard frost is less reliable here than in Maiethorn's core, which makes the winter wet rather than kind: rot, mud, smoke, and bad bedding do more harm than deep snow. People eat rye or wheat bread, beans, cabbage, apples, and eel or herring, with mussels and cockles when the tide is safe, and oat pottage when the fleet stays tied.

The coasts received the wood first. The Chart-run carried it inland, and the river also carries another district's weather downstream, so a quay can flood under a clear local sky. Food on the Salt Quay is this plain, the Crossing's catch, and whatever the hinge pays to bring across. Tithe-provision here is a market. You buy a hall-hour, a lot-night, a novelty page. The Taken-In who live in the stone cities are a working minority on lots, orchards, and river-gardens. They work at a polite distance, and the lots are in use. Brine in a lot is a failure of that provision. A west wind lays salt on leaves that were green at dawn, a rise on the Chart-run or a squall off the Crossing soaks those same leaves with fresh water, and a Taken-In person resting in the lot feels the scald and the rot as one week while the granary uphill still has bread. That split is the estuary's weather. Guilds count the flood, because a flood has a witness, and the salt-burn is quieter and often the one that takes the green. Sometimes a lot salts on a windless morning, when the estuary is flat and nobody filed a gale. The tender washes the leaves and will not say where the salt was in the air.

Separate from that lot, salt lies on the roots of mature coastal Trees above any storm line. Orentel's Tree on the rise is the case people know, on ground the tide does not reach. Tenders wash the roots with fresh water, the factors file the salt below the rise and decline to file this, and Orentel will not say why.

The Night Shore watches for hulls that do not arrive. A west-weather week begins as a long low swell, spends a false lull arguing whether anyone should sail, and finishes in a second wind that can break a berth or bring in wreckage with no crew in it. Chart-crews and the rightful owners meet at whatever the water brought. Prestige and necessity use the same water and do not pay the same rate.

A Hale-month storm on the Old Crossing can turn a Salt Walk into a missed year. Denlad is the inlet about a day's sail short of Orentel's estuary, a village with its own Tree on a low rise, and you can miss the week from there without ever seeing a crane. These spans are fair weather. A storm rewrites them. A later fever is not a second Grey Summer. People say "Grey" when they are frightened, and a clerk decides whether the comparison changes a rate.

## Heskoren

Heskoren is one broad band with several weathers inside it: cool coast, rough moor, wooded vale, high spine, and long river country. Winters run harder than on the Old World pair, and the kind season between frosts is shorter. A clear day is when the roofs, the hay, the carts, the witnesses, and the people tending a graft all try to move at once. A usual week is cold rain in a vale, sleet on a ridge, one brilliant dry day, and then a hard frost. On the west coast a long swell can keep the next hull outside while the shore spends those clear hours. The food is oat mash, rye bread, peas, cabbage, turnips, hard cheese, and smoked fish, with berries and nuts when the season allows. A first-meal bread at the First Bowl is a little sour.

The continent is not empty wilderness. It is worked pockets around surviving Trees, and older hearths that predate them, with country between that is too wet, too steep, too cold, or simply too far to hold densely. Young grafts face wind, thin soil, and a queue written in the Motherwood. A Taken-In person's upkeep is the living land itself, until a bad winter, a quarantine, or a dead planting cuts them off from it. Two-Bodied people range, scout, and herd, and a town can start treating a family's range as if it were public stock. Returned work the far sheds, the flood, and the dead, and a useful danger is not automatically the Purpose they chose. Stilled medicine is one tired stiller, or a rope. The high country is not Maiethorn's Rain-Wall. It has no thaw-levy and no Shelf-gate. Tengu and Fox-of-the-Snows hearths are denser on the heights because of the ground and the history, and a mixed hearth uses the same winter stores.

On a clear day a canopy shows on the next ridge, and the road does not. Someone says the wood spoke this week. Someone else says the colour is last year's leaves. Neither of them has walked it. Tunral is that kind of gap, a kitchen and a mile-post coastward of Harrow's Green, about a week along a road with no sight of the canopy. Huval, who keeps the post, sets a bowl outside at dusk on the side away from Harrow's, and will not say why. The bowl is not a first meal, and it is not a shrine. Harrow's Green is what the far green is, on the weeks the road allows. The hamlets are the rare case where the canopy is a sightline and a ford, not a rumour. On some clear days the green is there at dawn and gone by noon, with no cloud. Travellers blame haze, ridge-folk do not bother to blame it, and nobody measures it.

The live front is Saelvaeth's luck, which is why Harrow's Green is the grove the neighbours can see, with the Rise-water running from that rise down to the hamlets' ford. Natai, the march-town, stands west of the grove between Sanbreo and Harrow's, on its own ground, and the days between them are not recorded. Harrow's square is the grove-town's own ground, so a complaint can name Natai while the canopy the hamlets can see is still Harrow's. Vaethorn waits in a vale with no view of that canopy, and the people there do not want the view. Ornled has the same thin wood and keeps no congregation, with the slate kept at Sanbreo on the west-coast slate-shore. Vaelhesk is old greens, the land is the seat, and there is no city on it. Between mile-shrines the next stone is a rumour, though empty road-houses still make a bed for the people who did not go home.

After the first hard snow around some young Trees, a narrow melt runs back along the road by which the graft first arrived. It can lie on the shaded side while the open square stays white. People call it the old-road melt only when they need to give directions. Packed earth, a buried stone, or a tender's morning walk can account for any one example. No account covers enough of them to end the conversation. The melt does not point toward the next town, and it does not promise a Tree will take there.

## Kumbaan

Kumbaan is mild, wet, and cloudy, and it turns bright by night when the storm-wall opens a view of the moon. The wall is the wind, the current, the reef, and the surf working together at the edge of the island. It is not weather happening somewhere past the last beach. Inside, the same weather arrives diminished and still persistent: cloud, rain, salt carried farther inland than a guest expects, and a low thunder that can keep going under a hill sky that looks calm. A week is mild rain under low cloud, one bright moonlit night, reef-thunder with no local wind, and then a squall that closes the shallows and soaks the peat. People eat barley broth, oats, beans, peas, cabbage, turnips, onions, and goat milk or cheese. The shore adds mussels, limpets, fish, and seaweed, with berries and nuts in season. Fuel is peat, and what a wreck sometimes gives the hall.

There is no Tree here, and no graft crossed. A slip planted on the isle would die. Dead wood never became a Tree. A wrecked mainland pot is rot, fuel if anyone will burn it, and the discomfort of knowing why it was carried. Islanders date a rain, a birth, a debt, or a second sitting by the moon, and they describe the season by its work: ditches full, terraces open, shallows closed, peat drying, goats brought in. A mainlander can call a week springlike and be understood. They cannot convert the calendar by picking whichever mainland month has similar rain.

The hills, which people call the Paps, carry pasture and terrace-gardens on the surface. Halls, stores, and the rooms where people sleep through the day lie under the turf. Rain runs down every mistake in a terrace wall. Goats are common, and they undo a repair the moment it is finished. Large draught animals are uncommon on ground this steep. People, goats, small carts on the good paths, and unseen hands carry most of the loads. Those hands can hold a basket, lift a light net, fetch a line, gather limpets within reach, and bring the wet baskets in before the rain. They do not conjure food, they do not harvest a valley at once, they do not swim the wall, and they do not make a lean year generous by themselves. A great table after a poor harvest means the hearths contributed, the stores were opened, or the meal is thinner than the serving makes it look.

There is no ferry, and no ticket you can buy. Shore-sitters know a moon when the wall thins, the wreck-moons and the weeks after a rumour of a crossing, and that knowledge is weather. Nobody sells it as a sailing date. A quiet shore does not mean the outer reef is passable, and a violent night can still put a living wreck on the sand. Most ships wreck, turn back, or are never heard from again. Shore-sitters do not swim out, and they do not keep a rescue boat. They decide whether you are alive after the sea has already decided.

If you live, the landing goes in this order, and it does not skip. You are on the sand, or you are not, and a wreck that never makes the beach has not landed. A shore-sitter decides whether you are alive. The living get water and a blanket, and they are not asked their Kind or their colour. The dead go above the tide. The living are walked uphill, and the first night you spend as a guest begins at a hall, not on the sand. At the second meal a table-keeper decides whether you are kin, and the guest is told, "no one is a stranger twice." That belonging holds on the isle. It does not travel back to a mainland watch. Before you sail, a Strandoren quay still wants a road-word, the name of someone at your last hearth who will stand for you. On the isle the watch's three questions, your name, your last hearth, and who stands for you, are not asked.

The way back is the same wall, and the sitters do not send anyone home. You wait for a thinning, or you take a hull that will try. A Yumboe who wants off the isle sits the same sand from the other want. If you eat, eat what is set in front of you, and sleep the day if you can. Do not plant a Tree, do not file the hill in a clerk's book, and do not swim the wall. Do not repeat "no one is a stranger twice" at home as a saying you can spend. It is said to a guest at the table, and it is not a pass. Guest-right on the isle is the table, not a token you carry home to show a watch.

The hall a sailor comes into is Ndenjoo, which sailors call the Feeding Hill. Njunda keeps the table. The sand on that stretch is called Soonke, and Soonke is the person who sits it. The lamp on the path does not call ships.

On some nights later remembered as a thinning, peat smoke from the hill vents lies seaward while the grass and the rain move inland. Shore-sitters notice it after the wreck or the sail. They do not use it to call one beforehand. A bad vent or a crossed gust can explain the smoke for any single hall. The smoke stays an observation, and it is not a crossing calendar.

## Travel times

The unit is a **day's walk**, the interval a mile-shrine on the old roads already marks. A horse takes the same road and spares the blisters. A ship takes the water those walks already name. It does not invent a different country.

|---|---|---|
| **Near Mile** | Maiethorn inland to Thaeloren | **3–12 days** (a couple of weeks at the long end) |
| **Salt Walk** | Old Crossing, then the Near Mile | **3–5 days' sail** plus the inland days; typically **1–3 weeks** |
| **Long Mile** | West Water, then Heskoren inland | **weeks to months**; figure **6–12 weeks** for a coast-to-grove attempt |

Where a row says **not recorded**, the seat gives a place and a direction and does not give a time. The directions come from the atlas sheets. Larbril lies on the dry-side road toward Ornsael, short of the pass, and the counted days in the table are the days to the well, not a separate count for Larbril. Raillath is a halt, a few hearths and a shrine with water, and it is not Nelath. Nelath is the spur where the walk stops.

A Hale-month storm on the Old Crossing can turn a Salt Walk into a missed year. A thaw-week on the Rain-Wall can turn the Shelf-gate into a lake, and the Thaw-Break closed roads for two summers. The table is weather, not a promise.

The Closed Seat was an origin-gate on the grove. Hildal's Retreat shut its walls one day's walk from the Motherwood, inside Orenbren's lodging-country, and that city is Rothallo, which people still call the Inner Close. The book kept there is the Walled Book, and it belongs to the grove's country, not to the shore. Fifteen powers stand. Rothallo sits inside Orenbren as one of them, and it is not a sixteenth. The Hinge Shore is the coast of a different war. Its peace is the Hinge Hush, still charged as the Hush-rate. Rothallo's wall and the Hinge Shore's quay keep their own names.

## What a house keeps in the field

An ox ploughs, hauls timber, and pulls a cart. The herds are on the core farms, the Chart-run plain, and the broad vales of Heskoren. A breeding animal is worth more to a house than a week's wages, so people are more afraid of losing the ox than of the ox itself. The ox will not take a ford when the water is over the axle, and then it stops, so a driver who knows the animal waits, or finds the shallow, or unloads. Whipping it through is how a house loses the season. On a ford that is already shallow, the driver still gets down and leads, and they will not say why. A Two-Bodied person in an ox's body is a person. This animal is an ox, and it is not on a guild roll.

A goat gives milk, meat, hide, and dung, and it browses scrub other stock will not take. The herds are on the Rain-Shadow, the rough ground of Heskoren, and the terraces of Kumbaan. A house that loses the herd loses the cheese and the wall's upkeep in the same week. The house is afraid of the goat in two places: a terrace wall knocked loose, which is a season of soil in the wrong yard, and a well-share argument when the herd comes in and the coping is already crowded. The goat is not afraid of the wall. The goat is doing the job the scrub offered. A Two-Bodied person in a goat's body is a person. This animal is a goat, and it is not breeding stock for a guild.

A dog herds, guards a door, and keeps a quay or a hall honest about who is coming in. Farms, quays, halls, and road-houses keep them. The dog knows the way the house uses, and the house trusts the stop. The dog is afraid of floodwater and refuses a flooded path, so handlers who know the dog stop with it. A driver who drags it through is arguing with the animal about the water, and the animal has already read the path. A granary cat, on the same kind of work, marks spoilage, and that is a different animal. A Two-Bodied person in a dog's body is a person. This animal is a dog.

Horses, mules, sheep, cattle, pigs, geese, and bees are livelihoods in the same way, on the ground that can feed them. They enter a scene as property, food, labour, or loss. The adversaries of a wild road are a different matter from a house's stock.
