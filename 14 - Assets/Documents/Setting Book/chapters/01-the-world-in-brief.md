# The World in Brief

Vampires, werewolves, and the rest of that old kin live in the Turning in the open. Towns accept them, employ them, and need what they can do, and they live as neighbours, each of them changed into what they are now, with a job and a cost. The world calls that change a **Condition**, a becoming, and the body they were born with is a **Kind**, both of them true at the same time.

A Kind is the ancestry you were born, and the body that comes with it. Human, Elf, Drakona, and the other ancestries the game already has sit beside four written for this world. You will know a Kitsune by the fox's ears and the brush of tail, a Selkie by the sealskin they can wear, a Tengu by the dark feathers through the hair and down the arms and by the wings, and a Yumboe by a height about a mainlander's waist. Nobody blinks at a fox-tail in a market on Maiethorn, or at wings on a ridge of Heskoren. A Yumboe is ordinary on Kumbaan, the storm-walled isle, and a sailor's tale on the other shores. No Kind keeps a country of its own. The place that raises you is the rest of a life.

A Condition is what you become after that birth. Roughly three people in four acquire one over a lifetime, and the towns meet it with law, with a guild, with medicine, and with the work that belongs to it. The society is built around what a person with a Condition can do, and around helping them live with the cost.

Most of those becomings are given in public, in a child's tenth year. During the midsummer week people call Turning-Week, the family brings the child to a **Turning Tree**. Most towns keep one in the square, a living cutting taken from an older Tree and planted in new ground. A cutting that has only just been planted still needs years before it can Turn a child, so a town that is too new, too remote, or has lost its Tree sends its children to a neighbour. A coloured leaf falls, a Condition is **Given**, and the colour is the name of which one. If no colour falls and the leaves close around the child, the Tree has kept them as they are. That child is one of **the Kept**, wanted in their own right. Later, some people are **Struck** when they cross a threshold the Tree does not hand to a child, a drowning they survive, an oath, or a death. The Condition arrives with no leaf and no ceremony, and the people watching call it tragic, or miraculous, or a little suspect. Some of the Kept are Struck in the years after the hug. Over a lifetime the split runs about sixty percent Given, fifteen percent Struck, and twenty-five percent Kept. A child who never stood under a Tree is **unTurned**. Neighbours may still say Kept, and the word belongs to the hug the town was there to see.

Every Condition carries a **Tithe**, the upkeep you meet if you are going to live well with the becoming. Ordinary work and ordinary rest are how it is paid. Meet it, and you are a valued citizen. Fall behind, and a small, real edge shows, while the person remains themselves. No Condition can compel, control, or bend another person's will. Children are taught that maxim as the Inviolate Will, in the sentence "the will is one's own," and the law holds it because it is true of every Condition. The danger stops at property, at pain, and at fear, at the edge of someone else's choosing.

Some hold that the **Leaf-Mother** blessed the world with these gifts, and that the Trees are her hands reaching down to choose. Skeptics, who are full members of the same towns, say the Trees are simply doing what Trees do, and they say the same of the hug, which the faith calls a blessing of its own. What puts you in her faith is the practice of bringing the children, tending the Tithe, and keeping the festival, which counts for more than what you privately believe about who does the choosing. Right practice binds, and right belief is what the faith still argues about. The Motherfaith is the common faith of these lands, and it is one faith among several.

There is one origin Tree, the **Awakening Tree**. In the old liturgical tongue its name is **Thaeloren**, the Tree that witnesses, and everyday speech wears that long name down to the Thallow. It stands on Maiethorn. Every town Tree is a living graft of that same wood, cut tissue kept alive on the road and planted so the same tree grows on, not a seedling raised from a seed the Tree dropped. How far the grafts have spread is the map. From Maiethorn, old and dense with Trees, the wood crossed the water to Strandoren, the shore-lands, where the life is trade and the Trees are still many, and it is still arriving on Heskoren, where the grafts are young, scattered, and prone to sickening. On Heskoren, more children are hugged than in the world at large, more are Struck later, and more never stand under a Tree at all. The lifetime mix of the whole world stays about sixty percent Given, fifteen Struck, and twenty-five Kept, and the old heart runs the other way, higher on the Given. Past Heskoren's last capes, a storm-wall of weather, current, and reef has wrecked most crossings. Kumbaan, which sailors call the Sundering Isle, has no Tree, and no graft has ever taken there.

Fifteen great powers stand across those three large continents, and Kumbaan is not a sixteenth of them. Between the fifteen, smaller seats and free towns still answer to their own Trees, and a good deal of wild has no great power on it at all.

Under that wood, ordinary life is late-medieval. Towns hold charters, and people keep guild halls, markets, mills, mechanical clocks, and universities, with letters of credit and the ledgers of banking houses. There is no printing press, so clergy and clerks keep the calendars by hand, and there is no gunpowder. The **Two-Bodied**, whom the old stories call werewolves, do the common labor as neighbours who carry a second, animal self and still decide what that body does, so rescue, herding, and the heavy jobs have a person for them. What reaches past that late-medieval town is the further labor a Condition makes possible, paid for by a Tithe, present where those people live and absent where they are not. The **Long-Lived**, which is what this world calls its vampires, stop aging and forget nothing they have witnessed, so a court, a bank, or a university can keep centuries in one living person. The deathless houses, the banking houses that do not die, will finance a cathedral, a canal, or a university across a span no mortal lender would risk, and if that person is starved of anything new to learn, the memory begins to fade. The same bargain shows in the rest of the work a town cannot do without them: a surgery, because someone can hold a wound still; a mine collapse or a plague ward, because someone who died came back and took the job; thin ground that yields, because someone the wild has taken in works it; a warning that crosses a storm farther than any horn, because a voice can stay true, or because an element answers a maker's hands. A town looks at the work and knows whose becoming paid for it.

This is the **Years of Hands**, the age in which a town's own Tree is the ordinary way a child is Turned. The count this book uses is the **Cut-year**, numbered from the First Cut, the summer someone first cut living wood from the Awakening Tree and the cutting took. It is **Cut-year 387**, the 387th summer since that cut. The walk out to the origin remains, as devotion or as prestige, and it is still a necessity wherever the wood is travelling or a graft has failed. On Heskoren both of those necessities are still real. Other counts start somewhere else, at a crown, at a banking house, or in the year a local graft took, and some people will not start at the Cut at all. Who made the First Cut is still argued, and that the cut happened is not.

This book is the world as it stands.
