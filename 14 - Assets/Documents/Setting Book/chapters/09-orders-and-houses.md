# Orders and Houses

## The Tree-Wardens

The Tree-Wardens are the working clergy of the Motherfaith, the faith built on the Leaf-Mother. They are one order, with offices inside it. A town keeps them because someone has to water the wood, say a colour aloud so the reading counts for the town, and know when a scion is dying. A scion is a living cut of a Turning Tree, carried in earth and planted so a town that does not yet have a mature Tree can grow one. They recruit by the office, by the work, and sometimes by Condition, the change a person lives with and the Tithe, the upkeep that change asks, or the lack of either. They never recruit by Kind, the people a person is born as.

People call them tree-wardens, or just wardens. When a rite or a charter writes the name, it writes **the Orenhael** (OREN-hael), those who say the fall truly and keep the Tree sound. The Waiting Lands, which people also call Vaethorn, say **Hands-folk**. The Ledger Coast, Lestrand, says **tree-tenders**. The Tallied Crown, Threnmaieth, files them as **the Reckoned Hands**.

### Shape of the order

The Motherfaith does not run rival chapters. It is one order with several offices, and those offices sit in the same house. The argument already running inside the faith is three questions at once. One is whether a hug, the holding that leaves no colour, was wholeness or a passing-over, which the faith calls Tender or Wanting. Another is whether a Struck person belongs to the Mother's reach. Someone is Struck when a Condition comes later, at a death, an oath, or a drowning, rather than at Leaf-Fall, the day a child of ten stands under the boughs. That question is Wide-Hand or Narrow-Hand. The third is whether anyone is choosing at all, believer or skeptic. The Orenhael holds that argument inside the one order. A second Motherfaith order would only be a costume.

The wardens are needed before they are holy, because a town has to keep someone who can water the wood, name a rare colour, and know when a scion is dying. Doctrine comes after the watering. That is why a quiet skeptic can hold a town-hearth, and why Lestrand still pays its tenders.

The order is a college at the origin and one hearth in the town. The **First Seat** is that college, not a throne, and a town has one warden-hearth. Someone grows a town Tree from a cutting, and that planting is a **graft**. The Seat cannot direct a graft that has gone far from the Motherwood, and it does not pretend it can.

They tend the wood, they speak the colour, and they carry the graft. They do not police what a person privately believes. The pressure they meet is the Tithe that comes with a Condition, and the line of towns waiting for a Tree. It is not a court for heresy.

### The warden-hearth (the town)

Every town with a mature Tree keeps a **warden-hearth**, a house on the square or in the temple-yard, close enough that blight is smelled before it is announced. The warden, sometimes a small household of an apprentice and an elder, does the civic-sacred work from that house.

Tree-tending is the watering rites, the blight-watch, and the ordinary care of wood that can still die. The Leaf-Fall is the rite they officiate. They bring the child under the boughs, and they wait there with the town.

The Speaking is how they confirm the colour aloud. The colours a neighbour already knows, deep red, storm-grey, lamp-amber, sea-blue, pale stone, and copper-green, need the warden's word before the reading is the town's. Rare, mixed, or argumentative falls are why the office exists. A **white-fire** fall, the leaf that would name a Phoenix, is sent to the First Seat by runner. A town-warden who names a Phoenix from a book has just made history, and a problem.

Receiving the Struck is a quiet rite at the Tree after the fact. It is not a Turning. The Tree does not Strike. How warmly the rite is done follows the warden's Wide-Hand or Narrow-Hand lean, and that difference is how the argument inside the faith reaches the square.

A new town without a mature Tree borrows a neighbour's warden and a neighbour's canopy until its own scion comes of age. That borrowed year is remembered. People in the new town will tell you they Turned at Harrow's Tree before they had their own.

### The Speaking (colour-authority)

The Speaking is an act, and it is not always a separate person. In a small town the warden Speaks. In a pilgrimage-town, or at the First Seat, a **Speaker** is the elder who holds the rare-colour texts and the last word on a mixed fall.

They do not invent the palette. They keep it. People already know the common colours, and the Speaker is the reason a copper-green is not mistaken for the hug, and the reason a Watcher's second reading stays a witness alongside the warden, not a rival authority. Two houses can stand at the same fall. Two Speakers, each claiming the last word in one square, is a fight.

The failure the squares still cite is the Wrong Green, at Harrow's, in Cut-year 248, a hug Spoken aloud as copper-green. A town may Speak a second word over a fall. It may not stand the child again.

### The Road-hands (graft and sickness)

**Road-hands** carry living scions in living earth, plant them with ceremony, and are the people sent when a Tree sickens. The everyday name is the one used everywhere. The liturgical name is **Thaelvaeth** (THAEL-vaeth), the Tree gone to the waiting.

They are the order's thin edge. A dying Tree is a civic crisis, and a waiting town is a political one. The First Seat writes the **Cutting-leave**, the paper that says who may take wood, and for where. The Road-hand walks that paper. Far from the Motherwood they have almost no authority except the scion in the cart and whatever reputation three valleys will grant.

They are sent to keep sickening Trees alive, and to deliver a living scion to a town that has already waited too long. Time works against them, and so does thin soil, and so does a Cutting-leave written for someone else. They work in living earth and mortal wood only. They are a thin circuit of hands for a whole thin reach. A week out from the Motherwood, their authority has already expired. They advise. They do not command a town that has decided to meet a feared Struck person with a rope.

The Seat's support is conditional. A live Tree is a quiet success no one writes down. A dead one is a name on a complaint. Doing the work well gets you the next hard road, not a better cart.

The Tree hugged some people and left them without a Condition. Those are the **Kept**. The road fills with people who already range, with the Kept who will not sit a comfortable hearth, and with the Wide-Hand Struck who still want the Tree to acknowledge them. Any Kind may walk it. The road is not a unit of one people, and it is not stocked with the Seat's favourites.

The college will grant a grudging Cutting-leave, and it will still decide which waiting town is next.

### The First Seat (the college)

The faith's central authority sits in the **Motherwood** about Thaeloren, the Awakening Tree, on Maiethorn. Everyday the college is called **the First Seat**. It is a college of Speakers, elder wardens, and the Cutting-leave desk, and it is not a palace.

What the Seat actually holds, and this is all it holds, comes to three things.

Scions are cut from the Awakening Tree, or, as the work actually goes, from an established healthy town Tree acting on the origin's behalf. The Seat authorizes the cut. It does not uniquely possess every knife. A far college-town that cuts without leave is in schism with the Seat, not with the Tree.

The rare-colour texts are the second thing. They cover Phoenix, and the argumentative mixed falls, and they are the book a town-warden has never opened.

The third thing is the queue, the decision of which waiting settlement receives the next scion. That decision is the order's real power, and its injustice.

The Seat does not appoint every town-warden. A hearth is taken by apprenticeship and confirmed by the town that watched the new warden tend a year, the same kind of witness that makes a citizen, applied to the clergy. The Seat confirms Road-hands and Speakers. That is as far as the appointments reach. A far town's hearth is beyond them.

Beside the college, in a side-yard of the Motherwood, the order keeps a desk called the Remainder. The clerks there are not Speakers. They take back what did not take, what rotted on the road, and what a square swept up after the colours were Spoken. They jar blight so it does not travel with the next leave, and they box the colourless scraps. They do not Speak a colour, and they do not walk a box out of the yard unless someone with leave takes the earth.

The college keeps copies of what its people wrote, and the copies do not share a last line. Someone who asks for the Seat's position can be handed any of these and told it is current.

A warden-hearth in an unnamed Hands-town wrote on the back of a blight-slate.

> *The wood is sound. The child is ten. I will Speak. I will not write the colour onto anyone's roll unless the family asks. The Tree witnessed. A tablet is a second, lesser seeing. If the Seat wants this as a policy, they may send a policy. I have a watering-can.*

A Speaker in the Motherwood wrote the same summer, in a different hand.

> *A colour unspoken to the realm is a colour that can be stolen. File the fall. The hearth that will not copy is doing piety as a gap. We are one order. We are not one breath.*

A Road-hand on the Heskoren front wrote on the inside of a pot-wrap. Heskoren is the continent where the old doors and the grafts share the same ground.

> *The leave was for Harrow's. The three hamlets can see the canopy. I ate at the First Bowl first because the grove asked. The Seat may call that delay. The hill called it a meal. I am out of carrying-earth and out of argument. Send a second pot or send a better sentence than soil.*

A Remainder-clerk wrote in the side-yard after a mixed fall. The Remainder logs the scraps. It does not Speak.

> *Scraps logged. Colourless tissue boxed. The Speaking was storm-grey; the Watcher said amber at the serration. We do not Speak. We do not walk the box unless someone with leave takes the earth. Two accounts, one yard. Do not ask us which summer was true.*

One note is unsigned, and filed under "Reckoned Hands, refused."

> *Tarvae stood beside. I Spoke. I did not copy. The water was loud. If this is insubordination, the college may recall me. If this is the job, the tablet-hall already knows.*

A later hand has written *Rithim?* in the margin and then struck the question. The college has not decided whether that strike is horticulture or politics.

### Who may be a warden

Any Kind may hold the office. A Kitsune hearth-child, slight and quick-eyed, with a fox's ears and a brush of tail, and a human neighbour hold the same office if they can tend wood and speak a colour. The order keeps no separate chapter for foxes, for the ridge-born, or for any other people.

Practice comes first. You are taken as a hand when you are apprenticed to a warden-hearth, and you are confirmed when the town has watched you through a Turning-Week, the week of the Leaf-Fall. What you do at the Tree makes you of the order. People call that orthopraxy. What you privately believe about who chooses is the argument inside the faith, and it is not the hiring test.

Skeptics sit the hearth, and believers gather at the Seat. A town-warden may sincerely say that the Trees simply do what Trees do, and still be a good warden, because the wood is mortal and the colours are already known. The First Seat runs believer-heavy. Pilgrimage wants a Someone, and the rare-colour texts are kept as hers. A skeptic Speaker at Thaeloren is possible, and the college is uncomfortable about it.

A Condition, and the Tithe that comes with it or the lack of one, leans people toward the work that fits. It does not lock the door. Anyone who can do the work may still hold the office.

| Office | Often | Why the Tithe fits | Also, and welcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Town-warden | the Kept | They carry no Tithe, so they can sit a hearth the year round. | Anyone who will tend |
| Speaker (Seat and pilgrimage-towns) | Long-Lived | A Long-Lived person's aging has stopped, and the memory of a rare fall stays. The Phoenix texts are a century's job. | A sharp Kept scholar; a Wide-Hand Struck warden who has seen a threshold-colour from the other side |
| Road-hand | Two-Bodied | A Two-Bodied person also carries an animal body, and the road is the ranging. | Kept; the Taken-In, people the wild has taken partway in, when the job is a sickening grove |
| Grove-tending / blight | the Taken-In | Their Tithe is living green, lived next to living wood. | Root-Answered, a person the root-element still answers, for the carrying-earth |
| Threshold-receiving | Struck wardens (Wide-Hand lean) | They have been on the far side of the door. | Any warden the town will stand for |

The feared and still licensed are not barred. Those are the Stilled, whose gaze can hold a room or a wound still; the heavily Far-Voiced, whose feeling carries into everyone nearby; and the Bound, who live under a contract's Terms. They are rare at the First Seat. A Stilled Speaker is a comfort to a town that wants the room held, and a fright to a pilgrim. A Bound warden is easier on Lestrand, where the faith is already a profession, than in the Motherwood, where a written Term sitting next to a gift-teaching looks like the Fair Hand leaking in. A Phoenix is never clergy, because the Seat has no file for the problem a Phoenix makes.

Struck wardens must still be vouched anew, the way any Struck person must. Someone the town trusts still has to stand and say who they are. The order does not stand in for that witness.

### How they live with the other houses

What people do at the Tree is the peace. The Orenhael officiates the Leaf-Fall, and the other four houses stand at it. The fights stay local.

| House | Everyday arrangement | The hard edge |
|---|---|---|
| the Watching | The same Tree, and two blessings. A Watcher may offer a second reading of a rare fall, a witness alongside the warden and not over them. | Threnmaieth wants one name on the Threnhael, the crown's roll of persons. A Speaker who lets the withheld blessing stand is a column the crown can count. |
| the Fair Hand | The Leaf-Fall is a festival and a signing-watch in the same week. Book-hands and wardens share a square on Lestrand and ignore each other's sentences. | The warden calls the Condition a gift. The Book-hand calls it a debt. A Bound warden lives in both sentences. |
| the Old Ways | A healthy graft is kept as a guest. Kin-speakers who read the Tree as a guest go on pilgrimage and still walk the old doors. | When a Tree sickens in an old grove, the question is who is host, the warden or the door-keeper. Both are neighbours who love the wood. |
| the Open Table | A warden who crosses the storm-wall is a guest at supper, and there is no Tree on Kumbaan, the Sundering Isle, to tend. On the mainland they already share landings, a quay, an attic stair, a list of tenth-year children. | A host of the Open Table will not walk a child of the table to a Tree, and a warden will. That door, the week a child can be Given, is one week. There is no mission-chapter on Kumbaan. |

### How they live in the three corners

It is the same order in each corner. What changes is the local name, and the pressure on the hearth.

**Vaethorn (Hands-folk).** In the Waiting Lands the warden is most of the state a town can see. The reach of the Trees is thin, the faith runs hot, and there is almost no guild to issue a licence. A Road-hand here is doing the Seat's real work: sick Trees, borrowed Turnings, a scion that may not take. On this hearth a Kept apprentice is pitied for missing the gift they are about to spend a life tending.

**Lestrand (tree-tenders).** The warden is a paid civic office. The Leaf-Fall is a commercial milestone, and the warden Speaks so a guild-ticket can open, with the faith worn light. From the docks the Seat's queue looks like any other scarce good, and a Cutting-leave can be brokered. That brokering is Lestrand's sin among its own clergy, and it does not require a heresy.

**Threnmaieth (the Reckoned Hands).** This is the heaviest pressure, because the First Seat stands in the same sacred centre of Maiethorn as the Tallied Crown. The crown wants wardens named to the Threnhael as officers of the Reckoned Faith, the colours written down and the Speakers working as clerks of stewardship. Some Speakers cooperate, because they believe they are keeping the realm hale. Some refuse. The Tree witnessed, they say, and a ledger is a second, lesser seeing. Nearness is the pressure. The Seat can still refuse the roll, and some Speakers do.

### The scion-queue

A graft takes a generation to Turn, and there are always more waiting towns than there is carrying-earth, the living earth a scion travels in, so someone has to decide the order.

The First Seat frames that queue as horticulture. The wood will take here, the soil is thin there, and the carrying-earth is needed for the first. All of that can be true. It is also a choice about who lives another twenty years sending children down the road to a neighbour's canopy, and those years run Kept-heavy and Struck-heavy, which the frontier already knows as weather. The Motherfaith's kindness, if you hold it, did not write the queue. People did.

Road-hands walk the consequence. They plant what they were given leave to plant, they sleep in the town that was not chosen, and they learn not to say *queue*.

### Economy of the order

Towns house and feed the warden-hearth. The stipend is civic and paid in kind: bread, wood, and a place on the square. It is not a tax on Conditions.

Turning-Week is the festival share. There are markets, the reunion, and the year's namings, and the warden eats from the fair the way any other needed office does.

Cutting-leave has a fee. The Seat frames it as the cost of carrying-earth and of a Road-hand's year, and at the edge people experience it as the price of a future Tree. Lestrand will broker the fee, Vaethorn will call it an offering, and Threnmaieth will want it on the roll. This is how the Seat took over a practice that began without a fee.

Pilgrimage keeps the Seat: offerings, lodging, and the devout who settle in the Motherwood. The college is rich in nearness to the Tree, not in a tax on Conditions.

Venting-halls, greens, ranging-commons, and the safety-guilds belong to other houses. Wardens point a new Given, a child who has just received a Condition at Leaf-Fall, toward a guild. They do not licence the Stilled.

### A Cutting-leave

This is a Cutting-leave, in a First Seat hand, carried north in the same earth as the scion. The back is travel-stained. The front is clean.

> *The wood will take at Harrow's green. It will not take this year at the three hamlets past the ford — the soil is thin there, and the carrying-earth is needed for the first. Cut from a healthy hand, not from the First, and married with the watering-words. If the scion sickens, send to the Seat; do not cut a second without leave. The Mother does not reach twice by chance, and neither do we.*

The leave is competent and a little cold. It reuses the Tender's blessing, the sentence in which the Mother does not reach twice by chance, as a shipping clause. The clerk writes that the soil is thin at the three hamlets, and the three hamlets will run Kept-heavy and call it weather.

## The Watchers

The Watchers are the working people of the Watching, the faith that keeps the Tree and withholds the Mother-name. Their own name for that faith is Haelthael. They are not priests, and they have no mother-church, because the First Seat grew over the rooms that would have been one. What they still keep, in those rooms and under those chapels, is a vigil and a second voice. They are the neighbour who will sit up with the Tree the night before Turning-Week, and the second pair of eyes a family sends for when a leaf comes down strange. They describe what they saw, and they do not confirm it, because that confirmation, the civic word, stays with the warden. They have day jobs. The house is a practice, not a wage.

A second reading that becomes a confirmation is the house breaking itself. A Night Watch that becomes a hunt is the house breaking itself the other way.

People call them **Watchers**. The rite writes **the Nethoren** (NETH-or-en), those who keep the night and speak what was seen. The Waiting Lands say **the night-kin**. The Ledger Coast barely has a name, and a clerk who needs a label says **vigil-folk**. The Tallied Crown files the house as **the Grown-Over** and the people who keep the vigil as **the second column**.

### Shape of the order

They have no seat. The Watching has no college in the Motherwood, because that seat was grown over. Their oldest rooms sit under older chapels, or in houses that never took a warden's mark. Those rooms are the belief they keep: the Watching was already here. A Watcher who wants a mother-church has already become something else.

The house has two acts, and it does not keep four offices. The **Night Watch** is what the house does, and anyone of Haelthael may keep it. The **Second Reading** is a reputation, not an office. It is the person a town will ask to sit beside the warden when a fall is rare, mixed, or argued. There is no Speaker with the last word, no Road-hand circuit, and no Cutting-leave desk. Those jobs belong to a faith that has a Tree to carry.

A town does not keep a Watcher the way it keeps a warden. The wood still wants watering, and that watering is the warden's. What a Watcher is for is accurate speech, and a night of matching the Tree's attention with their own. Lestrand can live without that. Threnmaieth cannot stop counting it.

They do not police the Mother-name in the street. They withhold it. The pressure they meet is a door-beam turned so its older face sits against the stone, and a roll that wants a name for the withheld word. That pressure is not a hunt.

### If you meet one

A Watcher looks like a neighbour. You might meet a baker, a weaver, a Long-Lived person who copies texts, or one of the Kept who sits a quiet hearth. They do not wear a warden's mark, because that mark belongs to the warden. You know them because a neighbour said to ask the old room, or to send for the one who sat last year's mixed fall, or because you saw them under the Tree the night before Turning-Week with no lamp and no blessing.

They are useful the way a second opinion is useful. A town can live without them. A family that does not trust a single speaker on a strange leaf cannot.

### An ordinary year

Eleven months of the year they are not on duty. They bake, they copy, they range, and they keep a stall. The house-work that still happens in those months is the teaching, the naming of what was seen, and the care of the old room.

They teach the withheld blessing at the hearth, the year a child turns nine. It has the same cadence as the Tender's line, and the Mother is not in it. This is how you can tell a Watcher household in a mixed street. The child can recite both lines, or will only recite one.

After any Turning they attend, they name what they saw. It is not a sermon. They sit with their own child, or with a neighbour's if they were asked, and they make the fall into accurate speech: the colour, the way the leaf came, and what the year will ask. Devotion, in this house, is the description. A sloppy Watcher is a bad Watcher.

They sweep the old room. If the house still has a recut beam, they keep the hidden face of that beam from rotting. They hang the Night Watch coats. In Threnmaieth they answer the clerk who wants to be told that the room is still a room.

They do not water the Tree. That is the warden-hearth. A Watcher who starts watching for blight has started doing the Orenhael's job.

### The Night Watch

The night before Turning-Week is the eve of Eolthael, the high-solstice month, in a town that keeps the clergy's year. That is the night the Watchers keep a silent vigil at the town Tree. There is no blessing, no colour-reading, and no lamps if they can help it. The point is to be seen keeping the night.

This is not an office you are appointed to. A household that speaks the withheld blessing is expected to send someone. A town with three Watcher families may have six people under the boughs and no one in charge. A town with one old room under the chapel may have a single Long-Lived person who has kept the same night for eighty years. Both are the order, such as it is. They count the silences, not the hours. No one will explain this to a guest. A first-time sitter who asks how long is told, by whoever is already sitting, that they sit until it has been seen.

They do not officiate the Leaf-Fall the next day. The warden does that. They attend, because the practice is what binds them and because their children Turn, and they speak their own line when the Tender's blessing is said.

### The Second Reading

Where a town will have it, a Watcher may sit a **second reading** of a rare or mixed fall. It is not authoritative over the warden. It is a witness alongside. They describe what they saw, and they do not confirm it. Confirmation is the town's word, and it belongs to the Speaking.

The hour runs like this.

1. The child is still under the boughs, or has just been walked to the warden-hearth, and the town is still there. The warden Speaks. Storm-grey, say.
2. If a household asked, or if the fall was mixed enough that the square muttered, the Watcher is already standing to the side, and they do not interrupt the Speaking.
3. They say what they saw, in the square or afterward in the old room: the stem, the serration, the turn, the flinch or the lack of one. They will use colour-words. They will resist Condition-names if the leaf was not one colour.
4. Most years the town nods at both accounts and goes to the fair. The child is called Two-Bodied because the warden Spoke that name, and also someone said there was amber at the edge, and that sentence lives in the family.

A Second-reader is simply the Watcher three streets will send for when the fall is argumentative. Neither a diploma nor a confirmation from the Seat is what makes them one. A Watcher may describe the same fall while the warden Speaks. Two Speakers, each with the last word, is the fight the First Seat will not permit, and the Watching does not ask for it.

In Threnmaieth they are a known column on the Threnhael. The second reading is tolerated as counted dissent, the night is tolerated as a festival-eve custom, and the withheld word is what the roll wants a name for.

### What they carry

They carry almost nothing that looks like clergy. They keep a scrap for the description, plain paper and not a book, because a book would be a Settler's tool, and the Settlers are the Book-Hands. A coat lives in the old room and smells of last year's eve. In a Bare Tree household, where they will not say *she* at all, there is no Mother-mark on the door-frame. In a Masked one, where they will stand for the Tender's blessing and add the withheld line under it, the warden's mark is on the frame, and a second, older scratch sits underneath, where a clerk can miss it.

They have no stamp, no Cutting-leave, and no ticket, so they cannot licence a Stilled person and they cannot plant a Tree.

### What they refuse

They will not Speak, and if a town has no warden that year they will still only describe, because a civic confirmation from a Watcher is the house breaking itself. They will not take a warden's mark. Their oldest rooms are in houses that never took one, and under the chapels built over those rooms. They will not police the Mother-name in the street. They withhold it. They will not travel as a circuit, because a Watcher in another town is a cousin visiting.

There is no chapter-house circuit. The order meets, when it meets, because Turning-Week gathers people who already keep the same night. What looks like organisation from the outside is the building itself: the house that never took a warden's mark, the beam that was turned to the stone when the chapel was re-floored, and the room under the nave where the Night Watch leaves its coats. The First Seat knows those rooms exist. It grew over them. It does not inventory them, except in Threnmaieth, where inventory is the local weather.

### Who may keep the watch

Whoever will sit the night may keep it. A Kitsune hearth-child and a human neighbour keep the same night if they will sit it and speak accurately. Kind does not sort the watch, and the Watching has no chapter inside it that only one people may join.

Practice comes first. You are of the house when you keep the Night Watch and teach a child the withheld blessing. That belonging is older than the Motherfaith's. The withheld word is the dissent, and the standing at the Tree is the belonging.

The argument inside the house walks into the square. A **Masked** Watcher will stand for the Tender's blessing and add the withheld line under it, and a mixed town barely notices them. A **Bare Tree** Watcher will not say *she* at all. Both may sit a second reading. They will not always read the same fall the same way. That is the house arguing with itself in the square, which the square allows, and it is why they cannot present a single rival authority even if they wanted one.

A Condition, or the lack of one, may lean a person toward the night or the second reading. It does not close the door.

A Phoenix is a seeing no one in the square is ready to describe twice.

Struck Watchers must still be vouched anew, the way any Struck person must. The old rooms do not stand in for that witness.

The warden officiates, the Watcher withholds a word, and the conflict stays local.

### How they live in the three corners

It is the same house in each corner. What changes is the name, and the pressure.

**Vaethorn (the night-kin).** This is cousin-dissent in the Waiting Lands. The reach is thin and the faith is hot, and a Watcher household is unusual and not hunted. From a Hands-folk hearth the Night Watch looks like extra piety. The withheld Mother-name is the only sting, and a town that reads the Tree as a guest has larger fights, the grove among them, than this one.

**Lestrand (vigil-folk, when named).** They are rarely met. A Watcher on the docks is a scholar, or someone who came from the sacred centre. The signing-watch does not need a second reading, because the book is already the second reading. Faith is worn light enough that a withheld blessing is just another sentence on the quay.

**Threnmaieth (the second column).** This is where the pressure is heaviest. They are a known column on the roll, the Grown-Over, counted, and the crown does not hunt the households. The Night Watch is a festival-eve custom the crown can schedule. The second reading is tolerated until it contradicts the Speaker the Threnhael has already named. Laevila, a Long-Lived baker on Maiethlir's old extra-mile, the street where people still walk past what the local rite already gave them, still has the only copy of the beam from before it was recut. The pressure is a tolerated column on the roll, not a hunt.

### The recut beam

They remember a cost, and they have no institution that can keep a beam from being turned to the stone.

The First Seat did not steal a throne from them. There was no throne. What was lost is quieter: a beam recut, a room under a chapel that is now a vestry, a second reading that remains alongside because the civic word was given to the warden when the Mother-name grew over the seeing. All of that can be described as history. It is also a choice, still being made, about whose speech counts in the square.

A second reading does not buy a seat, and the Grown-Over column stays a column. They have hold of the Before and of the cost. They do not have the last word on a colour. A settled Mother-name would not promote them.

They do not walk a queue of scions. That grievance belongs to the Orenhael. The one they live with is the recut beam.

### Economy of the house

As a house, they have almost no money of their own, and that is how the practice stays a practice. Households keep the night, and the bread comes from the same table. There is no stipend, no Cutting-leave, and no share of a pilgrimage. A Second-reader eats as a neighbour. Some old districts leave a plate at the room under the chapel during Turning-Week, and that plate is courtesy, not a wage.

Threnmaieth has a tell. Being a column can become a living if a crown-clerk pays for a copied beam or a listed name. A Watcher who takes that coin has not left the house. They have let the roll furnish it. The Bare Tree branch will not eat with them. The Masked will, and they will argue after.

They point no one at a licence. They describe a fall. The halls, the greens, and the tickets belong to other houses.

### A second-reading slip

This is a second-reading slip, written in a Watcher's own hand the morning after a mixed fall, and filed by a Threnmaieth Speaker under "alongside, not over." The Speaker's docket-mark is cleaner than the slip.

> *Storm-grey at the stem, lamp-amber at the serration, and the child did not flinch when the leaf turned. I will not say Two-Bodied. I will not say Answered. I will say what I saw: the Tree looked twice and did not choose the easier colour. The warden Spoke storm-grey. I sat beside and said the amber. Both were seen. File me as the second column if you must. Do not file the amber as an error.*

The writer is a careful witness, a little stubborn, and useful to a clerk who needs a name for dissent. The slip's whole case is that seeing something is not the same as choosing it. The Speaker who filed it alongside was keeping the peace between the warden and the Watcher.

## The Book-Hands

The Book-Hands are the working people of the Fair Hand, the faith that keeps an account fair enough that a person can walk away from it. Their own name for that faith is Leddoren. They are notaries who are also theologians. In the morning they may stamp a hull. In Turning-Week they stand with a child's private book. When a Struck person comes to the table, someone who Changed later, by drowning, by death, or by being taken, and who cannot live this year as the town is, they help that person find a payable year: a hall, a warrant, and someone who will stand. On oath-day they will recite a Bound person's Terms, the year's obligations already written down, where a neighbour can hear them. They will not change those Terms.

They have no seat and many tables. On Lestrand they are often the same people as the commercial clerks, and the faith is the profession, worn lightly. The house splits on one question, which is whether anyone sits across the table when a Bound person wants the clause itself changed. A Counterparty hand believes someone is there to be asked, and may sit and wait. A Book Alone hand does not believe the far side of the table is occupied, and will not sit that hour. Neither hand will rewrite the Terms, and neither will sell an easing. The house calls that other party, when it believes in one, the **Counterparty**.

People call them **Book-hands**, or **Settlers**. The rite writes **the Leddhael** (LEDD-hael), those who keep the account fair enough that you can walk. The Waiting Lands say **Bargainers' clerks**. The Ledger Coast says **the Settling-hands**, ordinary, the way a quay says notary. The Tallied Crown files them as **the Uncounted Book**. Private books offend the crown.

### Shape of the order

The Fair Hand keeps guild-rooms and quay-shrines, and it does not keep a college in anyone's sacred wood. A high house in a rich port is the oldest book-room, old because the copies have piled up, not because that room can give the next quay leave to stamp. The next quay has its own table, and it does not write to the first for permission.

The faith is the profession. A Book-hand witnesses a signing, teaches a child to keep a book, and helps a Struck person settle a year that can actually be lived, which means a hall, a warrant, and a neighbour who will stand. On Lestrand the same person may stamp a hull before noon and recite a Bound person's oath-day after. The teaching of the house is the ethic of the stamp: fair terms, then walk. They copy a Bound person's Terms. They do not author them.

There is no membership roll. A roll of Book-hands would be a Threnhael sitting in a clerk's pocket, and the house will not make one. What makes you of the house is that you keep accounts honestly, and that you will die to keep a crown-clerk out of a personal book.

They sit at tables. They witness a signing and copy what was already written. They do not ride out, and they do not hunt hidden terms as an order of knights. The pressure they meet is the book that will not close.

### If you meet one

On Lestrand a Book-hand looks like a clerk, ink-stained, with a small stamp on a cord and a table in a guild-room or in a quay-shrine the size of a pantry. Before noon they may witness a hull, and after that they may stand a Bound person's oath-day, and the sentence is the same both times: fair wind, fair term. You would not know which hour was the rite unless you already kept Leddoren.

Off the coast they are rarer, and they look more obviously religious, because a pilgrim-town sends for them when a Struck person cannot live this year, with no hall and no one to stand, while the Hands-folk of that town would rather walk the person to a Tree.

They are useful the way a notary is useful. A port cannot live without someone who will stamp a signing. The faith holds that the stamp on a contract and the record of a becoming are the same kind of account. In this house's book, the becoming is the colour and the Tithe written down beside what the year owes.

### An ordinary week on Lestrand

The morning is the commercial table. Hulls, marriages, copies, and a deathless house's instrument are paid by the stamp. A Kept clerk, one of Lestrand's free, who carries no Tithe and belongs to no guild and for whom the book is a choice, may do only this hour and still be of the house, if they keep accounts honestly and will die to keep a crown-clerk out of a personal book.

Turning-Week and the oath-days are the signing-watch. They attend the Leaf-Fall as witnesses, and they do not officiate. They stand at the edge of the square with a child's first book and wait for a colour they can enter. A Bound person's oath-day is the high holy day. The Terms are recited in a voice the quay can hear, and the year's book is closed or marked owing. The Motherfaith calls this showing off. The Fair Hand calls it the only honest worship.

When someone Changed last week, the work is civic settling, and it does not rewrite a god's clause. A drowning came back Far-Voiced in a town with no ael-hall, no hall where a carried feeling can go. A death came back Returned, a person who returned with a purpose the street will not house. The Book-hand finds a year that can be paid: passage, a warrant, a neighbour who will stand. If no one will stand, they write *owing*. The quay steps around. That is still the house's kindness and its cruelty. It is not a Bound person's Terms being edited.

On a Bound person's oath-day they recite what was already sworn, and they do not improve it. A Bound person who wants the clause itself changed is asking the Counterparty, not the clerk. The honest house will witness the ask, or it will refuse to pretend that the clerk can answer. Anyone selling a new clause after the lamps is another table.

They never write the word *gift*, even as a loan. A first-year apprentice who copies *vael*, the word for a gift, into a personal book, in the line where *gift* would have stood, is made to strike it and write *grant*. No one calls this a rule. It is how the ink goes.

They do not cut grafts, tend Trees, or carry scions. A sickening Tree is a warden's crisis and a door-keeper's fight, and a Book-hand will only write the delay into a contract.

No Book-hand holds a last word over the others. If two Book-hands disagree on a clause, they write both readings and date them. The book holds both readings. That is not a split in the house. That is a book.

### How civic settling actually runs

This hour is for a Struck person whose Tithe cannot be lived in this town. It is not for a Bound person's Terms. The house calls the settling a **novation**: the payable civic terms of a year, passage or a warrant or a seventh day, are written onto a year someone can pay. The Bound person's clause is never what gets rewritten.

1. The Struck person sits, and the Book-hand names what is already true, in the words the house uses: you carry a voice, or you carry a purpose.
2. They name what cannot be paid here, a hall that is not in this town, or a warmth no one will give.
3. They find a year a neighbour can see being paid, which may be passage to a release-house, a warrant, or a seventh day.
4. They find someone to stand for the person. The Book-hand is not themselves one of the three pillars a town will accept as proof, unless the Book-hand is Bound, Long-Lived, or Far-Voiced.
5. If that fails, the mark is *owing, not broken*. The civic book stays open, and the quay learns the name.

### How a Bound petition-watch runs (if they sit it)

1. The Bound person speaks the ask, and the Book-hand writes the ask, not the answer.
2. They wait, and they do not invent a clause to fill the silence.
3. If nothing answers, they close the hour. The old Terms still stand, and the house was honest.
4. If something answers, they copy the new clause as heard. They do not improve the wording, and they do not take a fee for getting a better deal.

A Book Alone hand stops at the first step and sends the Bound person home. They will not play at a table they believe is empty. That is also honest.

### What they carry

They carry a stamp that is personal, and it is not a guild-licence. The ticket that lets a guild work is a different paper, and it belongs to the Stillers, the Hall-Keepers, and the Element-Guilds. They carry ink, and a cord-knife for striking a line. They sell, or give, the small personal books to children of the house. Those books are not the Threnhael, and a clerk who confuses the two has started a fight. On a Bound person's oath-day they have the year's working contract in hand, and they read it aloud as written, not as improved.

A high house is a room with old copies and a better lamp. It is not a palace. The next quay does not write here for leave.

### What they refuse

They will not show a personal book to the crown, and in their language a crown order that demands one is stealing a soul. They will not call a Leaf-Fall a gift, and they will not rewrite a Given child's past so the child was always a debtor, because the book opens at the colour and does not invent an earlier debt. They stay at the table. They do not ride out, and they do not carry a scion. They will not keep a membership roll of Book-hands, because a roll would be a Threnhael, and they will not rewrite a Bound person's Terms or sell an easing of a Pledge, because the clerk is not the Counterparty.

### Who may keep a table

Any Kind may hold the stamp. A Selkie quay-child, sleek and cool-skinned, carrying a sealskin that can be worn or set aside, and a human clerk hold the same stamp if they keep accounts honestly. Leddoren has no chapter of seals and no notary reserved to one people.

Practice comes first. You are taken as a hand when you are apprenticed at a table, and you are confirmed when a quay has watched you through a year's closings and at least one signing-watch. What you privately believe about whether anyone sits across the table, Counterparty or Book Alone, is the split inside the house, and it is not the hiring test. Both branches refuse hidden terms. That is enough to share a desk.

A Condition, or the lack of one, may lean a person toward the work. It does not lock the door.

The feared and still licensed are not barred. A Bound Book-hand is ordinary on Lestrand, and a problem for doctrine in the Motherwood, where a written Term sitting next to a gift-teaching looks, to a Speaker, like this house leaking in. A Stilled person at a table is rare and useful, because the room holds still for the clause. A Phoenix is not a clerk. There is no term that covers them.

Struck Book-hands must still be vouched anew. Civic settling is how the house helps that vouching, which is also how the house can pressure the person it settled. It still does not rewrite a Bound person's clause.

### How they live in the three corners

**Lestrand (the Settling-hands).** This is home, and here the faith is the profession. A high house is a proud old room on a rich quay, and it is still not a seat over the other tables. When a Tree sickens, the delay can be written into a contract, and on this coast a Cutting-leave can be brokered by the same people who close books. That brokering is Lestrand's sin among the wardens, and it belongs to the wardens' fee, not to a Book-hand's stamp, except in the hour when a clerk helps a waiting town write the delay. The Tithe-poor Settler who cannot close the book is still a person the rich city steps around, and the faith treats that step-around as part of the rite.

**Vaethorn (Bargainers' clerks).** On the pilgrim edge they are suspect, useful, and few. A Book-hand there is settling a Struck person's year in a land that would rather take them to a Tree to be acknowledged as Given. The Hands-folk will eat with them if the year comes out kind. A Narrow-Hand preacher will not.

**Threnmaieth (the Uncounted Book).** Private books offend the Tallied Crown. A personal ledger that is not the Threnhael is, to a Reckoned clerk, a second seeing the crown does not hold. Book-hands here work quietly, or not at all, or as commercial notaries who have learned not to say *Leddoren* on a docket. A demand for a Settler's book is, in their language, stealing a soul.

### The book that will not close

The house settles a person's year and closes the book. That is its kindness, and its cruelty.

Every civic settling that saves a Struck person who can pay is paid for, somewhere, by the Struck person who cannot. The Book-hand sees one table. The quay sees a step-around. A settled name for the Mother would not make that fading kind, and it would not turn a Bound person's clause into a clerk's draft.

Their other hard case is the Bound person they cannot help. A clerk who will not rewrite Terms will watch someone walk down the quay looking for a cellar that will lie to them. That cellar is the Holding Desk. The refusal to rewrite remains honest, and it is no longer a reason the clerk cannot follow. The week of upkeep sold off the books is the Slide. A witness sold for coin is the Standing Trade. Book-hands do not stamp those books either.

### Economy of the house

The stamp is the living. On Lestrand a Book-hand is paid as a clerk is paid, by the signing, the copy, and the year's closing. The faith does not take a cut of the Tithe.

High houses endow rooms. Money that does not die likes a fair copy that will outlive a hull. That wealth is nearness to the quay, not a tax on Conditions, and it is why the oldest rooms sit on the richest quays.

Civic settling is badly paid. The Struck people who most need a hall and a warrant are the ones who can least stamp a fee. Some Book-hands stay until the candle dies, and some mark *owing* faster, and both are of the house.

They may write a term that assumes a venting-hall or a share of a green, and they do not furnish one, because the halls and the greens are other houses' work.

### A civic settling

This is a civic settling, written by a Lestrand quay-hand the week after a drowning came back Far-Voiced and could not pay a singer's Tithe in a town that had no ael-hall. The back has the old storm-terms of the year, struck through. Those struck lines are not a Bound person's clause.

> *Grant: the voice, already carried. Old term: a hall that is not here. New term: one honest outlet each seventh day at the release-house in Greyhull, passage stamped, warrant by M. Senn the hull-clerk. If the seventh day is missed, the book is owing, not broken. Walk, if you can walk. If you cannot, the quay will not call it a gift.*

The slip is the house's kindness: a storm-year's terms rewritten so a person can live. "The quay will not call it a gift" is honest, and it is also how the quay steps around the person. Greyhull's release-house will take the seventh day. The eighth week the passage-money fails, the slip will still be fair, and the person will still owe a book that does not close.

## The Door-Keepers

The Door-Keepers are the working people of the Old Ways, the faith that still walks the land's old doors. Their own name for that faith is Vaeloren. They have no seat in a city and no ladder of rank above them, because the land is the seat, and a person becomes a door-keeper when the district already treats them as one. For a famous one, that means three valleys will walk to them. A hill, a drowning-stone, a grove, or an oath-rock is the office they keep, and there is no mother-church that can overrule the door.

People call them **door-keepers**, and also **Kin-speakers**. The rite writes **the Vaelbren** (VAEL-bren), those who keep the land's yield at the hearth. In the older sense, *vael* is what the land yields, a harvest and a returned child, and *bren* is the hearth. The Waiting Lands say **the door-folk**. The Ledger Coast says **grove-talkers**. The Tallied Crown files them as **the Unreached**.

They are the person three valleys walk to when someone needs to know which drowning-pool still returns and which grove still takes people in, and they are the person who names a Struck person kin at the door that made them, instead of walking them to a Tree to be filed as late.

### Shape of the order

The Old Ways cannot have a mother-church, so the land stays the seat. A hill, a drowning-stone, a grove, or an oath-rock is the office, and a person is of the house when three valleys will walk to them, not when a college confirms them.

Two jobs sit in the house, and they are often the same person. A door-keeper knows which doors still open and will walk them at the year's hinges, the solstices, the equinoxes, and the local turns a city calendar does not print. A Kin-speaker speaks at the ancestor-meals, where a place is set for the ones in the ground, and names a Struck person kin at the door that made them. In a thin-reach district, where a warden may not come for a generation, this is one tired person. In a town that reads the Tree as a guest, that finally got a Tree and will still pilgrimage to it while walking the old doors, the two jobs may split for a generation and fold back into one person when the old one dies.

They are not Road-hands. They keep no queue and do no Speaking. A Road-hand carries a scion up the road. The Door-Keepers do not carry scions, do not confirm colours, and do not authorise a cut. When a graft sickens in an old green, they argue over who is host, not over the horticulture, and the Road-hands already walk that other job.

This is a house of land and kin. They do not police the Mother-name, and many of them also bring a child to a healthy graft when they can reach one. The pressure they actually meet is a grove both houses claim, and a Struck person the town has not vouched. That is a harder ordinary week than any holy war.

### If you meet one

A door-keeper looks like someone who knows the district. There is mud on the hem, a worn token at the belt, and a sack that is sometimes food for a door and sometimes food for a meal. The token is a river-stone, a grove-twig, or a pinch of hill in a twist of cloth, and no two of them are alike. You would miss them as clergy if you came from Maiethorn looking for a chapel. You would find them if you asked who still walks the drowning-stone, or who will speak at the board for the ones in the ground.

They are useful the way a midwife is useful, except the birth they attend is a later becoming, someone who Changed at a door rather than a child just born, and the map they keep is which of those doors still open. A thin-reach town can live without a warden for a generation. It cannot live long without someone who remembers the water.

| Who | Why they come | What the door-keeper does | What they will not do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anyone in the district, at a year's hinge | The doors have to be walked at Deep Solstice, the long night and the low year, at the equinoxes, at High Solstice, and at the local hinges a printed map leaves off. | They walk. They greet water, then hill, then stone, leave food, and ask to be known, not Given a Condition. ### An ordinary season

Most weeks, someone asks whether the pool below the ford still returns, and the door-keeper goes and looks. They know which grove took someone in ten years ago, and whether that hill is still a door, and when the hinge is close they carry food for it. Under the reputation they have a day life, ranging or tending green or keeping a stall, and the reputation sits on top of that life rather than replacing it. A famous door-keeper is simply the one three valleys will walk to. A young one is the apprentice who carried the food last year and was watched doing it.

The walk is not only High Solstice and Turning-Week. They also walk Deep Solstice, both equinoxes, and the local hinges a Maiethorn calendar does not print, the first flood and the night the hill-ice breaks. They walk the old doors of the district and greet them water, then hill, then stone, and they will not say why that order is the one they keep. A guest who reverses it is corrected, and nobody turns the correction into a sermon.

At the meal there is a place at the board for the ones in the ground, and the hour is quiet and domestic. The Kin-speaker is the one who knows the names. In a thin-reach district this is the same tired person who walked the ford at dawn.

When someone Changed later, they do not start at the Tree. They start at the door: the river if it was water, the grove if it was green, the oath-stone if it was a vow, and the death-place if it was unfinished work. There they name the person kin. The Motherfaith's quiet receiving-rite, the one that can acknowledge the same person as Given, may happen the same week, and a Book-hand's novation may happen the same month. It is three courtesies, not a trial. The hard case is the Struck person the town will not house. The door-keeper has a name to give and not a civic pillar to stand on, unless they themselves are Long-Lived, Bound, or Far-Voiced, so they may spend a season walking with one person, and they may not be able to.

If the town has a healthy graft, they tend it as a guest in the grove. They may Turn under it. They will not pretend the graft was what invented becoming.

### How a kin-naming actually runs

1. They find the door, if it can be found. If it cannot, they use the nearest old door of the same kind, water for a drowning and green for a taking. A door that is only a Tree is a last resort, and a First-reading walker, one who keeps the old doors first and will not take the Tender's blessing, will hate it.
2. Food comes first. The door is greeted, and the person is not tested with a colour.
3. The Kin-speaker says the name the person already had, and then the kin-name the door is owed. There is no leaf and no stamp. The token at the belt is shown, not given, unless this person will walk next year, in which case they will find their own.
4. The town, if it is watching, still wants a warrant before it lets them stay. The kin-name is a courtesy, and standing-for, someone the town trusts saying who the person is, is the civic act beside it. A door-keeper who is also Long-Lived, Bound, or Far-Voiced can do both, and most cannot.

### The contested grove

An ailing graft in an old Taken-In green is the fight people know this house by, and you can see the shape of it from the path. It is warden against door-keeper, guest against host, and the scarcity that sends people on pilgrimage set against the old answer, which is that the grove already had ways. Both are neighbours who love the wood.

For an hour their hands do the same work. They water, they watch the blight, and they sit up with wood that can still die. What they are arguing about is not the care of the wood, but whether this Tree is a guest in the grove or a Tree the town failed. A Guest-reading Kin-speaker will go on pilgrimage and still walk the doors, and will tend a dying graft as kin. A First-reading door-keeper will tend the same graft as a guest in their house and still refuse the Tender's blessing. Both revere the Struck and the Taken-In. A reading that starts pitying them as "unreached" has already become Motherfaith, and this house will not use that word.

If a Road-hand arrives with Cutting-leave, the door-keeper's job is the meal first, and then the question of whether this grove receives a guest. They do not carry scions.

### What they carry

They carry the token from their door, a river-stone or a grove-twig or a pinch of hill, and they carry food for the walk. They carry names in the head, the ones in the ground and the doors that still open. They do not carry a stamp, a colour-book, or Cutting-leave.

### What they refuse

Confirming a colour is not their work, and neither is officiating a Leaf-Fall. If they bring a child to a healthy graft, the warden Speaks. They will not carry a scion, and they will not sell a cut. They will not call the Struck or the Taken-In "unreached," because that word is the Motherfaith leaking into a house that does not use it. Some doors they will walk and will not publish, so a curious clerk does not leave with a map of every one. They will not become priests of the undeath. An ancestor-meal is for kin who remained in the ground, and the Returned are a different door.

### Who may keep a door

Any Kind may keep a door. A Tengu ridge-child, lean and sharp-featured, with dark feathers through the hair and down the arms, and a human neighbour walk the same stone if they know it and will stand for kin. Vaeloren has no chapter only the ridge-folk may join, and no grove-guard that only the Taken-In may enter. The Taken-In are revered here. Reverence is not a membership test.

Practice comes first. You are taken as a walker once you have carried the food, been watched at a meal, and earned three households that will send for you. What you believe about the Mother, whether you read her as Guest or as First, is secondary to that practice. Many Kin-speakers also bring a child to a healthy graft when they can reach one.

A Condition, or the lack of one, leans a person toward the walk they already know. It does not bar the others.

### How they live in the three corners

**Vaethorn (the door-folk).** This is home ground, and it is where the fight stays live. The warden is most of the state a town can see, and the door-keeper is the older state the land still keeps. Towns that read the Tree as a guest, and that finally got one, did not throw their grandparents out of the rite. They go on pilgrimage to the Tree, and they still walk the old doors. In the First-reading districts, where a scion still will not take, they tend the dying guest and they refuse the blessing. What they are up against is the Waiting Lands' habit of reading a scarce gift as unworthiness, while the Hands-folk hearth stays a neighbour's house. A closed door is weather, something the land does, and it is neither a slight nor a verdict that the town was unworthy.

**Lestrand (grove-talkers).** On this coast a door-keeper is a curiosity from the frontier. One on the docks is a migrant or a story. The Settling will stamp a Struck person's terms and call the kin-name poetry. The house is useful if someone needs a drowning-stone found. It is not a rival for the quay.

**Threnmaieth (the Unreached).** The crown files them as a gap, people who were not at a proper Tree, and doors the roll cannot name. A Kin-speaker in the core is rare enough that a crown-archivist finds the rarity interesting, and the census wants a column to set them in. The land will not become that column. What follows from the want is pressure, and it is not a hunt.

### The host of a dying guest

Someone has to decide whether a sickening graft is a guest in the grove or a Tree the town failed.

The Road-hand arrives with leave and with a case about the soil. The door-keeper arrives with grandparents and with food. Both of them can be right about the wood. The years the grove spends arguing are years in which children walk to a neighbour's canopy, and the children who make that walk are Kept-heavy and Struck-heavy. The frontier already knows that mix as weather, the ordinary way of a thin place, and Vaethorn will sanctify the same mix as unworthiness if no one says otherwise.

Their complaint is not a queue. It is who is host, and who will stand for the Struck person the town will not. A healthy Tree in the grove would not change which wood came first, and it would not make a closed door into a slight. The grievance stays where they left it. The grafts are late, and the Motherfaith did talk as if becoming were new.

### Economy of the house

A door-keeper eats at the boards they speak for. There is no stipend from a seat. A famous one is housed because three valleys would be ashamed not to house them.

They hold no Cutting-leave and they charge no fee, because they do not sell wood. If a town that reads the Tree as a guest wants a scion, that coin goes to the Seat, not to the Kin-speaker. A First-reading district that pays for a Cutting-leave and still refuses the blessing is doing both courtesies at once, and the argument about it will start after the work is done.

Standing for the Struck is unpaid. The people who most need a kin-name are the ones a town will not house. Some door-keepers spend a year on one person, and some cannot, and both are of the house.

Garden-commons and ranging-commons belong to other houses. A Taken-In door-keeper may live in a green without holding any licence over it.

### Left tied to an ailing graft

Someone left this tied to an ailing graft in an old Taken-In green, the week a Road-hand's cart was seen at the ford. Two hands, and one knife-cut for the string.

> *The tree is young. The grove is not. We have fed it as a guest and we will feed it still. If you have come with leave, come to the meal first. If you have come with a second cut in mind, know that the hill already yielded a child this year and does not owe you a colour. We will not let either wood pretend it was first.*

The writer is polite and a little barbed, and the note takes a saying from a drowning-stone and sets it on a trunk. The house's case is that becoming happens at doors where there is no healthy Tree. The Road-hand who eats first will plant as a guest. The one who cuts without the meal will be remembered as a claiming, and the wood may still take.

## The Table-Keepers

The Table-Keepers are the working people of the Open Table, the faith kept on Kumbaan, the Sundering Isle. Their own name for that faith is Ndeyaan. They are the hill-hosts, and they are almost not clergy. When the faith travels at all, it travels as a Yumboe, one of the small pearl-pale people of the isle, keeping an attic abroad, and that happens rarely. The storm-shore is a different job on the same isle, and the Shore-Sitters are the ones who keep it.

On the mainland people say **table-keepers**, and also **hill-hosts**. Their own name is **the Njaalo** (NJAH-lo), a Yumboe coinage in the night-keeping tongue, "the hall that is kept." It is not a Maiethren word, and this house owes the liturgy nothing. Sailors say **moon-hosts**. Vaethorn has them as a sailor's tale. Lestrand says **the Moon-Feast people** when a quay wants a story. Threnmaieth does not file them. They are off the map.

They are the person who opens the hall, or the attic, and lays an extra place. On Kumbaan that is how a valley eats and how a stranger becomes kin. On the mainland it is one room, one supper, and a sentence that will not travel past the door.

The faith's quarrel is two sittings set against a Turning-witness, which is belonging because a town watched you Turn, and it is also a tenth-year week that a host will not spend walking a child to a Tree. What follows is the table itself: who is fed, what the host refuses, and what they carry.

### Shape of the order

Ndeyaan is almost not an order. It has no chapel, no college, no authority over colours, no graft, no book, and no door-walk that wants a title. The person whose hall the valley comes to on the bright nights is a table-keeper, and that is reputation rather than an office. When they die, the valley comes to the next hall that still sets the extra place.

There is one job, and there is no second office to be promoted into. The table-keeper keeps the table, lays the extra place, and on the second sitting says that no one is a stranger twice. That sentence is the whole of the work.

There is no seat across the sea, and there is no mission. A Yumboe who wants a hill-hall on Strandoren, the harbour continent west of Maiethorn, keeps Ndeyaan in whatever room they have, and they do not send for a scion, a stamp, or a Night Watch. The mainland does not send wardens to Kumbaan. The storm-wall, the weather and reef that close the isle, is the boundary.

This is a hospitality-house. It is not a bargain struck with a fairy, and it is not a guild of one Kind. The unseen hands fetch and pour as part of the hall, and you can see the cups move. They are not a binding laid on a guest, and sitting the table does not put a guest in debt. A human who has sat twice and will lay the place is of the table. A Yumboe who will not is just a neighbour who sleeps through the bright night. The house takes people by who will keep the table, not by the people they were born as.

### If you meet one

On Kumbaan a table-keeper looks like the person whose hill-hall the valley already knows. The hall a traveller can actually sit in is Ndenjoo, one of the Paps, and the host there is Njunda (NJOON-dah). They are small, pearl-pale, and silver-haired if they are Yumboe, and most are, because that is who lives there, and they open the door on the bright nights. By mainland noon they are often asleep. You would not call them a priest. You would go to their hall when the moon is up because that is where the food is and where a stranger is allowed to sit.

On the mainland they look like one person in a rented room who still sets a place no body will use. A sailor finds them by asking for the Moon-Feast. A clerk finds them by following a rumour and then files them as a guest. They cook, they pour, and on the second night they say the sentence.

They are useful the way a host is useful. They do not water Trees, stamp books, or walk drowning-stones. If you came for those jobs you have the wrong house.

### A bright night on Kumbaan

At dusk the table-keeper opens the hill-hall, often because it was their grandmother's, and often because it was the only roof that still fitted the crowd. Njunda opens Ndenjoo for both of those reasons. People arrive as the moon clears the Paps. The extra place is already set, slightly back and a little apart, and the unseen hands begin to fetch and pour, so that you can see the cups move. There are songs, and there is no reading of a colour and no reciting of terms. A Staying host says the dead's names when the dead fill that place. A Serving host does not say the names, and still fills the place, and calls the hands the hill's own courtesy.

In that hour the table-keeper opens, sets, and greets, keeps the extra place from being used as a spare stool, and sends a child for more bread if the hands have not already gone. If someone is sitting their second night, the host says the sentence. When the moon goes down the table-keeper sleeps. Daylight is not a fallen hour in this house. It is the time they keep for rest.

On one night of the moon sailors call Yoltal, Ndenjoo eats the heel of the loaf first. Every other night the heel is last, or it goes to the goats. The valley says the hands like the crust when the mainland is loud. Njunda will not correct that, and will not say it is the reason. They will not say why. A guest who asks is given more bread.

They do not confirm becoming, and there is no Leaf-Fall on the isle. They do not vouch a Struck person in the mainland sense. Struck people are rare enough here to be a family matter, not a class. They do not licence anyone, they do not graft wood, and they do not file a person onto a roll.

### An ordinary supper

The extra place is not only furniture for the feast. A poor house lays it on a quiet night. To omit it is the one real impiety, not because the people the place is kept for starve, since they do not, but because you have counted the household too small. The hands make this possible. That is not a metaphor the hall uses. It is why a table-keeper can host beyond their means without becoming a charity for Tithes.

A child of the house learns the work by carrying dishes and by being told not to sit in the extra place. That is the apprenticeship. There is no Seat to confirm it.

### An attic on Lestrand

A Yumboe on the mainland is a table-keeper by necessity. At morning, which is their night's end, they may work a quay kitchen, or mend, or copy, and they pay the rent in the local coin. In the evening they lay the extra place in a room that fits four. Someone climbs the stair. On the first night that person is a guest. On the second night the host says the sentence and means it. The quay will not file the guest as a citizen. The host will still set them a place on the third night.

This is how the faith travels, when it travels at all. It does not travel as a mission. There is no Njaalo chapter-house, no collection taken up for one, and no Road-hand of the moon. A second Yumboe in the same city may share a supper and not share a roof, and both of them are of the table. A mainlander who has sat twice and opened their own room is of the table too.

### The second sitting

The first night you are a guest, and a guest is already sacred. The second night you are of the table. On Kumbaan this is how a stranger becomes kin, nearly law, in all but parchment. On the mainland the same supper collides with citizenship by witness, because there you belong when your town watched you Turn, not because you ate twice.

A table-keeper who treats the second supper as belonging is doing their faith honestly. A warden who smiles and files them as a guest is doing theirs. Neither is a villain. The sentence does not cross the storm-wall intact. The host's job, abroad, is to keep the sentence inside the room and not pretend the clerk will honour it.

### What they carry

They carry an extra plate, the songs, and the sentence. On the isle they have a hall, inherited, or simply the one that still fits the crowd. On the mainland they have the rent and whatever the room will hold. They do not carry a stamp, a token from a land-door, a colour-scrap, or Cutting-leave.

The extra place is set slightly back from the board, not at the end of it. A mainlander who puts the plate at the foot, "for a servant," is corrected without a sermon, and the plate is moved.

### What they refuse

They will not read a colour, tend a Tree, or keep a Night Watch, and they will not turn the sentence into a Fair Hand term or into a binding, because the words are eat, and be the household, not sign, and owe. They will not test a guest. The storm-wall already did. They will not send a mission, collect for a hall across the water, or plant a Moon-Feast chapter in Vaethorn, and they will not omit the extra place in order to look poorer or more devout. That omission is the one impiety.

### Who may keep a table

Kind is not the test, and that courtesy is already the faith. A mainlander who sits once is a guest, and a mainlander who sits twice is kin. A human, a Selkie, or a Kitsune who will lay the extra place is of the Njaalo. A Yumboe who will not is not.

Practice comes first. You are a table-keeper when the valley, or the attic stair, comes to you and you set the place. No Seat confirms an apprenticeship. A child who has carried dishes on the bright nights is being watched, and a mainland convert who has sat twice and opened their own room is being watched by whoever they fed.

The argument inside the house does the dishes. A **Staying** host will say the dead's names. A **Serving** host will not, and will call the hands the hill's own courtesy. Both of them set the extra place, and both of them pass the same cup. The argument is for after the meal.

On Kumbaan a Condition barely leans the work at all, because there are no Given Conditions on the isle. Elsewhere, the life that fits the table often keeps it. The table still seats the others.

### How they live in the three corners (and off the map)

The three corners barely reach this house.

**Kumbaan (hill-hosts).** This is home. The Njaalo is simply whoever opened the hall. They keep the moon, not the solstice, and wholeness is the default, the way people already are when nobody is waiting to be chosen. There is no queue, no roll, no book, and no second reading of a person.

**Lestrand (the Moon-Feast people).** On this coast the house is a quay-story, and an attic-host is a curiosity who also sets a good supper. Sailors print "no one is a stranger twice" as a proverb on a card. The Settling will not enforce that card. The attic-host will enforce the sentence inside the room, and cannot enforce it past the door.

**Vaethorn (a sailor's tale).** The faith here runs hot and the reach of the Trees runs thin, so a table-keeper is a wonder if they ever arrive, and a test of how a household that reads the Tree as a guest treats a guest. They will be fed. They will be asked about the Mother. They will not understand why a people need to be chosen in order to be wanted.

**Threnmaieth (off the map).** They are not on the roll. A Yumboe in the sacred centre is a diplomatic incident of manners, not a column the crown can fill. The crown cannot file a people who were never at a Tree. That is a relief and a slight at once.

### The sentence that will not travel

On Kumbaan, the second sitting is how you belong. On the mainland, you belong because a town watched you Turn.

The host who treats the second supper as kin-making is keeping Ndeyaan, and the warden or the clerk who files that same person as a guest is keeping a different grammar of belonging. The sharper clock is Turning-Week. A child of the table is in it, the host will not walk them to a Tree, a neighbour will, and a door shuts on the last day of Eolthael.

Treating the Moon-Feast as a romance is itself a slight. They have no queue of scions and no mission, and an attic-host is one person at an ordinary supper, with no authority outside the room. That is enough.

### Economy of the house

On Kumbaan the hall is the living. A table-keeper is fed by the same feast they host, and the unseen hands are what make a poor house's extra place possible. There is no stipend, no Cutting-leave, and no share of a pilgrimage.

An attic pays its rent in the local coin. A Yumboe abroad works as anyone works. They are not a charity-order, and they are not part of the halls and greens that furnish a Tithe, so they may cook for a quay and still will not furnish a venting-hall.

There is no collection for a hall across the water. That collection would be a mission, and there isn't one.

### A place-card

This is a place-card, badly copied by a Strandor sailor who did come back, and sold on a Lestrand quay as a dinner-grace. The back is the sailor's own hand: "for luck at second sitting."

> *We have counted you. Sit. If you have a name we have not learned it yet; if you have a colour we have no tree to read it. The moon is enough light. Eat, and be the household. — and if you sit again, you are not a stranger. This is not a proverb. Do not sell it as one.*

The card is a hospitality blessing, and the quay's copy has already half-ruined it. The sailor thought it was a grace. On the isle the same words are how you belong, a legal sentence in all but parchment. The last two lines were added by the table-keeper when she saw him copy the blessing, and he sold them anyway. Lestrand files the Njaalo as a Moon-Feast story, lucky if you eat twice. The woman in the attic still sets the place.

## The Shore-Sitters

The Shore-Sitters are a second house on Kumbaan, small, and not an order of the mainland. The Table-Keepers are the ones who open the hall. The Shore-Sitters sit the storm-beaches. Almost no one reaches the isle, and someone has to be there on the sand when someone does. They share the faith of the Open Table, and the beach is their part of it.

People call them **shore-sitters**, and also **wall-waiters**. Their own name is **Njawaal** (njah-WAHL), a Yumboe coinage, "the sitting at the wall." It is not a Maiethren word. Table-keepers call them **the first night**, and the joke is half a joke: a wreck is not yet kin.

They sit the storm-shore with their back to the hill-hall and wait. They do not swim the wall, they do not test anyone with a colour, and if someone is still breathing they walk them uphill to a table.

### If you meet one

You meet a small person on a bad beach, with the lamp hooded so it does not call ships in. They look as if they have been there a long time, and they will not wade. They will take a living mainlander by the sleeve and start the path up. If the house leans Staying, they will name a drowned body, and if it leans Serving, they will not name the body, and either way they still carry it above the tide. Below Ndenjoo, the person the valley knows on that sand is Soonke, who decides whether a wreck is alive to be walked up. Njunda, at the hall, decides whether that person is kin.

You would not call them a priest. You would find them if you asked who sits the black sand when the wall is loud.

### What they actually do

On the nights that matter, which are storm nights, wreck-moons, and the weeks after a rumour of a crossing, they sit. If a stretch of coast is kept at all, one person keeps it, and the claim is reputation rather than a roster.

A living person gets water and a blanket, and nobody asks what Kind they are or what colour they carry. A dead person is carried above the tide. Cargo is whatever the hall can use. They do not loot it for a market, because there is no quay to sell it from.

The living are walked uphill to a table-keeper. The first night, the night a newcomer is only a guest, starts at the hall and not on the sand. The shore-sitter is the reason there was a first night at all.

They will not swim the wall, and they do not keep a boat for rescue, because the wall is not a door they walk. A mainlander who asks why no one came sooner is told that they do not call the sea, and that they wait.

They do not keep the extra place. That plate is the hall's work. They do not Speak a colour, stamp a book, or walk the old doors of the mainland, and they do not send anyone back across the wall.

### Who comes

Almost no one comes, and they treat that emptiness as part of the faith rather than as a failure of the watch. A shore-sitter's year may pass with an empty beach, and the job is still to sit. When someone does come, the whole valley hears it as news, not as a line on a shipping schedule.

### How they sit with the table-keepers

They keep the same faith and do two different jobs, so the shore is where someone decides whether the person is alive, and the hall is where someone decides whether the person is kin. A shore-sitter who starts laying the extra place on the sand has started doing the table-keeper's work, and a table-keeper who walks the black sand every storm has started doing this one. Both of those crossings can happen in a thin year, when there are not enough people to keep the jobs apart. Most years the two jobs are still held by different people.

### Said to a sailor who did come back

This was said to a sailor who did come back, the night before he was walked uphill. Someone copied it later as a quay-story, and copied it badly.

> *We did not fetch you. The wall does not owe you a hand. Sit until you can walk. If you walk, there is a hall. If you do not, we will still take you off the sand. Do not thank the sea. It was not kind. We were only here.*

The sitter sounds cold, and the message is plain. They did not fetch the sailor, the wall owes nobody a hand, and what he got was a wait rather than a rescue. They do not say the crossing was a gift, and they do not say it was a pilgrimage the Mother arranged.

## The Slide

The Slide is the illegal supply of what a Tithe needs. A Tithe is the upkeep a person owes for what they have become, and it has to be met or the failure shows in public. The Slide is not a fifth faith, not clergy, and not a copy of the Book-Hands. They do not help a person pay a Tithe. They step in when a person starts to fail, and they make the failing last.

Everyday speech calls them **the Slide**, and also **week-keepers**. In the cant of the trade they are **the Vaethledd** (VAETH-ledd), the waiting, reckoned. The Waiting Lands say **the last pot**, and when the commons have run out a pious neighbour may still say *gift-credit*. The Ledger Coast says **week-rent**. The Tallied Crown files nothing, because the roll already has a counted hall. A clerk who knows will say **the late week**.

What they rent is not falling, sold as one week of green, or one outlet, or one new page, and they are never in a hurry to let you get free.

### Shape of the house

This is a practice, not a gang. There is no seat, no colour, no roster, and no liturgy in a cellar. You are of the Slide when someone comes back next week, and that return is the whole of the reputation. When a keeper dies, the book of weeks goes to whoever still has a pot, a room, or a key.

They have one job. They furnish just enough of what the Tithe needs that the person does not become a public hazard, and never enough that the person recovers. The product is the week, and the price of that week is remaining theirs.

They think they are in a rent story, so they do not call themselves monsters. They extend, they keep a book, and they will tell you they kept someone whole. That is what makes them neighbours: a kind face, a real key, and a measured withhold later.

They are not clergy, and they are not the official doors. They do not stamp books, Speak colours, walk old doors, or open a hill-hall. They do not run the town greenhouse, the counted ael-hall, or a licence-guild. Those doors are the ones that make a person wait. On ordinary days the guilds pretend not to know these people. When an official gate closes on purpose, the pretence is over, because the guild and the Slide are then serving the same street.

### If you meet one

They look like a landlord, or like a cousin who still has a pot after the commons ran dry, or like a junior clerk at a deathless house, a long-house kept by the Long-Lived, who will extend a novelty subscription one more week. They may be a person with a spare room and a voice who will let you cry there, for a price that is never quite coin.

You would not call them a priest. You would find them if you asked who still has green when the leaf-lot is closed. A leaf-lot is a strip of living green let by the season, and when it is shut, someone still has a pot.

They are useful the way a week is useful. If you came for a stamp, a colour, or a false childhood, you have the wrong house.

### Who comes, and when

The people who come are already late. A Taken-In person needs living green, and without it they wilt. A Far-Voiced person needs somewhere for feeling to go, and without it a Cry, that feeling breaking loose, breaks into the street. A Long-Lived person needs something new, and without it they fade. The other needs are warmth, civic coin, a purpose that does not pay, or a family trying to keep the square from seeing any of this. The week-keeper rents the missing piece and stops short of a cure.

| Who | Why they come | What the week-keeper does | What they will not do |
|---|---|---|---|
| A Taken-In person cut off from land | The queue for a green is longer than the wilt. The people in that queue were Given at a city Tree and never left, or they are harvest-hands who wintered, or they work the lot and cannot save the cart-fare home. | They rent a pot, a strip, or a night on living dirt, and they extend if the week cannot be paid. | They will not give a share of a commons that lasts, and they will not walk anyone to a warden to be filed as housed. |
| A Far-Voiced person who lost a stage | The posting is isolated, the labor is dull, there is nowhere to vent, and the Cry is close. | They give a room, an hour, and a door that shuts: one outlet, and the next week is the same week. | They will not find honest work. They will not let the Cry happen in the street, because that brings the watch and ruins the book. |
| A Long-Lived person whose novelty has thinned | The endowment is late, and the fade has started. | They ration a page, a letter, or a gossip that is new enough. | They will not endow a library, and they will not be that person's deathless patron in public. |
| An Unbound person losing warmth | Company costs money, and the lack of anyone who knows them is getting hard to hide. | They sit with them, let themselves be known a little, and charge for the company. | They will not be family. They will not sign a new Bargain, the contract an Unbound person lives by, because that is another table. |
| A Bound person behind on civic coin | The debt is rent, a guild fee, or a year that will not close. | They hold the debt, extend it, and use the hold. | They will not rewrite Terms. The hold is money, not a clause, and they will not sell a cellar-easing. |
| A Returned person whose purpose pays nothing | The work the dead want done is not a trade that pays. | They give a week of keep so the person can keep walking. | They will not furnish the purpose. They will not send anyone to a plague-ward unless the ward pays them. |
| A family, before the town sees | A child or a parent is sliding, and the family will pay so the square does not have to watch it. | They take the week, take the family's coin, and keep the person quiet. | They will not tell the warden, and they will not make the slide a sermon. |

### An ordinary week

They extend first. That opening is how a person gets onto the book, and it is also what the week-keeper believes about themselves. A person arrives already late. The week-keeper does not withhold on the first night. They open the lot, the room, or the page, they write *extended*, and they name a day.

The withhold comes later, and it is measured. It is never enough to make a public hazard, a wilt in the square, a Cry in the market, or a fade a neighbour can name, because that brings the watch, the guild, the counted hall, and the book dies. It is never enough to let the person stand up and walk to a commons that would keep them. The asset is a person who is always almost whole.

They keep a book of weeks, not a ledger of coin. Coin is how some weeks are paid. What they are counting is how long you have not fallen, on their floor.

A child of the house learns the work by carrying water to the pots and by being told not to let a guest sleep a second week for nothing. There is no Seat to confirm it.

### What they carry

They carry a key, a pot or a spare room, a stack of pages that are new enough, and a book of weeks. They carry no stamp, no colour-scrap, no Cutting-leave, and no false witness to sell in the square.

### What they refuse

They will not pretend to be clergy. That means no colour, no book-stamp, no door-walk, and no extra place at a table that is not theirs. They will not rewrite a Bound person's Terms. They may hold a Bound person's rent, and that hold is money. They will not steal a scion, and they will not sell the sentence "I watched you Turn." Those belong to other trades.

They prefer not to push someone over the edge that ruins the week. A corpse, a public Cry, or a wilt the town can see is a ruined week and a ruined name. Nothing stops a keeper who is losing the book from withholding too far, and a measured week can miss.

They will not run a mission, a cellar-church, or an export to Kumbaan. There is almost no Tithe on the isle to own, so there is almost nothing there for a week-keeper to rent. They will not call the week a gift, and they will not call it a cruelty. They call it a week.

### Who may keep a week

Kind does not decide this house. A human, a Kitsune, a Selkie, or a Tengu who has a pot and a book can be of the Slide. A Yumboe abroad who has started charging for the extra place has committed a different wrong, the one the Table-Keepers argue, and that wrong is not the Slide's.

Practice comes first. You are of the Slide when someone comes back next week and you still have the key. There is no apprenticeship for anyone to confirm. A cousin who extended once and then let the person recover was kind. They are not of this house.

A Condition leans a keeper toward the week they already know how to furnish. It does not bar anyone else.

| Act | Often | Why | Also, and welcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| A pot, a strip, a night of dirt | a Taken-In person who kept a private lot | They already furnish green. | A Kept person, who carries no Tithe of their own, with a window-box. |
| A shut room, an hour of voice | a Far-Voiced person with a spare hall | They already know what an outlet costs. | A performer who lost the honest stage and kept the door. |
| A page that is new enough | a Long-Lived clerk, a junior at a deathless house | Novelty is already their weather. | Anyone who can still find a rumour the fade has not heard. |
| The book of weeks itself | whoever can read a late face | The house is the count, not the crop. | A Kept broker, unencumbered, who can move and can carry the book to the next town. |

The feared and still licensed are not barred. A Stilled person who rents hours of stillness is rare and tightly watched. A Bound person who holds civic debt is dealing in money, not in a Counterparty's Terms.

### How they live with the other houses

| House | Everyday arrangement | The hard edge |
|---|---|---|
| the Tree-Wardens | A warden furnishes a Tree, not a week. Contact is rare, and it is not a ban. | A Road-hand who starts a week-cart has left the wood. A scion is not stolen to pay a Tithe. |

| the Door-Keepers | A Struck person named kin at a door still has a Tithe. Kinship is not green. | A drowning-stone is not rented out as a lot. |
| the Table-Keepers | No contact that matters. The second sitting is not a week. | Charging for the extra place is a different faith's one impiety. |
| Official halls and greens | The official hall, the counted green, and the licence are the town's posted doors, and the town is glad someone takes the late ones. | The pretence ends when the official gate closes on purpose. The worst case is a public wilt or Cry that the gate caused. |

### How they live in the three corners (and off the map)

Vaethorn, which calls them the last pot, is home weather for the pious version. The commons ran out, and a cousin still has a pot. They will say they are tending a gift. The quiet obligation is the week: you are helped, and you are theirs. Public help in the Waiting Lands already shades into service you cannot refuse, and this private week is that same shade.

Lestrand, which calls the charge week-rent, treats the house as ordinary, posted, almost. A leaf-lot by the season is already the honest market. The Slide is the lot that will still open after the season-fee failed, at a price that is the next season, and the person who opens it is a landlord, not a preacher.

Threnmaieth calls it the late week, because the crown already holds the Tithe and the Slide does not compete with the counted hall on ordinary days. They sell the week the hall is late, the extra green the roll does not cover, and the novelty the archive will not release. They look like a clerk. The crown's count is cold and precise, and this house is the leak that count cannot file without admitting the channel failed. The roll pretends not to know.

On Kumbaan the Slide has no work, because the isle has no Tree and almost nobody on it carries a Given Tithe. A wreck walked uphill is a guest, not a debtor.

### The week that will not end

Whoever furnishes a Tithe that someone else depends on holds power over the person who bears it. The Slide is that power with a landlord's face, and with a patience the official hall is not allowed.

The town is glad of the arrangement. A person who is quietly almost falling is not a Cry in the market. The Waiting Lands can say the gift is still being tended. Threnmaieth can say the channel held. The quay can say the owing was marked and someone took them in.

The Motherfaith says she gave the need. The book of weeks is people's work, and nobody in a town expects the Tree to make the week fair.

The house has no queue of scions, no refinancing of a Bound person's clauses, and no authority except the one the Tithe gives its keepers, which is one key and a measured week. Adding a Term to a Bound person's contract is the Holding Desk. Selling a person who will swear they watched a Turning is the Standing Trade. They are not this house.

### Economy of the house

The week is the living. It is paid in coin, in labor, in a favor, or in a child sent to carry water, and the book counts weeks first.

They are not a charity-order, and they are not the official furnishing. A town greenhouse, a counted ael-hall, and an endowed long-house of books are the doors a town posts. A week-keeper may sit next to all three and take the overflow.

A junior at a long-house who rations novelty is how a fortune that never dies feels on the street: not a loan of a century, a page at a time. The house does not run the bank.

They do not collect for a hall across the water. There isn't one.

### A week-slip

This is a week-slip from a Lestrand leaf-lot, pinned inside a door that still opens after the season-fee has failed. The back is the same slip, last week, the date scratched once.

> *One week of the lot. Extended. Do not let the pot dry. If you cannot come, send a child. The week is the same week. We do not call this a gift. We do not call it a debt. Come before the leaves turn. If you have already turned, come anyway.*

The slip is a landlord's, practical and almost kind. The product is not falling, and the slip never calls the week a debt, which is how the book stays a book of weeks and never closes. "If you have already turned, come anyway" is the house's whole case: becoming did not save anyone from needing them. The child who carries water is learning the trade.

## The Holding Desk

When the honest stamp has already closed and a person still has clauses to sign, the everyday name for the people who will write the next one is **the Holding Desk**, and the people themselves are **hold-clerks**. They keep a back room under an honest table, and the oldest chair is on Orentel's lower slip.

A Bound person signed a contract and is still living under its Terms, the clauses that outlast a single year. The civic year is the honest account of that year. A Book-hand can close it upstairs. The desk opens when the year is payable and the Terms are not. On Lestrand people say the Holding Desk the way they say extension, as if a second term were ordinary business. In Vaethorn a preacher has no clean word for it and calls it **the second signing** when they want a sin. Threnmaieth files nothing, because a private Term that is not the Threnhael is already a problem, and this desk is that problem with a chair.

They are not the Slide. They do not rent a week of green, an hour, or a page. The desk works in clauses.

You would know them by the ink, and by a stool that does not face the quay. They look like a junior Settler who lost the front room. You would find them if you asked who still writes after the book is closed.

**Mutelo of the Lower Slip** (moo-TEL-o) keeps the oldest chair on Orentel and speaks the worn-down speech of the quay. They will tell you they kept a person walking. The book on the shelf is labelled *extensions*. The last page is not.

A Bound person finishes a civic year at an honest table, and the year is payable while the Terms are not. Fear of the Pledge, the payment the contract still holds over them, is what starts the next ask. The hold-clerk does not invent that fear. They name a smaller Term that covers the larger one for a season, and they call that an extension.

That is how the desk works. Each signing eats more of the person, until the last signing, which is the Given-Over, the person the guilds call the Forfeit. The law has never decided when the person on the stool stopped being one.

| Who comes | What the desk does | The limit |
|---|---|---|
| A Bound person behind on a Pledge | They write a covering Term, name a day, and extend. | They will not recite the old Terms as if they were gone. The honest table still has them. |
| A family who will pay to keep a sibling "working" | They hold the person as an asset that still signs. | They will not call this kinship, and they will not take them to a door-keeper to be named kin. |
| A creditor who wants a walkable instrument | They match a Counterparty who will accept the next signing. | They will not stamp a civic year. That is a Book-hand. |
| A Struck Bound person, raw from the signing that made them | Sometimes, if the ask is already live. | They will not sit the Intake, the shed that takes a newly Struck person in hand before the town reaches for a rope. That is the wrong shed. The Whisper, the thought a Bound person hears when they fall behind on a Term, suggests and never commands, and this is still a table. |

They extend first. That is what they believe about themselves. The withhold is the next clause, measured, never quite the last one until it is.

They will not rent a Tithe-week, whether that week is dirt, a Cry, or a page. They will not sell "I watched you Turn." That trade belongs to the Standing Trade, which sells a witness rather than a clause. They will not steal a scion, and they will not Speak a colour, which is a warden's naming and not a clerk's. They will not rewrite a Term the Counterparty has already named. They add a Term, and that distinction is how they sleep. They will not work a wreck on Kumbaan, because there is no Bound table on the sand.

A dead Bound person is a ruined book, and the desk does not want one. They still walk people toward the Forfeit.

A party meets the desk in the ordinary way the desk works. A Bound friend is being refinanced, which is the word they use for the next covering Term. A Book-hand's *owing* walks downstairs and does not come back up. Someone is asked to witness a signing that is "only a season." A Given-Over case has no settled law, and it needs a witness who is not the creditor's. Mutelo will hire runners, and they pay in extensions.

| House | Everyday | The fight |
|---|---|---|
| the Book-Hands | They work upstairs and downstairs of each other. A Settler who marked *owing* has made a customer. | It is the same Bound person in the same week, and the clerk who follows them down has started a case. |
| the Slide | The product is different, and a Bound person may be on both books. | Money is not a clause. A week-keeper who starts writing Terms has changed houses. |
| the Standing Trade | A Forfeit who needs to cross a border may buy a witness. | A false childhood will not pay a Pledge. |
| the Tree-Wardens | There is almost no contact. A quiet receiving-rite does not close a Term. | Wardens carry grafts, and they do not carry Terms. |
| the Reckoned Offices | A private Term that Threnmaieth cannot file is a leak in the crown's count. | Menirein wants the name on a roll. The desk wants the name off any roll that is not theirs. |

Lestrand is home weather for the desk, the coast where an extension already sounds like business. Vaethorn calls it a sin and still uses it when a Bound person would otherwise become a story that ends in a rope. Threnmaieth cannot file the desk without admitting the channel failed, so the hold-clerk is left looking like any other clerk.

This is an extension-slip from Orentel's lower slip, pinned under a stool. The back is last season's slip, the date scratched once.

> *Covering Term, one season. The upstairs year is closed. This is not that year. Come before the Pledge-day. If you cannot come, send no one — a clause is not a child. We do not call this a gift. We do not call it a sale. If you have already signed, come anyway. The next Term is the same Term until it is not.*

The writer sounds like a clerk being practical. What the desk sells is not paying yet, and the slip never uses the word *Forfeit*. "If you have already signed, come anyway" is the house's whole case: becoming Bound did not save anyone from needing them.

## The Standing Trade

Citizenship in this world is witnessed, not recorded. What travels ahead of you, when the town that watched you is far behind, is a witness: a person who will say they watched you Turn, which is the standing under a Tree where a child becomes what they become, or where a later threshold takes someone. The unvouched, people no living circle will stand for, will buy one.

Everyday speech calls the sale **the Standing Trade**. A buyer says **a standing**. A seller says "I was there," and means they will be, later, in a square that was not theirs. Lestrand prices it. Vaethorn calls it **false kin** when a Hands-folk hearth wants a sin. Threnmaieth does not need it on ordinary days, because the roll already stands, and wants it badly when a name is missing from that roll.

They are not the Slide, and they do not rent a week of green or an hour or a page. They are not the Holding Desk, and they do not write a Term. The product is a person who will stand and say they watched.

They look like a useful cousin at a ford, or a factor's runner, or a pilgrim who happened to be in the square that Eolthael. You would miss them if you came looking for a forger's press, because there is no press, only a person who will say they were there.

**Nomele of the Hinge** (no-MEL-eh) works the Old Crossing face, the Hinge Shore more often than Orentel, because the Old Crossing has two shores and a standing has to travel between them. Nomele speaks the worn-down speech of the quay. They do not sell paper. They sell who will walk with you to the next Tree.

A person who Changed unwitnessed, or who left the town that watched them, or who was never there at ten, the year a child stands to be Turned, needs someone to stand. Three kinds of person already do this honestly. A Long-Lived person carries the memory. A Bound person binds the word so it holds. A Far-Voiced person carries a feeling that will not fake. The trade sells a corrupt version of the same three.

| Kind of witness | What the buyer gets | Why it is catastrophic when it fails |
|---|---|---|
| A Long-Lived person who will remember you | A childhood that will not fade from the witness. | A Blood-Read, the reading that pulls a true fragment of memory from a drop of blood, will give that fragment. If the fragment is the sale, the standing dies, and so does every standing that witness has sold. |
| A Bound person who will bind the word | A sentence that holds like a Term, as if it were a clause of a contract. | The Counterparty, the other side of that Bound person's own contract, did not ask for this. A bound lie is still a lie the town can use, until the next signing eats the witness. |
| A Far-Voiced person who will make the feeling stick | The square feels that this person belongs. | A Cry in the same square can splash the false feeling onto everyone in earshot. The watch then has a riot and a fraud. |

Honest vouching is priceless because it cannot be faked cheaply. That is why the corrupt one costs a year, a child sent to carry water, a hold on a Tithe, or a name on someone else's book.

They will not sell a colour, the leaf a Tree gives a child, and they will not Speak one, which is a warden's naming. They will not write you onto the Threnhael. That is a registrar's work, and Menirein would rather list you than buy you.

They will not stamp a civic year, because the Book-Hands do that with a real table. They will not rewrite Terms, and they will not walk a Bound person toward the Forfeit, because that walk belongs to the Holding Desk. They will not rent green or an hour, because that rent is the Slide, and they will not steal a scion. They will not stand on Kumbaan, because the isle does not vouch by Tree.

A recant ruins the book. They still sell standings that will recant if the price is higher on the other side.

The customers are the people the law already called rootless. People who cannot point at a town that watched them are the trade's ordinary hour. Nomele will offer a standing the way a ferryman offers a boat. They can pay, refuse, or become the witness. A town that already knows the child will smell the sale. A neighbour who was there still stands for free.

|---|---|---|
| the Slide | The product is different. A week-keeper may send a customer who also needs to move. | The Slide does not sell a false Turning, and does not put one in its book of weeks. |
| the Holding Desk | A Forfeit who must cross a border may buy a witness. | The two houses keep two books, and they charge two prices. |
| the Book-Hands | Civic settling sits beside honest vouching. | A Settler who finds a standing they did not stamp has a case against it. |
| the Door-Keepers | A kin-name is a courtesy spoken at a door, not one of the three pillars. | A Kin-speaker who will stand for a fee has left the door-keepers' way. |
| the Watchers | A second reading is a description, not a childhood. | A false watch is a description of a fall the seller did not see. The Watchers treat that as this trade reaching into their room. |
| the Reckoned Offices | The roll wants a name. A bought witness is a second account the crown does not hold. | Sirtal will withhold a channel, the crown's furnished upkeep, from a name that will not sit still. Nomele will sell the steadiness. |

This is a fee-slate from the Old Crossing hinge, with no childhood written on it. The back is a list of squares, not names.

> *I will stand at the next Tree that asks. I will not say which summer. I will not say which child. I will say I was there. If you have a Long-Lived who was actually there, do not hire me — I will not win that room. If you have no one, come before the watch sets the question. We do not sell a colour. We do not sell a hug. We sell a witness. If the witness recants, the fee does not.*

The writer sounds like a ferryman. "I will not say which child" is the whole fraud: an honest witness names the child, and this one names only the square. The sentences around that fraud are plain about the product. The slate will not sell a colour or a hug, the two things a Tree does when a child stands, because what it sells is a witness.

## The Reckoned Offices

People call these four jobs **the Reckoned Offices**, or simply **the tablet offices**. They are not a church, and they are not a sixteenth power, because the fifteen great powers are already named and these jobs sit inside one of them. They are four jobs the Tallied Crown already had. What a traveller can do now is go to the tablet-hall at Maiethlir and find them by name. The grove and the First Seat are other buildings.

Maiethlir still has **Rithim** (RITH-im) as town Speaker, the person who Speaks at the town's Tree and names the colour that fell, or the hug. These offices speak for the Threnhael. They are not a replacement for the hearth, the neighbours who stood and watched the child. The gap between the Speaking and the copy is where a traveller can stand.

They recruit by the office and by the practice of the work, not by Kind. Long-Lived memory is common at the tablet. A Long-Lived person does not age and does not forget what they have witnessed, so here that memory is bent to the census rather than left free to check it. That bending is already how this corner of the Crown works.

The four of them do not share a single want. Each office has its own good-faith aim, and the harm comes out of that aim.

**Menirein** (men-EE-rayn) keeps the Threnhael's local book, and what they want from it is a roll that is complete. An unlisted child is, to them, a child the Mother cannot be thanked for, and a child the crown cannot keep hale. They will knock on a door, including the door of people who have just arrived, because they believe they are doing care. They do not Speak the fall themselves. Once they have heard it, they copy it, and if Rithim will not let the copy happen in the same breath as the Speaking, Menirein will wait in the square until the water quiets, and then write what they heard. Their good-faith aim is no one unmarked, and the harm comes from that same wish.

**Tarvae** (TAR-vay) is the crown's preferred Speaker when a fall is mixed or argumentative, a Leaf-Fall where the colour can be read two ways or the square is already arguing. They want the Speaking and the filing to stay one act. A colour Spoken and not written is, to them, a seeing that can be stolen. They will stand beside the town-warden, and they will not always wait for Rithim to finish a breath. They are still a Speaker, and they still name the fall truly when they can. The roll is the second seeing, the lesser one, and they refuse to call it lesser. Their aim is one name, spoken and filed in one breath, and the fight with Rithim is the one that is live. Tarvae does not lie about colours. The dispute is about when the copy is allowed.

**Videm** (VEE-dem) runs the counted hall and keeps the slate that matches the tended green. They want the channel to hold, so that the late week never comes. The channel is the share the roll can open or shut: green, a counted hour, a novelty the archive keeps, a stiller's bed. A stiller is a person whose gaze can hold a wound or a patient still, and the bed is where that work is done. A Far-Voiced person cut off from the counted hour is one leak, because without the hour the feeling breaks into a Cry that everyone nearby has to hear. A Taken-In person wilted off the lot is another leak, and a novelty the archive will not release is a third leak of the same kind. Videm will work late to stop a leak, and will also mark a name *late* so the leak can be blamed on the person who carries it. Their aim is no Cry in the market and no wilt in the street, and the injustice is that staying whole runs through them. They are not a separate faction of the hall. The hall is the civic greens and halls that keep people whole enough, held on a crown leash. Videm is the hand on that leash.

**Sirtal** (SEER-tal) writes the warrant that withholds the share. The warrant can close the green, the counted hour, a novelty, or a stiller's bed. Sirtal wants the roll and the channel to match before that share opens. A name that will not sit still does not get it, whether the name was bought from someone paid to claim they watched, is missing from the book, belongs to the Closed of the Inner Close, or is unvouched, with nobody who will stand and say they were there. Sirtal believes a share given to an unlisted person is how the realm sickens. Their aim is that the channel opens only for a name the offices already have, and the injustice is that same withhold dressed up as care. They will sit a gate to keep the share shut, and they will sit the gate a traveller is trying to use in the same way.

They will not capture the First Seat. Nearness is the pressure they use, and the college of the tree-wardens stays in the wood. They will not copy the Book of Tithes without a fight, and the lists stay two. The Walled Book ranks people by the Tithe they carry, while these offices count people and mean the count as care. They will not stamp a Bound person's Terms. Those are another table's paper. They will not sell a standing. The Standing Trade is a leak they want written on the roll, and it is not a service they will hire. They will not export a Threnhael to Kumbaan.

You can meet them one at a time, because they do not move as a squad. Menirein wants you listed on the roll, Tarvae wants your colour copied in the breath it was Spoken, Videm wants you on the slate that opens the channel, and Sirtal wants you kept still until the first three have you. When they do move together, someone is about to lose a channel.

| House | Everyday | The fight |
|---|---|---|
| Rithim, at Maiethlir | Rithim is the town Speaker. Some years the copy happens in the same breath as the Speaking, and some years it does not. | Tarvae stands in the square the summer Rithim will not copy. |
| the Watchers | They keep the second column, where counted dissent is written down. | A second reading that contradicts the name Tarvae already filed. |
| the Walled Book | Two lists, kept a country apart. | Clerks of the roll come to the Inner Close gate, and the two books stay separate. |
| the counted halls and greens | The counted hall and the tended green are the channel. | Videm runs late on purpose against a lot that still has beds, and there is a wilt the channel will not walk to. |
| the Slide | The late week is the week the channel will not admit. | The channel can no longer pretend it does not know the Slide. Videm knows that, and Sirtal files the leak as the person's own lapse. |
| the Holding Desk | A private Term is a second seeing, a contract the roll did not write. | Menirein wants the name on this roll, and the desk wants the name off it. |
| Maiethvael | The same hymns, sung over the opposite paper. Maiethvael is the gift-country on Maiethorn, and it will not keep a roll of persons. | A registrar sending clerks into that country, where the list is refused. |

These are four docket-marks from the same Eolthael, written in the tablet-hall by four hands.

> *Menirein: the child is unmarked. I will wait in the square.*
> *Tarvae: the fall was storm-grey. File it in the breath. A delayed copy is a stolen seeing.*
> *Videm: the counted hour matches the name. If the name is late, the hour is late. The hall held.*
> *Sirtal: no green until the name sits. A share to an unlisted bed is how a channel fails.*
> *Rithim (verso, not a Reckoned mark): I Spoke. I did not copy. The water was loud. The Tree was enough.*

The four marks are competent, and they are four different cares that cannot all be met in the same hour. Rithim's note on the back is the gap the offices exist to close.

## The Pourers

People call them **the Pourers**, or **the Cup Circle**. Neighbours call the practice **the five-year cup**, because five years is what the circle asks of a member in all, and they smile when they say it, the way people smile about a rich uncle's hobby. The Motherfaith is content to call the whole thing a private devotion. The Fair Hand will not put a stamp on it, because a stamp needs someone on the other side of the deal and the Hand cannot see anyone there. The Watching counts it as one more cost of a long life and says it has nothing to do with the Mother. The Old Ways do not treat the cup as a sacred place or as a door, and the Open Table has no use for it.

The Pourers say they made a god, and they call it the Poured God. That claim is the centre of the practice. The years go into the cup, and the god, as they tell it, is the presence they made on the other side of the gift.

None of this is a Turning. No leaf falls, no warden stands up to Speak a colour, and the practice is not one of the five lived faiths.

### If you meet one

You meet a Long-Lived person with a small, ordinary cup. They have given some of their years of memory away, one year at a time, up to five, and they are missing those years, though they are still recognizably the same person. They will tell you they invented the god on the other side of the cup, and they will describe how the circle pictures it this decade, as a librarian, a quiet guest, or a child who never left home. The picture they use may change from one decade to the next, and the rule does not. Members give years, the cup keeps what they gave, and the cup cannot Give a Condition or save anyone from death.

**Molevad** (mo-LEV-ad) keeps the minutes of the best-known circle, on a long-house shelf in Lestrand. Molevad comes from an old quay that the sea has worn down, is the oldest member in the room, and keeps both the book and the key to the shelf. Molevad is not a priest, and is not a Hand.

A second, quieter circle meets in Orenbren, in an upper room of a lodging-house that once slept travellers. It keeps the same practice. It does not ask Molevad's circle for permission, and the two circles do not write to each other.

### The ask

Every Long-Lived person is asked once to join the Pourers, and the asking is done in person by a member. At the long-house shelf in Lestrand, a member of Molevad's circle asks. In the upper room in Orenbren, a member of that circle asks. A Long-Lived person who lives in another town is still asked once, by a member who can reach them, and a person who has already been asked says so. That answer stands.

The invitation is a real one. The member asks because the circle will accept the person who says yes, and a yes is what makes them a member. From then on the member gives one year into the cup at a time, and five years is what the circle asks of a member in all.

A refusal is not punished. The person keeps their standing, people still consult them, and the work a court, a guild, or an archive already gave them stays theirs. A long-house will still seat a person who refused the invitation. Most Long-Lived people are not members, and every Long-Lived person still knows that the circle exists.

### The gift

A member gives one year into the cup. Each year is its own night, with its own minutes, and the member may stop before the next one. Five years is what the circle asks of a member in all. Members say they give "the memories that year held, and the year itself." For each year given, that means three things, and they do not all go into the cup.

The memory goes into the cup. The member puts one drop of their own blood in the cup, because a Long-Lived person already reads memory from blood, and afterward the member no longer has the memories of that year. The member still needs to keep learning, as every Long-Lived person does. What changes is that they no longer carry that particular year of other people's funerals.

The papers go onto the shelf. The member also gives the long-house shelf everything they wrote or kept in that year, the letters, the account books, the diaries, and the court notes. The papers are not put in the cup. They are filed on the shelf under the member's name, where other members can read them, and where a visitor Molevad allows can read them too. That filing is one reason the shelf in Lestrand holds so much. Letters name other people, and those people were not asked before their letters went onto the shelf.

The circle counts the member one year younger. At a long-house that keeps the circle, a Long-Lived person's age and standing are counted only in the years they still remember, so for each year given the circle counts the member one year younger. A member who has given three years is seated as if they were three years younger. Among the Long-Lived, age decides who speaks first, who judges a hard case, and who keeps a key, which means every year given costs a member rank inside those long-houses. Courts, guilds, banks, and Long-Lived people who are not members keep counting the ordinary way. The same person can be a bank's most senior clerk and still sit below a younger member at the long-house.

Members can read the cup the way a Long-Lived person reads a drop of blood. What they read is true, and it is also mixed, so one reading can hold memories of the same year from more than one member. The circle values that mix. They say the knowledge goes to the god they made. In practice the cup is where the memories are kept, and the circle is the group that reads them.

There are no priests. The oldest member writes down who gave which year, how the circle described the god that decade, and whether anyone asked to stop. One member has kept that book for so long that the circle cannot imagine meeting without them.

| Who | Why they give | What the circle will not do |
|---|---|---|
| A Long-Lived person who has buried too many people | The ordinary Tithe is the need to keep learning, and the heavier cost is outliving everyone they love. | It will not make anyone Long-Lived, because they already are. |
| A younger member who wants the shared store | They want to read memories of years they did not live. | It will not take years from someone who is not Long-Lived. The cup is only for the deathless. |
| A member who wants out | The next year is coming due. | It will not lock the door. It will make leaving cost them their friends and their seat. |
| A neighbour who wants a god | They come from curiosity, from pity, or for a good story. | It will not set the cup up in a public square, and it will not ask a warden to Speak it. |

### Leaving

A member may stop before the next year, or leave the circle. The years already given stay in the cup. The circle gives back the papers from those years and does not give back the memory, and members call the papers a **thinner copy** of what was given. The real cost is social. The circle treats the person as someone who weakened the god, so other Pourers will not read blood with them, and Long-Lived houses that pay for the long-house will not give them a seat. They are free to go, and they will be alone among the other people who do not age.

**Reimaethe** (RYE-may-theh) is one of them, in a rise-loft in Orentel. They have given four years, one at a time, and the fifth is still ahead of them. They want to stop before it. They want their papers back, thin as that copy is, because the papers are all the circle returns. Neighbours still call the loft a hobby. The long-house calls it a person they will not seat.

The members who have asked to stop are called the withdrawers. They have each given one or more years. They do not all want to stop for the same reason. They are not people who turned down the invitation, and a Long-Lived person who never joined is still received everywhere. They are not a second circle, they do not pour anyone else's years, they do not stand the cup in a square, and they do not say Mother over it. When a circle meets, a withdrawer stands in the passage and does not go in. They will not say why they stand there instead of going home.

### The work

The members keep a library on the long-house shelf in Lestrand, and that shelf is the deathless library on the Ledger Coast. They copy the Old Tongue, and they copy the common speech people already use. The copies stay on the shelf, beside the papers members have given with their years, and Molevad keeps the minute-book on the same shelf. The circle in the upper room in Orenbren keeps the records of its own meetings. People use the books and the copies, and the work of copying and keeping them is real.

They do not take over medicine. That work stays with the Stilled, who stop a bleeding wound, hold a patient still through the surgery, and can stop a fight before someone is killed. Towns already call on the Stilled as surgeons, menders, and peacekeepers, and the Stillers licence that work and keep the infirmary.

They will not try to Give a Condition, and they will not Strike someone into one, because the cup cannot do either. They will not assign work by Condition. They are not a crown, and they are not the Walled Book. They say every gift is freely given. They will not become a sixth faith. The practice stays in the household, and it stays small.

### What the circle says it made

Among themselves they say **the Cup We Made**. On a quay people say **the Poured God**, with the smile they keep for a rich uncle's hobby, and the name for the presence in the cup is **the Unspent**. Neighbours call the cup a memorial with airs. What a neighbour can see is ordinary: a cup, or a small bowl, usually common ware; one drop of the giver's blood; and a person who has given years away and is still recognizably the same person. They will tell you that they invented whatever is on the other side of the cup. There is no temple, only a shelf, and sometimes a second cup sits beside the first. The cup keeps what they gave, and the members read what it is storing by means of blood, because they believe they created the god they are giving the memory to. The cup cannot Give a Condition, it cannot save anyone from death, and it cannot make anyone Long-Lived. The minutes say that if a member asks the cup for a leaf, the cup keeps what it already has.

In Lestrand the Pourers are an ordinary hobby that happens to have a library key. In Vaethorn a preacher who needs a sin may call the cup an insult to the Mother's gift, and most households will not notice it at all. Threnmaieth wants the minutes on its roll. Molevad's circle files nothing the crown could use.

A long-house that keeps the circle still seats the members, and the members still learn new things from the books. Refusing a seat to someone who left is an argument about lodging, not a heresy trial. The Book-Hands find no other party to stamp, and a Settler who writes the cup up as a contract has misunderstood it. A warden will not record a gift to the cup as a late Condition, because the cup gives none. A hospice is not a cup. A Closed Heir of the Walled Book who is already Long-Lived may join as a private person, and ranking people is not the same thing as giving away your own memory.

### From the minutes

These are the minutes on the Lestrand long-house shelf, in Molevad's hand. Someone drew a face in the margin and scratched it out twice.

> *One year from S. into the Cup We Made. Face this decade: the quiet guest. Blood as record. The circle read a year that was not only S.'s. We do not write which. If S. asks the years back, the return is thinner and the copy stays. If S. asks the cup for a leaf, the cup keeps what it already has. We made the listener. We will not stand it in a square. We will not say Mother. We will say the shelf.*

These are the circle's own words. They record a member, S., giving one year, and they say that a reading of the cup held memories of a year that belonged to more than one person. They also say what happens if S. asks for the years back: the papers come back, and the memory stays in the cup. The scratched-out face in the margin is the circle changing its mind about how to picture the god.

## The Walled Book and the Inner Close

People call the roster **the Walled Book**, the class that keeps it **the Closed Heirs**, and the place **the Inner Close**. The walled city a first day's walk from the Motherwood is **Rothallo** (roh-THAL-oh), the capital of Orenbren. Orenbren is already one of the fifteen great powers, so the Close is not a sixteenth flag of its own. People still say the Inner Close, and the Close, and the walls answer to those names. It is the same city. Orenbren calls the arrangement a guest-compact. Its inns will lodge anyone outside the wall, and the Book rules inside it. Threnmaieth keeps the Threnhael, the roll that writes every person down and means to keep them, and it treats the Book as a rival roll, because the Book will not let Threnmaieth's clerks copy its pages. The First Seat stays in the wood. Rothallo is not the college.

The Closed Heirs are a political class, not a church. At Leaf-Fall a child stands for the Tree, and the Tree either hugs the child, who stays one of the Kept, with no Condition and no Tithe, or lets a coloured leaf fall, which Gives a Condition and the Tithe that Condition carries from then on. A warden Speaks the hug or the colour aloud. The Closed Heirs do not Speak colours, and they do not pour a god. Pouring, in this country, is a Long-Lived person giving years of their own memory into a cup, the deathless hobby of the Pourers, and a Closed Heir who is already Long-Lived may still pour as a private person. The Book does not take that cup up as its work. Its work is to classify people by whether they carry a Tithe, and then to write what the people inside the walls owe on top of that.

Folk still say there was an older empire of the untithed, people who carried no Tithe, and that it sat on the grove, and on that point the folk are right. The everyday historical name is **the Closed Seat**, and the walls of Rothallo are what is left of it. How the Closed Seat broke, the copy, the uprooting, and Hildal's Retreat, the withdrawal that carried a book behind a wall, is the history of the Closing. The Book is what that class is doing now.

### If you meet one

They look like lodging-gentry who never took a Tithe, and they do not take the heavy turns of work inside the walls. Each one carries a key to a gate in the wall, and a person with a Condition may not carry that key. You would miss them if you went looking for clergy. You would find them if you asked who still decides what a Given person owes besides the Tithe they already carry.

**Delamem** (del-AH-mem) keeps the Book of Tithes. Delamem speaks the conservative common speech and will tell you the Book is housekeeping, though the page that assigns labour is in the same hand as the page that assigns a hearth-tax.

**Talnin of the Inner Close** (TAL-nin) is a Closed Heir of the present generation. The name is on the heir-roll, and the tenth year is not finished, so the Tree has not Spoken this child's colour yet. Delamem has already drafted the turns of work for the case if the leaf is not a hug.

What the house fears is a Closed child Given at the next Leaf-Fall, Threnmaieth copying the Book onto the Threnhael, and Orenbren deciding the guest-compact is only a tax on beds.

### What a visitor sees

A visitor to Rothallo does not need to read the Book to see the rank. It shows in four places.

- **Where a person sits.** At a meal in a Closed house, the Kept sit at the end of the table nearest the host, and a guest who received a Condition is shown to the far end, past the last Kept guest. When the Book is read aloud in the Book's house, the Kept sit on the front benches and everyone else sits behind them. Nobody is turned away from the table. The host says where each guest sits, and the guest is expected to sit there.
- **Whose word is taken.** When two people disagree in front of a clerk of the Book, over a debt, a boundary, or a broken pot, and one of them is Kept and the other has a Condition, the clerk writes the Kept person's account as what happened and writes the other account underneath it as "also said." The person with a Condition can still win, and to win they need a second Kept person to say the same thing they said.
- **Who holds a key.** Only a Kept person may hold a key to a gate in the wall, to the Book's house, to a cistern, or to a granary. A person with a Condition holds the key to their own door and to their own stall, and no others.
- **What the school teaches.** The children of the Closed houses learn their letters at a school beside the Book's house, and other families inside the walls may send a child if they pay. The teacher, **Volnai** (VOL-nai), tells the children that a hug is rare, and that a child the Tree hugged was kept back for something better than a colour. Volnai believes this and teaches it every year. The lesson is wrong about the count, which the next section sets beside the teaching.

A person with a Condition who lives inside the walls works for wages, keeps what they earn, owns their tools and their household goods, marries whom they choose, and can own a stall. The Book writes a licence for that stall, and a Kept stall-keeper needs no licence. A person with a Condition can walk out of the gate on any day it is open and live somewhere else. Nobody owns them, and they are not slaves. What they cannot have inside the walls is the heir's place, a gate key, a vote in the Close's compact, or the first word in front of a clerk.

### The lesson and the count

The Close teaches that a hug is rare, and the teaching is wrong. Most towns have Kept neighbours, people the Tree hugged and left without a Condition, and most of those neighbours have never heard of the heir-roll. The shares of who is hugged and who carries a Condition are the ones given with the Conditions. The Close does not teach a second set of numbers for Rothallo, for the Closed houses, or for the heir-roll.

What is actually rare is a family that has stayed without a Condition on purpose, generation after generation, and treats that record as a birthright. The Closed houses manage it in two ways that reinforce each other. An heir who receives a colour is struck from the heir-roll, so the roll only ever lists people who were hugged. An heir also does not swear the kind of oath that can Strike a person, and stays away, where they can, from the other ways a person can be Struck. A Strike is a Condition put on a person later, by a drowning, an oath, or a death. After enough generations, every name on the roll belongs to a hugged person. A family that has kept that record is rare, and being hugged is common.

Delamem and the other clerks of the Book can read the count, and the school does not teach it. If a visitor brings the count to the Close, a clerk will say the count is someone else's record and does not bind the Close, and the school teaches the same lesson the next year.

### The Book of Tithes

The Book of Tithes is a roster rather than a hymn, and the roster does three kinds of work.

- **Taxes.** Coin, board, and a cut of self-paying labour, work a person is paid to do on their own account. An Answered shop inside the walls, a craft-house of a person an element answered and still answers, pays a craft-due the Ember-yard on the quay does not. That yard is where the fire-answered keep their craft. A Two-Bodied ranging-week, the week a person spends in the animal body they also carry, is scheduled as owed work before it is allowed to count as a ranging.
- **Restrictions.** Where a person with a Condition may hold a key, a stall, or a vote in the Close's compact. The Kept hold those. A Given person who keeps a stall does it as a licensed exception, and the exception is written down.
- **Labour.** Everyone who lives inside the walls owes the Close turns of work, the way a town expects each household to take a turn on the watch. The Book keeps the list of turns: the green, the hall, the wall, the road, and the beds outside the gate. A Kept household's turns are mostly the watch on the wall and the keeping of keys. A household with a Condition is given the heavier turns, and more of them, and the Book picks the turn by the Condition. The turns are not the Tithe, so a person with a Condition owes them in addition to the Tithe they already carry, and a person who does not want the turns can leave the city. The Book's list has no claim on anyone who lives outside the walls. The Book does not sell a person, lend a person to an employer, or hand a person over the way an owner hands over a worker. It cannot send for someone who has left.

A Closed Heir who is Given at Leaf-Fall loses status. The Gift is a disinheritance. The hug keeps the name on the heir-roll, and the colour takes it off. The person is still a person, and is no longer a Closed Heir, and the Book already has turns of work written for what they have become.

A Closed Heir who is Struck later, by a drowning, an oath, or a death, is treated the same way. The Book strikes the name from the heir-roll when the clerks learn of it, and writes the person's turns of work under the Condition they now carry.

This ranking is the opposite of Threnmaieth's care-count. The Threnhael writes everyone down in order to keep them, while the Book ranks by whether you carry a Tithe and will strike an heir for receiving a gift.

Orenbren's sleep-line at the gate records who slept in a bed, and the Close's Book records who owes turns of work. Both lists are kept in Orenbren's country, and neither lets the other copy its page.

They will not call the Book a scripture. A preacher who does is either bringing the Motherfaith into a roster, or doing the Close a kindness the Close did not ask for. They will not Speak a colour or officiate a Leaf-Fall. A warden may come in for Turning-Week, and the copy made after that Speaking is the Close's copy, not the Seat's and not the Threnhael's. They will not pour years into a cup as the Book's own work. A Closed Heir who is Long-Lived may still pour years of their own memory, which the Book does not count as its business. They will not hide a Phoenix. A white-fire fall inside the walls would be a problem for the Book to rank. They will not add a sixteenth flag. Rothallo is already Orenbren's capital, and the fifteen great powers already include Orenbren.

The Book reaches people on ordinary paper. A labour-roster names someone. A Given heir wants the heir-line back and will pay in board. Delamem wants a colour-copy the warden has not released. Threnmaieth wants the Book, and the Close wants those clerks off the gate. A Kept pilgrim who takes work inside the walls is put on the heavier turns, because the Book has no record of their hug. Travellers can be hired by the Close, by Orenbren's lodging-guilds, or by a Closed child who just took a colour.

The Book shares a country with other houses that also write people down, and each fight is about which writing a person has to obey.

| House | Everyday | The fight |
|---|---|---|
| Orenbren | Guest-compact. The inns keep beds outside the wall, and the Book rules inside it. | The inns charge for beds and the Book assigns turns of work, so a guest can owe both. The sharpest case is a Turning-Week when the Close will not lodge a Given pilgrim and Orenbren has no other bed. |

### Outside the walls

The Book itself stays inside Rothallo, while the habit of ranking a hug above a colour keeps traveling, and three places show how far that habit reaches and where it stops.

**Orenbren, outside the wall.** The lodging-guilds do not ask a guest's colour, and the sleep-line at the gate does not record it. Near the gate it is different. An inn that wants the Closed houses' business keeps its best table for Kept guests on the nights a Closed house dines there, and moves a guest with a Condition to another table if the Closed house asks. The guild does not forbid this. In Turning-Week, when the Close will not lodge a Given pilgrim, the inns outside the wall take that pilgrim in, and some of them charge more for it.

**Vaethorn, which rejects the ranking in public.** At Eolvaeth, the gift-hall teaches that a colour is the Mother's gift and a hug is the Mother keeping a child as they are, and that neither makes a person better than the other. The gift-hall says this out loud in its Eolthael preaching, and it says the Close's teaching is wrong. A Given person who has left the Close can eat at the gift-hall without being asked for a house. That is why Hithaen (hith-AYN) walked there. Hithaen is one of the disinherited, the Given people a colour struck off the heir-roll, who will not let the next town copy the Book's turns of work onto a slate. They eat where a hearth will have them. They do not ask to be let back into the Close, and they do not preach that the Kept should lose the Book. Hithaen eats in the gift-hall with a letter from behind the wall still unopened, is not a leader of the others, and is not Talnin. When two of the disinherited meet, they do not trade the old house-name. They will not say why. The Waiting Lands have their own unkindness, pointed the other way: a hugged child there is sometimes pitied as passed over by the Mother. The two teachings disagree with each other, and neither one matches the count.

**Brenthael, which copies the seating.** Brenthael is the town with its own Tree a day's walk past the Third Hearth on the Near Mile. At its Turning-Week supper in the square, Kept people sit on the benches nearest the Tree, and people with a Condition sit on the outer benches, even when that splits a family. A child who has just received a colour gets up from an inner bench and moves to an outer one before the food is served. Brenthael has no copy of the Book, no heir-roll, and no list of turns. Nobody in the town can say when the seating started. They keep it because it is how the supper has always been laid out.

### A leaf from the Book of Tithes

This is a leaf from the Book of Tithes, Inner Close, in Delamem's hand. The line for heirs and the line for turns of work share a fold.

> *Talnin, Closed, heir. If the fall is a colour, strike heir. Enter labour under the colour as the page already holds. The hug keeps the line. We do not file this as a slight. We file it as a house. Orenbren may lodge who they witness. They may not lodge who we rank. The Threnhael may count who they can see from the river. They may not copy this page. A gift is a gift. A gift is not a seat.*

This is a page from the Book of Tithes. It records an heir, says the Book will strike the name if the Tree gives a colour, and tells Orenbren and Threnmaieth that neither may copy it. Delamem writes that the Book does not mean the striking as a slight, and that is how a child learns that a leaf can take a name.

## The Protectors (as the square knows them)

When anyone speaks of them, the everyday name is **the Protectors**. The building the world is allowed to know is **the Care**. It is a hospice-endowment, old money behind a closed court, and the story that travels is that the one Phoenix is being kept safe there, which is why no town has seen them in a generation. In that story a Phoenix is the one person the tales have been about. The Care says it holds that person, and says there is only one.

They worship the one. They do not hold the worship in a square, where a town could come and look.

**Vimein** (vih-MAYN) keeps the outer book of the Care. Vimein speaks the conservative common speech, quietly, and says little. They will say the Phoenix is in care, and they will not produce them. They will offer a viewing of the closed court, a hymn that does not name a Mother, and a request that you not carry a story back to a square that has finally gone quiet.

You would take them for a hospice clerk, and the building is a hospice. The wider world has not seen a Phoenix because the Care says it holds the old name, and the squares have stopped looking.

A neighbour can see a hospice with old money and a closed court, and a generation of squares that talk about the Phoenix in the past tense and the present tense in the same sentence, as if the one were both gone and still in the building. They will not stamp a ticket, rent a week, classify a Closed Heir, or pour years into a cup. A Closed Heir is someone the Walled Book has already classed. Pouring a year of memory into a cup is the Pourers' act, not this desk.

What they admit to refusing is the parade. They will not produce the Phoenix for a square. They say every square was staring at the one, and the Care ended that. They say there is only one Phoenix. They will not open the inner court to a relic-hunter, or to a Speaker who wants a colour-copy, a second writing of the leaf. They will not work a wreck on Kumbaan. There is no white-fire, the colour a Phoenix is known by, on that sand.

You meet them at the door and not past it. Someone is asked to carry a letter to the Care, and is not let in. A town wants the Phoenix for a festival and is told the one is in care. A guest who got as far as the inner door is turned away with a kind sentence.

### A visitor's slip

This is a visitor's slip from the Care, in Vimein's hand. The inner court is not on the slip.

> *The one is in care. The stories may rest. We do not produce a person for a square that has already decided what they mean. If you have come to worship, the hymn is said in the closed court and not in the street. If you have come to hunt, you will find a hospice. We keep the one. We keep the quiet.*

These are the Care's own words to a visitor. They say the Phoenix is in care, that nobody will be shown them, and that the Care would like the story left alone.
