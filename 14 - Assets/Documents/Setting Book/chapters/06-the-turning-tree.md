# The Turning Tree

A Turning Tree is a broad, slow, long-lived hardwood, with a canopy like a great oak or a great beech, though the tree is its own kind. It stands at the ceremonial heart of a town, usually in a square, on a green, or in a walled yard the town keeps for it. Out of season it looks like an unusually grand old tree, and the town treats it as one: children climb the low boughs, courts and markets meet in the shade, and lovers carve nothing into the bark, because cutting a mark there is taboo. In the tenth-year solstice week, the midsummer people call Turning-Week, the tree turns. The canopy shifts through colours no ordinary tree wears, and a leaf falls for a child, or the leaves close around them.

People call it a **Turning Tree** for what it does at that solstice. It turns, and it turns a child into what that child will become. When a coloured leaf settles on the child, the colour names a **Condition**, the change that child will grow up carrying, a becoming rather than the people they were born. When the leaves close round the child and no naming colour comes, the child is one of **the Kept**, wanted as they are. The devout call each Tree **a Hand of the Mother**, because they hold that a mother is reaching down through the wood to choose, and the reverent word for that kind of tree, in the liturgy, is **motherwood**. In the old liturgical root tongue the Awakening Tree's own name is **Thaeloren** *(THAEL-or-en)*. *Thael* means Tree and *oren* means to witness, so the name says the Tree that witnesses. People wear that long name down, in everyday speech, to *the Thallow*.

### The Awakening Tree

There is one origin Tree, the **Awakening Tree**, the great first Tree and the place the world's pilgrimage is aimed at. It is old past any dating the faith can rely on, and it is vast, and it is the one Tree that nobody grafted out of another tree, which is why every other Turning Tree descends from it. The liturgy calls it **the First Hand**, and people really do make the journey: devout families travel so that a child can be Turned under the origin, and some of those families settle in the country around it. The faith's central clergy sit in the Tree's shadow as the **First Seat**, a college of tree-wardens, the people whose work is the Trees, with no throne in that shadow. The Tree stands on Maiethorn, in the Sacred Core, the old country of hills, river valleys, and Tree-crowned towns around that grove.

### Grafts

Every town Turning Tree is a living graft of that one tree. When a town takes root, and especially when the town is so far off that a family could never reach the Awakening Tree, someone carries out a **scion** and plants it in the new town's square. A scion is not a seedling that grew from a seed the Tree dropped. It is cut tissue of the one Tree, kept alive on the road, and planted into the new ground so the same wood grows on. That planting is a **graft**.

A town gets two things from the arrangement. Ordinary families walk to their own Tree for the **Leaf-Fall**, the ceremony at which a colour falls or the leaves hug, so the rite stays local and the distance of the road does not exile anyone from it. This is the ordinary way in the Years of Hands, the years when a town's own Tree is what people expect. It was not always so. People devout enough to want the origin still travel to the Awakening Tree, either to have a child Turned there or simply to stand under it. On Heskoren, the continent where that wave of living wood is still unfinished, and in any place where a graft has failed, the walk is a necessity and not only a devotion.

The devout call each grafted tree a Hand of the Mother. Anyone in the square can see the cut and the planting, and whether a mind is choosing through that wood remains the faith's argument, which the horticulture does not close.

The graft is a rite, and it is also the work of keeping living wood. The tree-wardens cut a scion from the Awakening Tree or, in ordinary practice, from a healthy town Tree that is cutting on the origin's behalf. The cutting travels in living earth, and it is planted with ceremony. The first time a carrying succeeded, someone took living wood from Thaeloren, or from a branch that came away in a hand, carried it as far as the wood can go in a day and still meet new ground green, set it into that ground, and it took. Everyday speech calls that taking the **First Cut**. What the college captured afterward was not the living wood, which it could not own, but the right to say who may cut next, and that right is a paper. The cutting had been a heresy before it was a permit. The clergy count their years from the taking itself, so the taking is Cut-year 0 and the present year is Cut-year 387.

A young Tree cannot Turn anyone yet. A scion that has just been planted needs years, a generation by the reckoning people actually use, before it is mature enough to hold a Leaf-Fall. While it grows, the new town leans on a neighbour's Tree, and the children travel to the nearest town that can Turn them. That borrowing is where a lot of the small texture of the world comes from: rivalries between towns, customs that belong to the road, and the sentence people still say, "we Turned at Harrow's Tree before we had our own."

### When Trees sicken and die

The wood is mortal, so a blighted or dying Tree is a real crisis for the town, because the town may lose the power to Turn its own children and have to fall back on a pilgrimage or on a neighbour. A Tree that has died is replaced only by a fresh graft, and then by the long wait while the new wood matures. A mature Tree is hard to move once it is in the ground. The practice is to graft a new scion, rather than to dig the old tree up and carry it.

A town with no Tree is what the frontier usually looks like. The place is too new, or too remote, or it has lost the Tree it had. Towns in that condition send their ten-year-olds onto the road to be Turned, and the journey is one of the ways the map marks its own edge.

### The Leaf-Fall

The Leaf-Fall is the rite the rest of the world is built around. It is held in Turning-Week, the tenth-year solstice week, which is the High Solstice of midsummer, when the Trees hold their colour and the leaves fall.

Every child who has turned ten since the last solstice is brought by their family. The witness-circle, the people gathered to see that child, comes together, and a tree-warden of the town officiates. The First Seat is present only in a week when people have come on pilgrimage. The hour is a sacred rite and, at the same time, the largest festival the settlement holds all year, something like a christening and a graduation and a fair held in the same afternoon. In a village the witness-circle simply is the town. In a city the circle is a hearth, a group of people who already know the child, and the square is still full.

The children come to the Tree one at a time, or in the small cohort who share that year, and they stand where the canopy can reach them, and they wait. The Tree turns, and it lets fall whatever it is going to let fall. The rest of the town watches from the edge of the boughs.

A healthy Tree, with a child under it and the town watching, does one of two things, and the wardens do not write a third outcome into the book.

A colour falls, and a Condition is **Given**. One leaf, or more than one, in a colour that means something, drifts down and settles on the child. The colour is the name of the Condition. This is the outcome people expect, the one they call proper, and the one they celebrate. The child grows up already knowing what they will become, and the town trains them and guilds them toward that life.

Or the leaves hug the child, and there is no colour, and the child is Kept. Instead of a coloured fall, the ordinary leaves gather and wrap the child, a green embrace with no colour that names anything. The faith holds the hug to be a kindness the Tree chose, and the schism argues over that reading, which the faith has not closed.

The Leaf-Fall is warm, and it has weight, and it belongs to the whole town: a breath that the circle holds, every eye on one child, a parent who was Given this same colour years ago weeping when it falls again, or a parent who had quietly hoped the leaves would hug. The **Tithe**, the upkeep a Condition later asks of the person who carries it, belongs to the years after this afternoon. The shadow that belongs to the rite itself is a human one, a wrong word spoken over a fall, or a week that was missed.

### Turning-Week in a city

A Gift can still be given in that one week, and not in some other week, and the Tree is still the centre. A city of tens of thousands does not grow a second rite to get through the number of children. It divides the witnesses into hearths, the groups of people who already know the child they have brought.

The stands run by hearth across the week. In the morning, the afternoon, and the eve, a quay-gang or a courtyard or a guild-stair or a lot takes its turn, eighty to two hundred people who already know the child, so that hundreds of ten-year-olds are never stood up together as one crowd nobody could read.

The warden **Speaks**. To Speak is to say aloud whether the Tree gave a colour or a hug, and to name the colour when there is one. The warden does not write those gifts onto a roll. The week-slate, the board kept for the week, only records which hearth stands at which bell. It is a festival schedule, the same kind of board that says who has the square for the fair.

The legal fact is that a hearth witnesses a child, and a crowd does not. When a desk far from the city asks who saw the standing, one pillar of the hearth carries the memory. The pillar stood in that hearth, and already carries one of three Conditions: a Long-Lived, an ageless keeper of memory; a Bound, who swore a contract in extremity and is still keeping its terms; or a Far-Voiced, whose voice carries a feeling exactly as far as they mean it to, and no farther.

Threnmaieth, the crown on Maiethorn that keeps a written roll of its people and calls the writing a way of keeping them whole, is the one place that writes the stand down. A clerk who copies the colours off a week-slate has begun a register of Conditions, and a register is a thing the hearths do not keep.

A village still watches the rite as one circle.

### Reading the colours

The colour of the leaf that falls is how the town knows which Condition has been Given, and the way that reading is known is also what gives the faith a civic office. Ordinary people know the common colours. Everyone in an ordinary town can already name the deep-red leaf of the Long-Lived, and the storm-grey fall of the Two-Bodied, who carry a second animal self and still command it. The tree-wardens hold the reading that counts. They confirm it out loud at the ceremony, and they handle a fall that is rare, or subtle, or mixed: a colour almost nobody present has seen, or a fall that could honestly be read two ways. In a small town the warden is the one who Speaks. In a pilgrimage-town, or at the First Seat, a **Speaker** is the elder who holds the rare-colour texts and the last word on a mixed fall.

The Conditions that are only ever Given have colours that are clear and widely known, because those colours are only ever seen at a Tree. Three Conditions can also be **Struck**, which means a person can come to them later, away from the Tree, and those three can fall as colours too. They are the Far-Voiced; the Stilled, who can hold a moment still, a bleeding wound or a blade already swinging or a patient under the knife; and the Taken-In, a person the wild took partway into itself and gave back changed. A Far-Voiced who was Given and a Far-Voiced who was Struck share a nature and do not share a story. The Answered are Given only: a person whom one element answered, fire or water or earth and stone or air, and that element has gone on answering ever since, under a leaf of lamp-amber or brass.

| Leaf-colour | Condition | Who can read it |
|---|---|---|
| **deep red / wine** | Long-Lived | folk-known, and already a proverb |
| **storm-grey** | Two-Bodied | folk-known, and already a proverb |
| **lamp-amber / brass** | the Answered | folk-known |
| **sea-blue** | Far-Voiced | folk-known. A child Given the blue leaf is trained toward it, and a Far-Voiced who was Struck has no leaf at all |
| **pale stone / marble-white** | the Stilled | folk-known, and feared, so the warden says the name carefully |
| **copper-green / verdigris** | the Taken-In | folk-known, once you know not to confuse it with the hug. This green is the wrong green for a hug: metallic, and not the Tree's own green |
| **white-fire / opal** | Phoenix | clergy-known, from the texts. Almost no town now alive has seen it fall. A warden who names it at a ceremony has just made history, and has also made a problem |

The Tree never Strikes, so the Returned and the Bound have no colour, and the Unbound has none either. A Returned is a person who died with something unfinished, and whose purpose got them walking again. The Bound come by the contract, not by a leaf. The Unbound is what a term-limited contract leaves behind when the term ends and the other party to that contract collects the piece the person named at the signing, so they walk away free of the terms and short of that piece, and that becoming chains off Bound instead of falling as a leaf of its own. The hug that marks the Kept is ordinary summer-green, and it carries no naming colour. That is why the leaf of the Taken-In must not be that same ordinary green.

A fall that is rare, or mixed, or already being argued, two colours at once, or a colour nobody in the square has seen, or a reading of Phoenix that someone wants to be wrong, is why the tree-wardens are a civic office and not only a devout one. White-fire is the leaf of a Phoenix, a person who dies down to ash and comes back a little further from the person they were, and there is only ever one of them alive in the world at a time, which is why a living town almost never sees that leaf, and why naming it is history and a problem together.

The Tree only **Gives**, or it **Keeps**. People who are Struck received a Condition through a later threshold, a drowning, an oath, or a death they survived, and that change happens away from the Tree, with no leaf and with no ceremony. Whether the faith still counts the Struck as the Mother's own doing is the schism that is still live.

### Questions a warden gets asked

These are the questions families actually bring to a warden, and the answers are the custom of the town. The door is still **one week**, the High Solstice week of the year a child turns ten. In the Walking Years the only Tree was the origin, so a tenth year was a journey or it was no Leaf-Fall at all, and the towns of the Years of Hands did not grow a second chance beside that narrow door. A healthy Tree, with a child stood under it, still gives a colour or a hug.

| Question | What the town does | What that does to a life |
|---|---|---|
| **We missed the week.** The child had a fever, or the pass was shut, or the boat did not come, so the child was ten and was not under the boughs. | They are **unTurned**. The child never stood, and the door has shut, so next summer they may visit the square and they do not stand for a Gift. The doors by which a person can still be Struck open later, somewhere else. The expensive mercy is the old one: keep a nine-year-old through the winter near a Tree that is sound, so this particular week cannot be missed. | The child has no colour and no hug. The neighbours remember the miss, and folk will call the child Kept, though the law will not, because Kept is a hug the town was there to see. |
| **We will not stand.** The family refuses the Tree. The Old Ways, the land-faith that was already walking rivers and groves before the grafts, treat a refusal as another door. The Open Table, the supper-faith of the storm-walled isle, will not walk a child of the table to a Tree. Others refuse from fear, or from a quarrel. | The Tree does not chase them. The legal result is the same result as a miss: the child is **unTurned**, and the door is shut. A warden may ask one time. The warden does not put a rope on a child in order to produce a Gift. | The Old Ways already count that refusal as a different door, and not as a failure of the wood. The Open Table's quarrel over the walk can use up the week. |
| **Who brings an orphan?** There is no living parent, or there is no parent who will come. | The town acts as the family. Kin are asked first, then the household that feeds the child, then the **warden-hearth**, the warden's house on the square. An orphan who stands is witnessed the way anyone else is witnessed. An orphan that nobody brings is a miss. | Some of these children are older than the grafts: a Given child whose only crowd was a continent away, back when the origin was the only Tree, or a child of a road-house with nobody to walk them home. What they need is someone to vouch for them. They do not need a second leaf. |
| **An adult who never Turned.** A road-child, a child whose family refused, a child of a storm-year, a child raised on the isle, a child who was held over and missed the week even so. | They remain **unTurned**. They do not stand for a Gift at thirty. The town can vouch for them as the person the town raised, and they can still be Struck later. Some hearths file such a person as Kept in order to be kind, and that kindness is sloppy law. | The life is a story a person can carry, and it is not a late appointment the Tree will keep. What remains is a later door, or an ordinary life with no Tithe and a thinner witness. |
| **Can they stand again?** The family wanted a different colour, or they regret the hug, or a parent is sure the Tree missed. | **No.** A standing that was completed, whether it ended in a colour or in a hug, under a Tree the warden called sound, happens once. The blessing already says that the Hand does not reach twice by chance. A warden who offers a second Leaf-Fall is not being generous. That warden is inventing a rite the wood has never answered. | A town may correct a **Speaking**, the word the warden said aloud, and a town may not run the fall a second time. The year people cite is the Wrong Green. |
| **Our Tree is sick this week.** The canopy is blighted, or it is dying, or the scion is still too young. | Do not stand the children under wood the warden already knows is unsound. Borrow a neighbour's canopy, and borrow it in this same week. A warden who lets a cohort stand under a Tree they know cannot Turn has spent the week, and those children are unTurned. | The next year does not repair a week that has already been spent. |
| **The child is already Struck.** They drowned at nine, or they died and came back in the spring with a purpose still unfinished, or a contract no ten-year-old should have been able to reach did reach them. | Bring the child to be **acknowledged**, and do not bring them for a Gift. The Tree does not Strike, and it does not lay a second Condition on top of a first one. Some canopies hug such a child anyway, and the town reads the hug as a welcome. A colour falling on a child who is already Struck is a problem for the Seat, and it is not a second life. | The child is Struck. The town vouches for them again, and the child is guilded if the door they came through is a hazardous one. The tenth year is how the square takes them in. It is not a chance to do the year over. |
| **Twins, or two colours, or a fall that nobody will name.** | Two children mean two standings and two outcomes. A fall that is mixed, or that people are already arguing, is the reason the Speaking exists. Colours the square already knows, the folk can read for themselves, and the warden's word is what makes that reading a civic fact. The texts that cover the rare falls are kept at the First Seat. | Two practices in one square is a common arrangement. A Watcher, of the older Tree-faith that keeps the wood and will not say the Mother's name, may describe the same fall alongside the warden, and not over the warden. Two Speakers who both claim the last word in one square is a fight. |
| **The warden named it wrong.** | The Gift does not move when the word was wrong. The town is what moves. Someone Speaks a second word, and the child is not stood again. The town trains the child toward the life the leaf actually gave, un-trains the life it named by mistake, and apologises to the neighbour whose week it used. | The year the square still cites is the **Wrong Green**: Harrow's, in Cut-year 248, a hug Spoken aloud as copper-green, and two summers the child spent in a garden that had no way to teach them, because there had been no Tithe to pay and no Gift to tend. The square goes on citing that year. What it has is a civic name that wasted years of a life. The Tree is not haunted. |
| **White-fire fell, or someone wants to say that it did.** | A warden of a town does not guess at Phoenix. They send a runner to the First Seat. To name white-fire is to make history, and it is also to make a problem. A Speaking of Phoenix that turns out to be false is the loud cousin of the Wrong Green. | Almost no town now living has seen white-fire fall. If the Seat agrees that this child received it, the child cannot hide what they are. The weight of that belongs to the Phoenix, and not to a ruling invented in the square. |
| **A child of the Open Table, or a Yumboe, under our boughs.** A Yumboe is one of the good people born on Kumbaan, the storm-walled isle, and not a child the Tree made. | If the child stands during the week of their tenth year, the Tree does what the Tree does for anyone who stands. If the host will not bring the child, the week can end without them. There is no mission sent to Kumbaan, and there is no warden of the moon. When the fight over the child happens, it is already happening in the port. | The Open Table holds that the child was never lacking anything. A warden from the mainland holds that a tenth year is a tenth year. Neither of those people is the villain of the week, and the door does not wait while they finish the argument. |
| **The city cannot know every child.** The port holds tens of thousands of people, and hundreds of ten-year-olds come due in the one week. | The children stand **by hearth**, in the slots the week has named, and the square is full. What gets remembered is the street that brought the child, together with one pillar who can carry that memory later, and the colours are not written down. | "I am of the third quay" is how a person of the city says where they belong. |

**UnTurned is not the same thing as Kept.** Kept means the leaves hugged the child and the town watched it happen. UnTurned means the child never stood. From the street the two lives can look alike, because neither one carries a Tithe and neither one carries a guild, and only the Kept life was witnessed at the Tree. Citizenship still depends on who saw you. A neighbour who is unTurned is vouched for as the child the town raised, and not as a colour, and not as a hug.

A failure in the rite means a standing was not completed, and the Tree has not produced a third outcome to cover the gap. While the Tree is healthy, the child is under the boughs, and the town is watching, a colour falls or the leaves close in a hug. A missed week, a refusal, or a week spent under wood that could not Turn leaves no Given. The later doors, the ways a person can still be Struck, are the whole of the mercy that remains. If a fire takes the square, or a bough comes down, or the child is pulled away before the fall, and the week still has days left and the Tree is sound, the standing is finished later in that same week. Once the week has gone, the town treats the child as a miss. When the Speaking fails, the precedent is the Wrong Green: the town corrects the word that was said, and it does not bargain with the wood for a different life.

A parent who asks what happens if the Tree simply does nothing is asking a question the hearths do not keep a form for. A skeptic will say that is because it is just a tree, and trees that Turn, Turn. A believer will say the Hand does not come to the square to shrug. The working clergy side with the book they have: two outcomes, or the child was not there.
