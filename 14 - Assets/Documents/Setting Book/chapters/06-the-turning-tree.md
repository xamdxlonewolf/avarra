# The Turning Tree

A Turning Tree is a broad, slow, long-lived hardwood. Its canopy is like a great oak or beech, but it is its own kind. It stands at the ceremonial heart of a town, usually in a square, a green, or a walled temple-yard kept for it. Out of season it looks like an unusually grand old tree, and townsfolk treat it as one: children climb the low boughs, courts and markets convene in its shade, lovers leave the bark uncarved, and to cut a mark in it is taboo. In the tenth-year solstice week it turns. The canopy shifts through colours no ordinary tree wears, and a leaf falls for a child, or the leaves close around them.

The everyday word is **Turning Tree**, for what it does at the solstice: it *turns*, and it *turns children into what they'll become*. The devout call each one **a Hand of the Mother**, and the reverent species-word in liturgy is **motherwood**. In the old liturgical tongue the Tree is **Thaeloren** *(thae-LOR-en)*: *thael* (Tree) + *oren* (to witness), "the Tree that witnesses." Everyday worn form: **the Thallow**.

### The Awakening Tree

There is one origin Tree: the **Awakening Tree**. It is the great, first Tree. People come to it on pilgrimage from across the world. It is old beyond reliable dating, and vast. The cuttings begin with it. Every other Turning Tree descends from it. In liturgy it is **the First Hand**. Devout families make the journey to be Turned under the origin; some settle nearby. The faith's central clergy sit in its shadow: the **First Seat**, a college of tree-wardens, the people whose work is the Trees. It stands on Maiethorn, in the Sacred Core, the old country around that Tree.

### Grafts

Every town Turning Tree is a living graft of the Awakening Tree. A **graft** here is horticulture in the old sense: a cutting taken from living wood and joined to new ground so it grows as the same plant. As towns take root, especially far-flung ones a family could never reach the origin from, a sapling grafted from the Awakening Tree is carried out and planted in the new town's square. A **scion** is that cutting: tissue cut from the one Tree, kept alive on the road and joined to new ground.

Everyday families walk to their town's Tree for the **Leaf-Fall**, the tenth-year solstice when the leaves fall for the children. A family can keep the rite in its own town. That is the ordinary way in the Years of Hands, the age when a town keeps its own Tree. It was not always so. The truly faithful travel to the Awakening Tree itself, the first Hand, to have a child Turned there or simply to stand under the origin. On Heskoren, the outer land, and wherever a graft has failed, the walk is a necessity.

The faithful hold that because every scion is continuous tissue of the one Tree, "the Turning Trees are her hands" is literally true: when any town's Tree chooses, it is the one power choosing through a grafted hand. Skeptics grant the grafting. It is observable horticulture. They hold that the act is the plant, doing what a plant does, and that the plant is the whole of it.

A graft is a rite as well as horticulture. Cutting a scion from the Awakening Tree (or, in practice, from an established healthy town Tree acting on the origin's behalf) is done by the tree-wardens. The cutting is carried in living earth and planted with ceremony. How that rite was captured, first as a heresy, then as a paper, is the First Cut.

A young Tree can't yet Turn. A freshly planted scion takes years, a generation by common reckoning, to mature enough to hold a Leaf-Fall. New towns lean on a neighbour's Tree in the meantime; children travel to the nearest turning town. A younger town still says, "We Turned at Harrow's Tree before we had our own."

### When Trees sicken and die

They are mortal wood, not indestructible. A blighted or dying Tree is a genuine civic crisis. A town may lose its ability to Turn its own children and fall back on pilgrimage or a neighbour. A dead Tree is replaced only by a fresh graft and the long wait. A mature Tree is hard to move once planted; the practice is to graft a new scion rather than transplant.

A town without a Tree is the natural frontier condition: too new, too remote, or Tree-bereaved. Such places send their ten-year-olds on the road to Turn, and the journey marks them as people of the frontier.

### The Leaf-Fall

The Leaf-Fall is the rite the whole world is built around. It is held in **Turning-Week**, the week bracketing the High Solstice of midsummer, when the Trees hold their colour and the leaves fall.

Every child who turns ten since the last solstice is brought by family. The town gathers as witness. A tree-warden officiates: the person who tends that town's Tree and says what the leaves did. The town's own warden does it. The First Seat is present only in a pilgrimage week. It is at once a sacred rite and the town's biggest festival of the year: part christening, part graduation, part fair.

Children come to the Tree one at a time (or in the small cohort of a given year), stand within the canopy's reach, and wait. The Tree turns and lets fall what it will. The rest of the town watches from the edge of the boughs.

Two outcomes:

- **A colour falls, and a Condition is Given.** One or more leaves of a telling colour drift down and settle on the child. The colour names the Condition. This is expected, "proper," celebrated. The child grows up already knowing what they'll become, and is trained and guilded toward it.
- **The leaves hug, no colour, and the child is Kept.** Instead of a coloured fall, the ordinary leaves *gather and wrap the child*: a green embrace, no naming colour. This marks one of **the Kept**: kept whole, wanted in their own right. The faith holds the hug as a chosen kindness. The schism argues over that reading.

The feel of the hour is a held breath, a whole town watching a single child, a parent who was themselves Given the same colour weeping when it falls again, or a parent who quietly hoped for the hug. The Leaf-Fall itself is warm, weighty, and communal. The **Tithe**, the upkeep a Condition must meet, is where the dread lives.

### Turning-Week in a city

A Gift can still be given only in that **one week**. The Tree is still at the centre. A city of tens of thousands keeps that one Tree and that one week. It **nests** the witness: the watching is split into smaller circles.

- **Stands are by hearth**, the neighbours who already know the child, in named slots of the week. A festival schedule, like who has the square for the fair.
- **The warden says the colour or the hug aloud.** The colours are not written in a roll.
- **The crowd in the square saw a crowd. The hearth saw the child.** That is the legal fact. One pillar of the hearth carries that memory when a distant office asks. A pillar is a Long-Lived, a Bound, or a Far-Voiced who stood there.

A village still watches as one circle. The size of the square is not solved by writing down who received which gift.

### Reading the colours

The colour that falls tells the town which Condition has been Given.

The core colour-meanings are folk-known. An ordinary townsperson can read the obvious ones. Everyone knows what the deep-red Long-Lived leaf or the storm-grey Two-Bodied fall looks like. The tree-wardens hold the authoritative reading, confirm it aloud at the ceremony, and handle the rare, subtle, or mixed falls: a colour few have seen, or a fall that could be read two ways. This gives the wardens a civic job as well as a devout one. Someone has to say what the leaf meant, and people also pray at the Tree.

The Given-only Conditions have clear, well-known colours (they're only ever seen at the Tree). The Struck-capable Conditions (Far-Voiced, the Stilled, the Taken-In) *can* fall as colours too, which is why a Given Far-Voiced and a Struck Far-Voiced share a nature but not a story.

| Leaf-colour | Condition | Who can read it |
|---|---|---|
| **deep red / wine** | Long-Lived | folk-known (already proverbial) |
| **storm-grey** | Two-Bodied | folk-known (already proverbial) |
| **lamp-amber / brass** | the Answered | folk-known |
| **sea-blue** | Far-Voiced | folk-known (a Given blue-leaf child is trained; a Struck Far-Voiced has no leaf at all) |
| **pale stone / marble-white** | the Stilled | folk-known, and feared. The warden says it carefully |
| **copper-green / verdigris** | the Taken-In | folk-known. This green is metallic, distinct from the Tree's own summer green and from the hug |
| **white-fire / opal** | Phoenix | clergy-known from texts. Almost no living town has seen it fall. A warden who names it at a ceremony has just made history (and a problem). |

The Tree never Strikes. Struck comes later, by a threshold away from the Tree, so Returned and Bound have **no colour** (and the Unbound has none; it chains off Bound). The Kept hug is ordinary summer-green, *no naming colour*. That is why the Taken-In leaf must not be ordinary green.

Rare, mixed, or argumentative falls (two colours; a colour no one present has seen; a Phoenix-reading someone wants to be wrong) are why the tree-wardens are a civic office as well as a devout one.

A Tree never Strikes. The Tree only **Gives** or **Keeps**. The Struck, Conditions that arrive through a later threshold, a drowning, an oath, a death survived, happen *away* from the Tree, with no leaf and no ceremony. The faith's reading of the Struck, whether they're still the Mother's doing, is exactly the live schism.

### Questions a warden gets asked

What a warden answers, when the week is already going wrong:

| What they ask | What you say | What you do not |
|---|---|---|
| **We missed the week.** Fever, a shut pass, a boat that did not come. | They are **unTurned**: the child never stood. Their week for a Gift is over. Next summer they may visit the square; they do not stand for a Gift. A Struck threshold can still come later. The costly mercy is the old one: house a nine-year-old through the winter near a sound Tree. | Call them Kept. Kept is a hug the town watched. |
| **We will not stand.** The Old Ways, a faith with its own rites from before the grafts arrived; the Open Table, the Isle's own faith; fear; a quarrel. | The Tree does not chase them. Same legal result as a miss: **unTurned**, and the week for a Gift is over. A warden may ask once. They do not put a rope on a child to make a Gift. | Treat refusal as a failure of the wood. |
| **Who brings an orphan?** | The town is the family. Kin first; then the household that feeds them; then the **warden-hearth**, the warden's house on the square. An orphan who stands is witnessed like anyone. An orphan no one brings is a miss. | A second leaf as consolation. |
| **An adult who never Turned.** | They stay **unTurned**. They do not stand for a Gift at thirty. They can be vouched as the person the town raised. They can still be Struck. | A late appointment. |
| **Can they stand again?** Wrong colour wanted. Hug regretted. | **No.** A completed standing — colour *or* hug, under a Tree the warden called sound — is once. You may correct a **Speaking**, the colour or the hug as the warden said it. You may not rerun a fall. | Offer a second Leaf-Fall. The wood has never answered one. |
| **Our Tree is sick this week.** | Do not stand them under unsound wood. Borrow a neighbour's canopy *this same week*. A warden who lets a cohort stand under a Tree they know cannot Turn has spent the week. Those children are unTurned. | Next year as a repair. |
| **White-fire fell. Or someone wants to say it did.** | A town-warden does not guess Phoenix. They send to the First Seat by runner. Naming white-fire is history, and a problem. | Guess from a book in the square. |
| **A child of the Open Table, the Isle's faith. A Yumboe, a person of that isle, under our boughs.** | If they stand in their tenth-year week, the Tree does what it does. If the host will not bring them, the week can end. No warden is sent to Kumbaan, the storm-walled isle, to fetch a child. | Wait for the argument to finish. The week does not wait. |
| **The city cannot know every child.** | They stand **by hearth**, in named slots. The square is full. The memory is the street that brought them, plus one pillar who can carry it. The colours stay unwritten. | A city register. |

Kept means the leaves hugged and the town watched. UnTurned means the child never stood. Both lives can look the same: no Tithe to meet, and no guild. Of those two lives, only the hug was witnessed at the Tree. Citizenship still runs on who saw you. An unTurned neighbour is vouched as the child the town raised.

A miss, a refusal, or a week spent under unsound wood leaves a child unTurned. Struck remains the later mercy.
