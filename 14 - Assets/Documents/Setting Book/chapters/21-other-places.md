# Other Places

The twelve seats have their own chapters. What follows is the ground those chapters point at and do not sit in: three stones nobody has been allowed to date, a hall on the far side of the storm-wall, and three towns the roads already use when a Tree is sick, when a guest has to be fed before a colour is named, or when a mile has been pulled into a square and then stopped. Harrow's Green stays the grove-town in Saelvaeth's march, Ornsael stays the well-town in Saelthael's dry country, and Larbril stays the city. The First Seat, the college of tree-wardens, stays in the wood. One kind of square is still only a kind. Two claimants on one green stays unwritten, and the last section of this chapter says what that hole is.

## The Low Wall

In Orenbren lodging-country, the stretch of inns within the first days' walk of the Motherwood, a low run of stone leaves the present road, stays outside Rothallo, the city people still call the Inner Close, and stops in a fold of ground that is now lodging-fields and the back garden of an old road-house. Everyday speech calls it the Low Wall. Folk also call it the Wall Against No One. The Close, the walled ground people still live in, will tell you the wall is theirs. Scratch the lichen and the quarries part company, because the Close is one quarry and this wall is another, so the claim and the rock are already arguing. What no one could date sits with the leftovers of the count. The stone is from before the Walk, the years that have no start and no first year anyone can honestly write, and it is not a date for the Turning Tree, and it is not the Inner Close.

The wall is shoulder-high in most places, higher where the ground dips, and broken in three gaps that people use as gates. It does not enclose a town, and it does not face the grove. It faces out, if out still means anything, toward lodging-fields and that back garden. No one living built it. The Watching, the faith that keeps the Tree as something already seeing and will not let a later name start its history, will fight any Cut-year a clerk tries to scratch into it. A Cut-year is a numbered year in the count the wardens use, running from the First Cut, and the present year of that count is Cut-year 387. The Old Ways, who set a first-meal where they mean a guest, will leave that meal at a gap and will not call the wall a guest-Tree. Motherfaith wardens, who serve the faith that gives the Tree a mother, want a ribbon taken off, because children already climb the wall the way they climb a mile-shrine, and this wall is not a mile-shrine.

The Closing, the later work of dressed stone that people still live inside, made a different wall. Hildal's Retreat is that work, taller than this and still inhabited. People who say the Low Wall is the Close's outer ring are doing a claim.

**At the table**

- **Want** A child brought down off the high dip, labour taken off the garden, and a year kept from being scratched into the panel.
- **Have** Three gaps, an inward step, a blank name-stone, and a garden-end loft.
- **Fear** The Close confirming the wall as theirs, a clerk dating the Before, and a knife-ribbon left here in error.
- **Live conflict** The Close's claim, a Watcher's refusal, the lodging-guild's nuisance, and the climb itself.
- **Enters a scene** A labour-gang in the person-gap, a child on the high dip, and a chisel at the panel.
- **How this site talks** As a walk and an argument, not as a warden's notice.

One gap has a socket that might have held a post, one has been widened so a cart can pass, and one is only wide enough for a person. The Close's labour-roster sometimes assigns a walk through that last gap as owed work. The Book, the Close's own record of who owes, cannot make that walk count as owning the stone. On the field side the footing is built as if someone expected to stand behind the wall and look out. There is no walkway left, and there is a habit of standing there anyway. Mid-run, a slab carries a panel that once held letters. The letters are gone, and they were taken off rather than worn away. Watchers say that taking is correct. A Closed Heir, someone who still claims a rank inside the Close, says the panel was theirs. A lodging-guild says a later pilgrim stole a luck-name. At the garden end the wall dives into the back of a hearth that still lets beds, a road-house of the same kind as the Third Hearth and not Thilim's house. The innkeep waters the last course of stone as if it were a pot-ring, the circle where a pot would sit, and it is not a pot-ring.

It was one summer, and it was not a school of diggers. In Hale-month of Cut-year 381, Hale-month being the spring month when the land is counted sound and the crowds come to climb, **Nurreith** *(NUR-rayth)* and three lodging-guild hands opened the bed under the blank name-stone. Delamem, for the Walled Book, had paid for a chip that could be filed as the Close's quarry. A Watcher stood in the cut and would not let a chisel touch the panel. **Thuda** *(THOO-dah)* wanted the gang off the garden by dark, and pulled them. What they fought over was the chip, and under the chip the right to call the wall a page. Delamem needed the quarry to match. Nurreith needed the labour back on the guest-beds, and also needed the chip to be worth the afternoon, or the guild would call the day a waste. The Watcher needed no Cut-year on the stone. Thuda needed the person-gap empty and the last course left as a pot-ring, which it is not.

The chip was the other quarry, and nobody scratched a year. They filled the bed the same week, and the fill is newer than the taking of the letters, so the fill can date the quarrel and cannot date the wall, the Tree, or the Before. The next person with a shovel is still a person with a shovel. No office came of the afternoon, and no method came of it either. The Close still wants a confirmation, the Watchers still want the panel blank, and the garden still wants its evenings. Nurreith is a given name, the kind a neighbour says out loud. A stranger who hears *Nurreith of the garden-end* has already made more address out of it than Thuda likes. There is no house-name.

You come in the way the wall already lets people through. The cart-gap has been widened, so you walk in the way a delivery would, and the Close prefers this gap when they want the wall to look like theirs. The person-gap is only wide enough for one, and the labour-roster assigns a walk through it as owed work, so you can be sent in or you can send someone out. The socket-gap might have held a post, and Old Ways leave a first-meal here some weeks, so you can step over a bowl to go through. Under the name-stone the panel's bed is loose if you have an afternoon and a reason. The way in is a crawl, not a dungeon level. It goes along the footing toward the inward step and stops, and there is no under-city. Thuda lets beds against the last course, so you can sleep in the garden-end loft and wake with the wall in the window.

While you are there, the Close wants a confirmation they can file, and Delamem will pay for a quarry-match that is not true. A Watcher will not let a Cut-year touch the panel, and will stand in the chisel's way. The lodging-guild wants climbers off the high dip before Eolthael, and wants the labour off the garden now. Thuda wants the person-gap empty by dark, will feed you, and will not call the stone a shrine. Hale-month is when the crowds climb. Ledger-month, the late-summer month when contracts come due, is the quieter one, and it is meaner about who hired you.

You can walk the wall in an afternoon, sleep in that loft, and be asked to fetch a child down off the high dip. The hire changes with who finds you. The Close will pay to have the wall confirmed as theirs, a Watcher will pay to stop a clerk from dating it, and Thuda will pay to pull a labour-gang out of the person-gap. What you might find, and still no year, is a different quarry's chip in the name-stone's bed, with the Cut-year 381 fill on top of it, a pot-ring that is not motherwood, a knife-ribbon someone left because they thought this was the nameless knife's first house, which it is not, a second footing behind the inward step that never got a walkway, and scratch-wear on the person-gap that is newer than the Close's claim. A visitor can see a ruined wall, a climb, and an inn's back garden. From that they can guess two quarries, a claim, and a Book that assigns a walk through a gap. Nobody knows who the wall was against, or whether against is the right word.

> *The Close sent three Conditioned to walk the person-gap as owed work. They are not our beds and they are not the Close's wall. The children will climb whether we complain. If a Speaker asks, it is not a shrine. If Delamem asks, it is not a page. If a Watcher asks, we did not scratch a year. Pull your labour off our garden. The complaint calls the three walkers Conditioned, people who carry a Condition, the acquired change that stacks on the ancestry a person is born with, and it dates the stone by when the beds were full, which already counts years without cutting a Cut-year into the panel. A Speaker, in that note, is someone who would ask whether the wall is a shrine. Delamem would treat a gap in the wall as a row in the Book, and a gap is not a row.

## The Seeing-Ring

On a hill in Maiethorn's old Watching country, the hymn-country where people kept a watch on the Tree before they gave it a mother, north-east of Maiethlir, stands a ring of stones older than any name a clerk will be allowed to write. Someone later put a shrine on the ring, and someone after that took the shrine's roof off and left the stones. Everyday speech calls it the Seeing-Ring, folk call it the High Stones, and Watchers keep a night there without calling it a temple. The lower ring is undated, and it is not a date for the Tree. The upper work is overlay, from the Hands years or from late in the Walking years, and which of those it is remains argued. The Hands years are the years when a town could Turn under its own Tree. The Walking years are the older years, when Turning meant the walk to the one Tree. **Tithsael** *(tith-SAYL)* keeps the present night. The days' walk from Maiethlir is not what this hill is for. The grove, and the First Seat, stay in the Motherwood.

The hill is not Thaeloren, the origin Tree in the Motherwood, and it does not want to be. On a clear Eolthael you can see a smear of Motherwood weather from the top, which is why later people built a shrine. If the First Hand is there, her hand at the origin, showing in that weather, a seeing-place here can be talked into hers. The lower work is a ring, not quite a circle and not quite a wall, of stones set to make a person turn as they walk, so that you look out. That looking-out is the argument the Watching needs. The Watchers keep the Tree and say it was already seeing, and that a mother-name came after the seeing. Each layer on this hill is a story laid over a seeing. The upper work is cut from a kinder quarry, stone that is not the ring's own. The steps do not match the ring's tread, and a socket once held a door-beam. A Tender's mark, the sign a tree-warden pecks to bless a place, is pecked over a withheld pattern. The roof is gone, and the mark is still there if you know to rub the lichen. Wardens call the emptying respect, and Watchers call it finally.

**At the table**

- **Want** The night kept, a colour-ribbon taken off the socket, and a year kept from being cut into the ring.
- **Have** A ring-walk, four out-looks, an emptied shrine, and a pecked mark.
- **Fear** A roof put back on, a congregation forming, and a clerk with a chisel.
- **Live conflict** The Watchers' seeing, the wardens' emptied respect, and a roll that wants the hill written down as a person.
- **Enters a scene** A chisel, a ribbon, a child who wants a door that is not a slight, and rain on the open socket.
- **How this site talks** As a night and a refusal, in Tithsael's slate, not in a Speaker's notice.

Clockwise is the local habit of the ring-walk, and a Watcher will not tell you why. A warden will tell you it is Turning-Week in stone, the week of the Turning copied into a direction, which is overlay so confident it sounds like folklore. Four gaps are wider than the rest. One faces the smear of the origin, one faces the Old Crossing weather, and two face nothing a present map cares about. Nobody says what those two face. What is left of the shrine is steps, a beam-socket, and no roof. In Eolthael someone ties a cloth ribbon on the open socket, and by Leddorn, the later month when contracts are reckoned, it is gone. Tithsael takes the ribbon off if it is a colour-ribbon, a ribbon tied for a colour a Tree Gives, and leaves a hug-ribbon, tied for the hug that Keeps a child and gives no colour. That is how a Watcher does a kindness the Seat will not understand. Pilgrims say the wood sent the ribbon, or that it fell from the origin and landed on this hill. It is cloth that someone on the hill ties, and they will not say why they chose a colour, and the origin did not drop it. A person can untie it and hold it, and it is a strip of cloth that does not Speak and does not cast anything. You can feel both cuts if you put a thumb on the pecked mark, the warden's sign and the older pattern under it. Tithsael keeps the night not as a service but as a seeing. People come with a cough, a question, or a child who was not Held and was not Given, neither kept by a Tree nor given anything by one, and who wants a hill that does not owe them a name.

| Watchers | The Before, kept | That a night can become a congregation |
| Tree-wardens | A shrine they emptied politely | That emptying is an admission the ring was not theirs |
| Threnmaieth clerks | A hill that should be on a roll, a list of persons | That a seeing is not a person, and so cannot be entered |

The hill path is ordinary, and you climb it in a morning. Once you are in the ring, clockwise is the habit. The emptied shrine steps are the later tread, and they do not match the ring. A warden will call these steps the door, and a Watcher will call them overlay. Between the lower stones and the hill, on the sea-gap side, there is a squeeze a small person can do and a mainlander can do with a scraped back. It may be a drainage-gap or a seeing-gap. It goes a body-length, opens on one out-look from underneath, and there is no temple-crypt. Tithsael lets you sit the night if you did not bring a colour-ribbon and did not bring a year to cut.

A clerk wants a Cut-year cut into the ring. Tithsael will not let the chisel touch, and a party can stand with either side. A warden wants a roof put back on the socket, and calls that respect. Watchers call a roof the start of a congregation. A colour-ribbon shows up on the socket in Eolthael, and Tithsael takes that one off, while a hug-ribbon is left where it was tied. Maiethvael will try to furnish the night, to bring oil and a lamp, without putting anyone on a list, and furnishing a night is already a bill. The smear of the origin shows only on a clear Eolthael. The other two out-looks show themselves in any weather, which is worse for a pilgrim who came wanting a meaning.

You might find a second withheld cut under the Tender's mark, a pot-ring of ordinary clay, a view of the origin that makes a pilgrim weep and a Watcher shrug, a scratch under the ring that is not a letter, and lichen that grows only on the overlay quarry. A visitor can see a stone circle, a ruined shrine, and a night-watch. From that they can guess two builds, a later overlay, and a faith that will not let the first cutting of living wood be the year the count begins. Nobody says why two gaps face nothing on a present map, or what seeing meant before it meant a mother. This hill holds no gate of the Closed Seat. You can be hired to stop a clerk, or to put a roof back, or to take a colour-ribbon off, or to rub the pecked mark and say which hand was first, and the hill will not tell you which hand that was.

*Tithsael's night-slate, one line a week, kept under a stone that is not in the ring. It is a watch kept in writing, not a service.*

> *Ribbon off. Clerk came with a year; I would not let the chisel touch. A child asked if the hug lives here. I said the hill does not Give and does not Keep. The child said that was a better door than a slight. I did not correct them. The origin was a smear. The sea-gap was weather. The other two were themselves. If you are a Speaker, this is not a colour. If you are Limrae's child, this is not a row. If you are Hildal's child, this is not a seat. I am going to sleep on the inward step of a different stone. The slate shows that Tithsael turned the clerk's chisel away, heard the child call the hill a better door than a slight, and did not correct that. It refuses a Speaker's colour, refuses to be a row for Limrae's child, and refuses to be a seat for Hildal's child. Then Tithsael walks off to a different stone and does not take the hill along.

## The Dry Stair

In the dry east of Saelthael, the Rain-Shadow, where the towns sit on wells, a rise above a well-town holds a flight of steps cut into the rock. They climb to a door-stone and a basin that has not held water in any summer a living person will swear to. Everyday speech calls it the Dry Stair. Folk call it the stair that does not climb to a Tree, and they say that both as a joke and as a warning. The stair is undated, and it is not the Tree's original temple, not a first shrine of the fox-born who keep hearths in this dry country, not a ruin left by one born people ruling the rise, and not Ornsael's hill. Ornsael is the other well-town, farther into the dry, and its drink is a well. Larbril is the city. This stair is the climb, and the basin stays dry.

The Rain-Shadow still walks west after the core stopped. The core is the country that used to walk that road and then stopped, and Saelthael's dry east did not stop, so the well-towns still send people toward a Tree. This stair does not join that walk. It climbs up, only as far as the rise, to nothing a Hands-child is trained to want. A Hands-child is a child of the present years, raised to expect living wood at the end of a climb like this, and the top of these steps is bare stone. The steps are worn in the middle, because people have gone up for a long time. At the top there is a slab with a threshold cut into it, standing like a door with no wall, and behind it a basin pecked into the rock that is dry. Taken-In who know wells, the people a dry town asks when a hole in rock will not hold, say the rock was never a good cistern, because the pecking is wrong for holding. Old Ways visitors on a visiting week leave a first-meal at the threshold and do not call the slab a guest. Children dare the last step and come down louder than they went up.

A later warden planted a slip in the upper basin, a cutting of living wood carried in a pot, so the stair would have a point. The slip died, and the Seat did not send a second. What is left is a thumb of dead wood in a crack at the lip. A person can work it free and hold it, and it still will not green and will not take in a pot, and what they are holding is not a scion and not a colour. People say the stair refused a mother. The rock was dry, the pecking was wrong for water, and the slip died of that. The refusal is the story they prefer to the dirt. Saelthael already knows about dirt: the soil is why a slip dies on this rise. Dead wood is not a Tree.

**At the table**

- **Want** A dare finished, a second slip stopped before it is planted, and a missed child sat with, without the town calling that child Kept.
- **Have** A worn flight of steps, a door-stone, a dry basin, and a well-town below that is not Ornsael.
- **Fear** Outsiders claiming the rise as a Fox shrine, a clerk copying the scratches on the risers, and a warden who comes to plant again.
- **Live conflict** The joke, the shame, and a west-road that still goes.
- **Enters a scene** A slip in a pot, a child on the last step, a year rubbed into a riser, and night on the slab.
- **How this site talks** As a climb and a dare, in a child's count, not in a warden's notice.

The steps are countable, and the count changes if you ask three children. One step is broken, and people skip it and call the skip luck, or a slight, or nothing, without agreeing which. The door-stone is a threshold with no wall and nothing over it, so you can stand in it and be on both sides of a door that does not enclose anything. Door-Keepers, who officiate at a real door and at the meal that makes a guest, will not officiate when they have come this far west, though they will eat if a bowl is there. The pecking of the basin is the wrong shape for a well and the right size for a pot, which is why the warden tried a slip there. A ring of salt-white sits in the pecking. It is not ash brought from the Motherwood, and it is not a colour. The well-town below is not Ornsael. It is a lesser walk-hold, a well-town that still sends its people west. The stair is the town's dare and its shame at once: visitors ask where the Tree is, the town points west, and then someone laughs at the stair. A few steps have a scratch that might be a person. Watchers would not date them. A Threnmaieth clerk, the kind who copies a mark onto a record, would want them copied. The town rubs them with a thumb and does not read them aloud.

The climb takes a quarter-hour, whether you skip the broken step or not. Behind the basin a crawl starts where the pecking is wrong. It is a slit a hand can disappear into, and then a shoulder, and it opens on a second basin, lower in the rise, also dry, with the same salt-white and no hole a Tree was set in. You enter the stair from the town's shame. Visitors ask where the Tree is, the town points west, someone laughs, and then someone dares you.

A warden with a pot has another slip, because the last one died, and still wants the stair to have a point. The well-town's warden is unnamed, and is not Theisva of Ornsael. A clerk wants the scratches on the risers copied, while the town rubs them with a thumb and does not read them, and a party can stand with either. A child who missed Eolthael is unTurned, and not Kept. Kept is the name for a child the leaves closed around and gave no colour, and the town will not put that name on a miss. Someone will ask you to sit the door-stone overnight so the child has a door that is not a slight and not a Tree. Fox-of-the-Sands hearths want no outsiders claiming the slab as a Fox shrine, and outsiders will try. At night the last step is louder going down than it was going up, and the wind in the basin is not water.

You might find that the salt-white is mineral, or ash of ordinary brush, or something the town does not have a word for. You might find the second basin, a west-road name scratched over an older scratch, and pecking-tool marks that are the wrong angle for a cistern on purpose. A visitor can see the stairs, a dry fountain, and a dare. From that they can guess a failed planting, a habit of doors that have no grove behind them, and a town that walks west because this place is not a Tree. Nobody says what the basin was for, or why the pecking is the wrong angle on purpose.

The count shows that the town climbs the stair to know the difference between a dare and a mile-shrine, one of the ribboned climbs on the same west road. The Road-hand who copied it wrote a dare down as if it were a mile.

## Ndenjoo

Ndenjoo *(nden-JOH)* is a hill-hall on Kumbaan, the Sundering Isle, and the own-name comes from the isle register, while sailors say **the Feeding Hill**. The valley comes here on the bright nights, and what they come to is a table, because there is no Turning Tree.

Ndenjoo is one of the Paps, a green hill honeycombed underneath, with pasture and standing-stones on the turf and the life below. It is the hall the valley already knows. **Njunda** *(NJOON-dah)* opens it because it was their grandmother's, and because it still fits the crowd. That keeping rests on reputation, not on an office. When they die, the valley will come to the next hall that still sets the extra place. The storm-beach is a walk down, not a quay. **Soonke** *(SOON-keh)* sits that stretch of sand and does not swim out. When a wreck comes in with living people on it, they walk those people uphill, and the first night is spent as guest at the hall, not on the sand.

This is an ordinary home that happens to be nearly impossible to reach. The uncanniness is the distance, and the hands you can see without bodies. The home is not a paradise, and it is not a monster-haunt. Mainland noon-faiths are loud here the way a guest who will not sit is loud. Kumbaan never had the pilgrim walk, and it never had a Tree. What the hill carries from the wall is people who arrived and did not leave, and people who want to leave and cannot book a thinning. A thinning is a moon the sitters know, not a ferry.

**At the table**

- **Want** An extra place laid. A second night that makes kin. A guest who can sleep the day.
- **Have** A hall under the turf. Unseen hands. A path down to Soonke's sand.
- **Fear** A wreck that becomes a crowd. A kin who will not leave. A noon that will not stay quiet.
- **Live conflict** Njunda seats; Soonke only decides alive; Saalo wants a crossing out; Nolas has sat twice.
- **Enters a scene** A wreck walked uphill tonight; the hands stop; a guest loud at noon; a hull rumour.
- **How this place talks** A second night at the table. There is no warden, no notice, and no Tree.

The hill is why anyone stayed. The hall is a long room under the turf, with lamps that face the table and not the sea. On the surface there are terrace-gardens, a standing-stone the children dare, and goats. Below that are the hearths, the feast-board, and the extra place, set slightly back from the rest. Water is a hill-spring and caught rain. Food is the terrace, the shallows when the wall is kind, and whatever the unseen hands fetch, so that a poor house still sets a table that looks like more than its means. Fuel is peat, and whatever a wreck sometimes gives the hall. There is no lot, no counted hall, and no stiller. There are no walls. The boundary is the last path-cut toward the sand, and the storm. A slip planted here would die. Dead wood is not a Tree, and no one in this valley is waiting for a colour. A few dozen hearths live under the one Pap, and they come to the one hall.

Life is under the hill, so you do not walk through a town. You walk into a Pap and come out at a board, where the table is the civic heart and the sacred heart at once, because the rite is supper. The extra place sits slightly back, not at the end, and it is laid at every meal that matters, not only at the feast, so that omitting it is the one impiety. Unseen hands fetch and pour while people sing, and nobody reads a colour or recites a set of terms. A child of the house learns the work by carrying dishes, and by being told not to sit there. When the moon is down, people sleep in the cool day-rooms, because daylight is a Sabbath of sleep and not a fallen hour, and a mainlander who is loud at noon is a problem the whole hall feels. The path to the sand is Soonke's stretch, with a hooded lamp that does not call ships, so the living go up and the dead go above the tide. The standing-stone on the turf is a dare rather than a mile-shrine, and there are no ribbons on it. Visitors from the core will try to climb it as if it owed them a leftover, and it does not.

On one night of the moon sailors call Yoltal, which is the worn mainland name for Turning-month, the hall eats the heel of the loaf first. Every other night the heel is last, or it goes to the goats. The valley says the hands like the crust when the mainland is loud. Njunda will not correct that, and will not say it is the reason. They will not say why, and a guest who asks is given more bread.

Homes here accommodate the becomings the isle actually has. A rare Struck is family news, not a class. A Yumboe is what you are because that is who lives here. Mixed mainland Kinds are guests if they crossed, and they do not grow quarters of their own.

Njunda keeps the table, and that is the whole job. They want the extra place laid, and they want **Nolas** *(NOH-las)* gone without the place being omitted. Soonke sits the shore on this stretch. They want the living walked uphill, and they want the lamp left unmistaken for a harbour. They will not seat anyone on the sand. **Saalo** *(SAH-lo)* is of the valley, and wants a crossing out to find **Waandi** *(WAHN-dee)*, who left two moons ago and sent no word. Saalo is a sibling, not a scholar of Trees. Nolas came off a Netstrand hull that tried the wall. They have sat twice, which makes them kin inside the hall, and they will not go back to a ship. The valley is tired. Staying hosts say the dead's names when they fill the extra place. Serving hosts do not say the names, and they still fill the place. The argument waits until after the dishes. There is no town-warden, no roll, no First Seat, and no Road-hands. The Open Table is the faith, and this is a hall that keeps it.

You cannot book a passage to Ndenjoo. You are on the sand, or you are not. Soonke decides whether you are alive to be walked up, and Njunda decides whether you are kin. The second sitting is how you belong on the isle, and that belonging does not travel back to a mainland watch. A road-word, the mainland custom of being vouched for by a name, a last hearth, and someone who stands, still helps you leave a Strandoren quay before you sail. On the hill, nobody asks for it. The shore-sitters leave the arrived where the beach put them. A thinning is a moon the sitters know, not a ferry. Most hulls wreck, turn, or do not return, and the sitters do not send anyone back. Saalo is waiting anyway.

What can come up the hill is a wreck walked uphill tonight, the people wet and breathing and nobody asking them for a colour, so the hall has to open on a first night when the extra place was already set and now there is also a living stranger. The hands stop when a child sat in the extra place, or the place was used as a spare stool, or a Serving host and a Staying host argued during the pour. The valley is hungry, and fixing it is manners, not a miracle. Noon-noise is Nolas, who will not sleep the day. The noise is mainland loud, and the hill feels it. A hull rumour is someone on the sand who saw a sail. Soonke will not swim out to it, Saalo will want to go down, and Njunda will want the hall kept. A warden who arrives is still a guest. There is no Tree to tend. The argument about a tenth-year week belongs to the mainland, not to this valley.

This season, Njunda wants Nolas to leave as kin, not as a slight, and wants the extra place to stay laid. Soonke wants this wreck off the hill before the next loud night, and wants the lamp still hooded. Saalo wants a crossing toward Waandi, or a word that Waandi sat a second night somewhere that is not here. Nolas wants the hall as a hearth. They have been counted, and they will not be a passenger again.

*Said to the extra place before the hands arrive, at Ndenjoo on a bright night. Children are taught it. Njunda added the second line after Nolas sat twice.*

> *We have counted you. Sit. If you have a name we have not learned it yet; if you have a colour we have no tree to read it.*
>
> *If you have sat twice, you are of the table. The table is not a ship. Sleep when the moon is down. The sand does not owe you a second hull.*

It is a grace said over a meal. Sitting twice makes a guest into kin, and that is why the hall fills. A sailor who copies only the first two sentences will sell them on a quay as a proverb.

## The Mill-hold

The Mill-hold is a Hands-town whose Hand is standing in water. A Hand is a town's grafted Tree, a cutting of living wood that took and now Turns the children at home, and everyday speech says *the Mill-hold*, while travellers who only saw the drain say *the culvert*. It is not Orenbren's capital, which is Rothallo, and it is not Brenthael. The three corner seats stay Eolvaeth, Orentel, and Maiethlir. A few hundred hearths sit around one mill-race and one canopy.

West of the Rain-Wall, on Maiethorn's four-season core, a mill-town grew around a graft that took beside a race, so the town has bread and a Hand in the same water. For a generation they Turned at home. The Near Mile goes on past Brenthael and past this hold. Children here were told the road went to the Motherwood, and most Hands-children did not keep walking. The culvert choked this Liren. Liren is the thaw, the month when water runs again, and the culvert is the drain that keeps spring-water off the roots. The mill-race stayed full, and the Hand stood in it. What the wardens call root blight is ordinary horticulture, waterlogged soil and crowded roots and a drain someone assumed someone else had cleared, and it is this year rather than a curse or a sermon.

Unsound wood spends the week. A warden who lets a cohort stand under a Tree they know cannot Turn has made those children unTurned, and a later summer does not repair this week. The civic crisis is wearing a child's summer, which is the only summer the Given-door will open. The town's daily work is meeting need, so the neighbour's week returns, the week of walking to someone else's Tree. Devotion belongs to people whose Hand still Speaks. Nobody books a blight.

**At the table**

- **Want** A neighbour's week that still has room, and a mill that still turns after the culvert is opened.
- **Have** A neighbour-slate, a shovel at the drain, and Brenthael's canopy two days grove-ward.
- **Fear** A child stood under unsound wood, Brenthael's extra-mile week too full, and a second cut taken from this Hand while it is sick.
- **Live conflict** Milsun's drain against Talen's race against Thurrei's wood.
- **Enters a scene** A bough coming down, mill-foam on the lip, a slate with one name too many, or a child already in the square.
- **How this square talks** A neighbour-slate and a shovel. Two working people, and a Road-hand who came for the wood, not for the children.

Counted from the grove, the Third Hearth is three days out, Brenthael is one day further, and the Mill-hold is two days past Brenthael's boughs. Six days out, still lodging-country, and still not a throne. Brenthael is the neighbour Hand, and its Tree is sound. Fair weather is a promise the culvert did not keep. A side-cut of living water feeds the race, and the same cut was supposed to take flood off the square. The oldest houses face the Tree. The mill-house and the race sit on the lower side, where the water wants to be. No walls stand here. The boundary is the last mill-share and the ranging. Water for people is not the crisis. There is drink, and there is too much of it in the wrong place. Food is mill-grain, terrace gardens, and pigs on mast-rights older than the lodging-guild. Fuel is coppice the Two-Bodied also range. A travelling stiller, one of the Stilled who hold a wound or a moment still, is a Near-Mile luck, not a resident of the hold.

The Tree at the centre is mature enough that grandparents Turned under it, and young enough, for the Motherland, that someone still remembers the year the road stopped being required, though the leaves this Hale-month are late and a little colourless at the edges. Under the square the culvert is brick and timber older than the mill-house's present roof, with climbing-scuff on the lip from children who have heard a stone-story, shovels, a smell of wet bark, and mill-foam that holds a day after the race is drawn down. The mill-race is the town's bread and its name, so opening the culvert to save the Hand lowers the race, and Harvest is not theoretical. The neighbour-slate is chalked on the mill-door rather than on a warden's lath, and it carries the names of this year's ten-year-olds and which Brenthael household has room, Talen's hand on the recto and Meirim's marks on the verso when they arrive. The upper room used to sleep the extra mile when people were walking in toward the grove, and this Hale-month it sleeps the necessity-column going the same way for a different reason, so it is not a merchant-loft yet. The greens are potted and hauled up off the wet, because there is no lush lot, and the Taken-In here are neighbours whose planting, Milsun's, sits above the race on purpose. The ranging stays at the edge, where mixed Kinds work and no Kind-flag hangs on the mill, and the far shed is Returned work downwind of the drink and the grain, with no infirmary.

This is Maiethorn, Hands-country, where the town Turned its children at home and most of them did not keep walking past the boughs. The Mile still goes on, and this year the wood is sick, so the children walk in, toward Brenthael, while a Road-hand is here with earth rather than with a colour. A Hand can fail, and that failure is weather, not the end of the age. The Seat will call thin soil, the mill will call a race, and Talen will call a date in Eolthael. All three are looking at the same square.

Mill-foam on the drain-lip keeps last Eolthael's colour a day longer than the water does. Milsun will not say why. Talen moves the foam with a stick, and Thurrei wipes it off a pot-wrap and does not write it down. A miller still takes a share of the grain. The square does not turn on that miller unless you ask whose race was kept full. When the race is drawn down, the mill-share sets the stones and leaves one measure of last year's grain on the lip until the water comes back. They will not say why.

The talk in the square is about the drain and the slate. **Talen** *(TAH-len)* is town-warden. They Speak the conservative way, so the office is a practice and not a people Talen was born. Talen will not stand a child under unsound wood, and wants the mill to keep turning through Harvest. The same water that has to come off the roots is the town's bread. The document of this square is the neighbour-slate, not a notice about a stone. **Milsun** *(MIL-sun)* is Taken-In, on the drain and the planting. Milsun has been saying the culvert would choke since last Tally, the autumn count, and wants the mill-family to stop taking the Tree's turn as if plenty were a hymn. A sister's child is on this week's slate. Milsun will not follow you home. Home is wet if the Hand dies, and dry if the race does. **Thurrei** *(thur-RAY)* is a Road-hand, here for the wood. Thurrei brings living earth and the watering-words, and a report to the Seat if the scion sickens, and will not cut a second without leave. The queue that would replace this Hand is the same queue that has not yet given the three hamlets past the ford a first Tree. Thurrei knows that sentence and will not say *queue*. What Thurrei wants is one night when no one asks about the hamlets. **Meirim** *(may-REEM)* of Brenthael is the neighbour-host. Meirim wants the Third Hearth's stone down for an extra-mile crowd, and now has a necessity-column walking in from the other side. Same square, two crowds. Meirim is not this town's warden. The square that used to watch children Turn has to walk grove-ward this year and come back. Orenbren lodging-custom keeps hospitality as rent in Eolthael. A Hands-town that sends its children inward looks, from a bed-guild, like failure. Talen hears a date.

What arrives while someone is already talking is a bough coming down, the wood wet and the Hale-month crowd under it, or the culvert at first light, when Milsun digs and the race drops and a mill-hand is already there with a count of the turns the mill lost, or a slate with one name too many, because Brenthael's verso says the beds are full and a child is still ten this week and the Given-door is still one week, or a child already in the square at dawn, dressed for a fall Talen will not Speak. Unsound wood does not become a hug because someone is hopeful. Thurrei's pot in the upper room smells of earth. A second cut would be a fight with the leave, not a kindness.

*A neighbour-slate, chalked on the mill-door in Hale-month, Cut-year 387. Talen's hand is on the recto. Meirim's marks go on the verso when they come. The blight is not named.*

> *Ten this week: eight. Brenthael can take six if the extra mile sleeps in the loft and not the square. Two names left. I will not invent a seventh bed.*
>
> *The race is drawn at dawn so the lip can be opened. Grain after. Colour is not this door's work.*
>
> *A pot is upstairs. It is not a pilgrim. It is not a child. I can tell. I will not always say which of the eight is Milsun's kin.*

Talen is doing a week, and will not file a blight. Milsun will read the slate as a slight on the drain. Thurrei will already have walked the roots.

## The First Bowl

The First Bowl is a guest-grove. An old green kept host-rights here before any leave was written, and the right is to feed a guest. A young Hand stands in the green now, cut from Harrow's Green after Harrow's took, acting on the origin's behalf, and married into the ground after the live front had a canopy of its own. Everyday speech calls the place *the First Bowl*, which is what you do before you cut, tend, or Speak. The folk hearth-name is *Lonasir* *(lo-NAH-seer)*. The Seat still writes *a later planting in the Far Yield*, and it does not use the hearth-name. This grove is not Vaelhesk's capital, because the land is the seat and Vaelhesk names no city. It is not Vaelun, not a second Harrow, and not a sick Tree. The Mill-hold, in Orenbren, is the sick Tree. A few dozen houses sit around one old green and one young guest.

The vale is a pocket on Heskoren, in the Far Yield, and what the pocket still has is an old green, ancestor-doors, and first meals. The Mother's wood stands here as a guest. A Cutting-leave, the Seat's paper for a cut, is a claiming if it arrives without the meal. Door-Keepers walk here as Kin-speakers. They did not carry the scion, they do not confirm colours, and the thing they argue is who the host is. The cut came from Harrow's after Harrow's took. What stands here now is a healthy Hand, and it was not a first Tree for the three hamlets past the ford. The hamlets can see that from the ford even when they cannot see this vale. The wood went past them into Yield-land. Brenod calls it the queue. Vaelun calls it a guest that should have sat at their green. Ornath has looked at the other road. None of those kitchens is this grove. The village's daily work is host-rights, and the right is the meal. Devotion is a misreading visitors bring, because they come looking for a chapel. Nobody books a first bowl. A Road-hand's necessity leaked through once, and it leaked through in a pot.

**At the table**

- **Want** The bowl before the colour. A child who can be kin and Given without one name eating the other.
- **Have** An old green, a young guest, a bowl, two people who both love the wood.
- **Fear** A warden who will not sit. A leave that treats the meal as delay. Vaethorn calling refusal deprivation.
- **Live conflict** Delvor's bowl against Vilraet's Speaking against Brudu's morning water.
- **Enters a scene** The bowl set out; a pot that has not sat; a child waiting in the rain; Nethiro from Vaelun with a question about the heel.
- **How this square talks** Two settings of one bowl. No slate. The leave does not mention the meal.

The vale is a day's walk from Vaelun, sometimes two, and the walk goes away from Harrow's, deeper into the Yield rather than back along the Rise-water. You do not pass Harrow's square to reach it unless you are carrying wood from there. The weather is sheltered. People grow rye, oats, cabbage, and hay, with apples on the warm slope and pigs on woodland mast under local rights. The kind season is short: one brilliant dry day, and then rain. A clear day is when everything moves at once, so Leaf-Fall, the week the children stand under the Hand, and a hinge-walk can land on the same dry hours. Water is a vale-beck and a planting-ditch. It is not a named river, and it is not Ornsael's well. The fight over a morning bucket is host against guest in the same green, not a town emptying itself for drink. The houses sit dispersed around the green. You walk from hearth to grove, and you do not walk through a town. There are no walls, and the boundary is where the old green ends and the moor begins.

The old green is Taken-In ground, with ancestor-doors and a first meal older than a Cut-year, and it is Brudu's planting, so the hearths are mixed Kinds and the green is not a Kind-quarter. The guest is a young Tree at the green's heart, Spoken for a few years, not mature the way Harrow's is and not unsound, with leaves that take colour. Vilraet Speaks when the bowl has gone around, or tries to. The bowl itself is wood, darkened by use, set on a stump that was never a mile-shrine, and some years the rim carries two chalk-marks, *sat* and *cut*, though most years only one of those marks is honest. The door-path goes water, then hill, then stone, and a guest who reverses it is corrected without a sermon, because the path is not a procession-route for Leaf-Fall even when the crowd wants the boughs and the two routes cross in the mud. The warden-hearth is new, relatively, and practice-first, with its door facing the guest, which is where Vilraet lives, and a pot may sleep in the loft when a Road-hand is passing through with someone else's wood. At the vale-edge the Two-Bodied work the same woods the doors walk, with no Kind-flag, and downwind the far shed keeps Returned work on a different door from the ancestor-meal.

The green was a door before it was a square, and the children were already the land's. A household can refuse a neighbour's week at a Motherfaith Tree on purpose. The guest-Hand Speaks in some years. Some of the ten-year-olds stand here, some still walk to Harrow's, and some do not walk. The Harrow-sentence is a luck this vale did not inherit by asking, and the wood arrived anyway. Vaethorn's gospel is spoken on many hearths, and this square lives in Vaelhesk custom. A waiting-gospel that calls refusal deprivation is the enemy of the meal. The hamlets' cup asks whether to send, and this bowl asks whether to sit. It is the same water-country, and it is a different question.

The first-meal bread is always a little sour, and Delvor says that is how you know the grove sat down. Vilraet will not eat the heel. Brudu puts a pinch from another hill in the bowl, and will not say which door it came from. After the last person has gone in, a rye-straw is laid on the door-path's first stone and left until first light. The First Bowl will not say why.

Two claimants share the green with one tender, and this sharing does not close the unwritten square at the end of the chapter. **Delvor** *(DEL-vor)* of Lonasir is the Kin-speaker. They ask about the bowl before they ask about a leave. This season they want a particular Struck housed, kin-named at the grove-door rather than walked to a Tree to be filed as late. They may refuse a neighbour's week on purpose for a child they believe is already the hill's. They will tend the guest as host, and they will not carry a scion. **Vilraet** *(vil-RAYT)* is town-warden of the guest. The practice comes first, and the office is not a Kind. They want the week Spoken so a marriage in the vale can be witnessed this Eolthael without a walk to Harrow's. The year the wood arrived, Vilraet did not sit, or sat late, and Delvor has not forgotten. The leave in the hearth does not mention the meal. **Brudu** *(BROO-doo)* is Taken-In, and the old planting. They want the morning water before the guest's pot. They will not follow you home, because home is the green that was here first. They are tired of being asked if this is Taken-In country. The question is the slight. **Nethiro** *(neth-EE-ro)* of Vaelun is a Door-Keeper's cousin, and walks here when the bowl is set. Vaelun's work is still host-rights at a kitchen that refused the neighbour's week, and this grove is the later planting that kitchen points at. A Road-hand comes through sometimes, and either that hand planted or their circuit did. They are told to eat first, and if they do not, they are a thief for a season. The Seat's leave still says *a later planting in the Far Yield.* Clerks who learn *Lonasir* have learned more than the minute wanted them to.

What enters is the bowl set out, before Leaf-Fall or before a pot is opened or before Vilraet Speaks. If the bowl goes around, they sit. If it does not, the child still stands, or does not, according to the hearth and not according to the leave. A pot that has not sat brings an earth-smell, a Road-hand on a clock, and Delvor at the stump. A warden with a pot is a guest or a thief, depending on whether they sat down. A child waits in the rain while two honest claims take the dry hour. The colour and the kin-name both want to go first. A hinge-walk collides with the crowd when someone reverses the door-path and is corrected without a sermon. A Struck the vale will not house stands where Delvor named them kin, with the courtesy and without a pillar, and standing-for is still civic.

*Said at the stump, not written. One voice is Delvor's, one voice is Vilraet's, and the bowl is the same.*

> *Sit. Then Speak. The wood is a guest. I will not let a colour go first in a house that fed it.*
>
> *The week is one week. If the bowl lasts the rain I still have a fall to name. I have a Tree. I did not invent it.*

The three voices disagree, and nobody settles it. At dawn someone still decides whether to lift the bowl, wake the child, and take the colour or the door-path first. A third line, if Brudu is there, is only: *The planting drinks at first light. The guest can wait until the bowl.*

## Nelath

Nelath *(NEL-ath)* is the road-end Tree-town, the place where the walk stopped, so the square is the last mile. Mile-folk say **the spur** and mean the road, then use the town's name once they are in it. It is not a capital of Orenbren, and it is not the First Seat. Rothallo is the capital. The three corner seats stay Eolvaeth, Orentel, and Maiethlir. The warden is Raetoth, and the neighbour who argues with that warden is Lithsur. This town is not Harrow's Green, not Brenthael, and not the Mill-hold. It is one square at the end of a spur, with fewer hearths than the Mill-hold.

West of the Rain-Wall, in Orenbren lodging-country, a graft took where a side-day of the Near Mile used to end. The main Mile still goes on to the Third Hearth, Brenthael, and the Mill-hold. This track leaves that road and does not rejoin it. When Hands made the origin-walk optional, this town did not leave the feeling in a sentence. They brought the next stone in. The boughs closed what had been the day after. Many Hands-roads end at a canopy while a cart-track continues to a ford, a mill, or a neighbour. That sentence is true at Harrow's, and it is true at the Mill-hold, whose children can still walk grove-ward. It is not true here. Beyond these boughs there is no ford. There is a scar: a ditch, thorns, and the width of a road that is no longer a road. The town's daily work is being the place where the walk stopped. A pilgrim may leave a ribbon. A family in need, if one comes, turns around and uses the Mile. Nobody books a spur that cannot be walked through.

**At the table**

- **Want** The stone left in the square, the scar left closed, and a bed that is not being asked to be a next day.
- **Have** A sound Hand, a stone that used to be a further day, an upper room, and a ditch of thorns where the road was.
- **Fear** The stone hauled back onto the scar, a child told the ditch is a mile, and a slate that files the name over the door.
- **Live conflict** Raetoth keeps the mile in the square. Lithsur wants the name back on a day of walking.
- **Enters a scene** A rope on the stone, someone coming out of the scar, or a Road-hand asking which bed is the next day.
- **How this square talks** A cistern-chalk and a neighbour. Two people. The Mile is a different road.

The junction is on the Near Mile, a day short of the Third Hearth, about two days from Thaeloren in fair weather. From the junction the spur is one day, and then the canopy. You do not pass Thilim, or Meirim, or the culvert. The Low Wall is a different fold, closer to the wood, and has no Hand. The graft was planted beside the old day-mark because living earth and a child want a drink. The drink for people is a cistern by the warden-hearth, and the stone is not the well. Settlement nucleated around the canopy and then stopped, because there was nowhere further to put a house. The oldest houses face the Tree, the spur comes in on one side and becomes the square, and no walls stand here. The boundary is the last garden and the scar. Food is the spur's gardens, a pig on a mast-right, and whatever a Mile-cart leaves when someone is willing to turn around. Fuel is coppice. A travelling stiller is a Near-Mile luck, not a resident, and the Taken-In who keep the green around the Hand are neighbours, not a nation.

The Tree at the centre is a mature Hand, old enough that grandparents Turned under it, and the road's requirement ended in the living memory of the deathless and in stories for everyone else, so the wood is sound and this is not a sick year. Leaf-Fall is held in the square because the square is the mile. The stone that used to stand a day further on now sits in that square, with a flat top, climbing-scuff, a ribbon some years, and a name cut in it. **Raetoth** *(RAY-toth)* will not read the name aloud. **Lithsur** *(LITH-sur)* will, and wants the stone back on the scar so the name is a day's mark again and not a festival climb. The cistern by the warden-hearth is the day's drink, and it is not a mile-shrine and not Seine's pot at the Third Hearth, while the stone is not the drink either, even though a notch on the stone still holds a little rain after the square has dried. Raetoth calls that weather, and Lithsur calls it the day. Beyond the boughs the scar is thorn, ditch, and a line you can follow for a stone's throw before it closes, with no cart-track and no neighbour's canopy, so people who try it come back scratched and thirsty while the water-notch stays on the stone, which is here. The upper room has too many beds for the trade the town has now, because it slept the day that was pulled in. It is let, and it is not a bank. A name is cut over its door that no one reads aloud, and no lodging-slate has copied it, so when a Road-hand asks which bed is the next day, Raetoth will not answer that question with a bed. Around the Hand the greens are enough to live from and not a ticketed lot, the ranging stays at the edge where mixed Kinds live and work and no Kind-flag is hung, and the far shed is Returned work downwind of the cistern, and it is not an infirmary.

Children Turn at Nelath, because the walk stopped and the square is the last mile, and the road ends at the boughs. Someone still walks the scar and will not call it closed. A Road-hand still treats the upper room as if a pot were going further, and no Tree failed this year. The Seat will call the requirement over, Raetoth will call the mile the square, and Lithsur will call a name that used to be a day. All three are looking at one stone. The rain-notch stays wet, and a name is cut over the door of the upper room. Nelath will not say why the notch stays wet, or whose name that is. A clerk who copies either one has already misunderstood the town.

Raetoth is town-warden, practice-first and not a Kind. They Speak the fall at home, and want the stone left in the square and the scar left closed. A neighbour says *Raetoth*, a stranger hears *Raetoth of Nelath*, and there is no house-name. What Orenbren witnesses is who slept, and it does not list a family. Lithsur is a neighbour, a given name with no house behind it. Lithsur wants the stone back on the scar so the cut name is a day's walk again, will bring a rope in Eolthael, and will not call that rope a riot. Lithsur will not take a house-word to win the argument. The name over the upper-room door is not a person you can hire. A Road-hand passing the junction sometimes turns down the spur with earth or with nothing, sleeps upstairs, and asks for a road. They are not a chapter of this town. Orenbren is a compact of hearths. This town is one of them, and it is not the compact's crown. The First Seat is in the Motherwood, beside the country, and not in this square.

Hugs are less common on a sound Motherland Hand than elsewhere, but not rare, and a missed week still hurts. A family that needs a neighbour's Tree walks back to the Mile. That walk is a repair, not this town's brand. The road-mend stops at the boughs, the thorns stay, and the spur stops with them.

What enters is a rope on the stone, the Eolthael crowd in the square, Lithsur with the rope and Raetoth with the square, while the Hand is still going to Speak and the argument does not get a second week. Someone comes out of the scar through thorns, with no water, and with a story that the ditch went further than it does, and the cistern is the drink, while the stone is not. A Road-hand in the upper room asks which bed is the next day. The name over the door has an answer nobody will read, and the scar does not have one either. A child on the stone asks how far it is to the Tree. They are already under it. The climb is not a map.

*Chalk on the cistern in Hale-month, Cut-year 387, in Raetoth's hand. The name on the stone is not copied.*

> *The mile is the square. The scar is not a road. I will not put the stone back to make a day this town ended.*
>
> *Lithsur may say the name. They may not take the stone in order to say it. A rope is not a mile.*
>
> *Drink here. The notch on the stone is not the drink. The upper room has beds. The name over the door is not a slate. If you came for the Mile, you left it yesterday.*

The chalk shows that Raetoth will keep the stone in the square, and that Lithsur may say the name and may not take the stone in order to say it. Closing the road did not unsay the name. The Hand does not decide between them.
