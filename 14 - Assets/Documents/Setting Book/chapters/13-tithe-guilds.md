# Tithe-Infrastructure and Guilds


People call this work **the furnishing**, or simply **the greens and the halls**. There is no church-name set over the whole of it. In the Waiting Lands, which write themselves *Vaethorn*, the same work is **the gift-works**. On the Ledger Coast, written *Lestrand*, it is **the provision**. Under the Tallied Crown, written *Threnmaieth*, the clerks file it as **the counted channel**.

What the sector does is keep people whole *enough*, in public, and then stop at the gate. A Condition is a change a person comes to carry. A Tithe is the upkeep that change keeps demanding, the living ground or the hour or the new page a person has to have if the change is going to stay livable. The furnishing is the town's way of meeting that upkeep until the share runs out. After the share, the Slide will still sell a week. What the Slide rents is not falling, one week of green or of an outlet or of a new page, and the week is measured so the person does not quite get free.

The greens-keepers and the hall-keepers are parts of this sector. They keep a lot or an hour, and they are not houses with colleges of their own. The Stillers and the element-guilds remain craft-guilds. They stamp a licence where a use can hurt someone standing nearby. The Intake is the shed that does not face the street. A person newly Struck, changed outside the childhood ceremony and not yet safe to stand next to, is caught there before the town reaches for a rope.

## Shape of the sector

Furnishing and ticketing are two jobs. A town may put both under one roof, and they stay two jobs even then. Furnishing is the share of upkeep: a bed of living dirt, a scheduled hour, a library that is open. Ticketing is the licence for a hazardous act. The product of the official door is a person who can walk away until tomorrow. The Slide's product is a person who cannot. The town is glad of both. On an ordinary day the guilds pretend not to know the week-keepers next door. When an official gate closes on purpose, or a wilt or a Cry can be walked from that gate to the room that still opens, the pretence is over, because the two doors are then serving the same street, and the town has to say so.

A ticket names a competence, the practiced skill, and not the Condition itself. The people who might be asked for one are a large minority, about three in ten, the Stilled, the Far-Voiced, and the Answered, if they practise the use that can hurt a bystander. The papers actually issued are fewer than that, and fewer again on a frontier where a rope is as likely as a stool. Three guilds keep the papers, and they keep no shared list. A stiller cannot ask the element-guilds for every Answered person in the city. The state watches the charter, the guild's leave to operate, and it cannot ask for a list of this city's Far-Voiced. The arrangement makes its own friction. Guilds guard their monopolies. A ticket can start to be treated as a thing the guild holds, and an unlicensed practitioner threatens the charter as much as the street.

There is no Furnishing Seat, no college set over the other towns. A town keeps its own lot and its own hall. A deathless house, a house of people who do not die of age, endows its own stacks of books. Nothing in this sector tells a far commons how to water a bed.

## Who furnishes what

Each gate has a shape of *enough*, a moment when it closes, and a place the need goes when the gate has shut. The official door does not follow you past that line.

## Fellowships

A mill, a hull, and a road each have people who keep them through a season, and those people are the fellowships. The work is the ordinary keeping of that mill, that hull, or that road, and it does not licence a body. The licences already written stay where they are: the Stillers stamp a Gaze, the element-guilds stamp an element, the hall-keepers stamp a Voice-ticket, and the Intake catches a raw Struck person before the town reaches for a rope. A mill-share, a hull-wright, and a road-mend do not stamp any of those. They will not rent a week, which is the Slide's rental, or refinance a Term, which is the paper the Holding Desk refinances when the honest stamp has closed, or sell a standing, which is the witness the Standing Trade sells, a person paid to say they were there when a town was not.

A Gale-Answered miller, an Answered person whose element is the wind, can hold an element-guild ticket for that wind and still owe a share of grain. The ticket is the hazardous act, letting the element speak. The share is the season of work. A Tide-Answered wright can hold a ticket for the water and still be the person who walks the hull. A Root-Answered mender can hold a ticket for the stone and still be the person with the shovel. In each yard the fellowship is the season, and the ticket is only the act.

You are of a fellowship when you have kept the thing through a season and someone of it will say so. There is no Seat set over the other towns, and no apprenticeship a crown confirms. The fellowship does not ask what Kind a person was born, the ancestry they arrived with, and it does not keep a chapter of any born people. Kumbaan, the Sundering Isle beyond the storm-wall of weather, current, and reef, does not keep these three. No graft ever crossed to that isle, so there is no race to share, no quay that classes a hull, and no Mile to mend. They will not follow a Taken-In person home. That walk belongs to the greens-keepers. There is no town where the fellowship is the government, and there is no boss to send for.

**The mill-share.** A miller takes a share of the grain that comes through the mill, and the fellowship is the people who will say whose race this is, the mill-race that turns the stones, and whose share of that grain is owed.

At the Mill-hold the miller is not the person the square turns to, and the share is still taken. When the culvert is opened to keep water off the Hand, that town's Turning Tree, the race drops, and a mill-hand is already there with a count of lost turns. They do not Speak a colour, which would be naming a leaf in the square, and they do not carry a scion. A scion is living wood cut to plant a Tree, and that cart belongs to a Road-hand.

A Gale-house may coax the wind at a mill. If the person is Answered and lets the element speak, so that the wind answers the hands rather than the room, the coaxing is a ticket. The stones, the sacks, and the share belong to this fellowship even when nobody in the mill is Answered.

When the race is drawn down, the mill-share sets the stones and leaves one measure of last year's grain on the lip until the water comes back. They will not say why.

Threnmaieth would like that measure on a roll of persons. The mill-share keeps it with the grain. The Walled Book, the closed house that ranks the people who live inside its walls, can give those people a turn of work at a mill. That turn is the Book's assignment of a person. It is not a share of the grain.

**The hull-wrights.** They know whether a hull will take water, and they look at the bend, the caulk, and the line to know it.

The Hinge Shore decides whether a hull is a pilgrim, a pot, or cargo, and the Book-Hands stamp the paper that records the class. A Tide-Answered person may work the bend under an element-guild ticket. The hull-wrights do not classify the cargo, do not set the Hush-rate, the single charge on a crossing of the Old Crossing, and do not sell a witness who will say *I was there*. The Standing Trade sells that witness. This fellowship sells a hull that floats.

On the Down-Bank there is no hull yet. A traveler who rehearses a hull-class at an inn is early, and a wright who starts the classification there has left the craft and begun the Hinge Shore's job. At Denlad a tied hull is a hull waiting out weather. The wright will not call it a hold and will not charge a house-share for the tying. At Orentel the fellowship sits under the crane. It is not the White Note House, and it does not keep the berth.

Netstrand charters hulls. The charter is a crossing, and the wright is the person who will still walk the hull after the quote is written.

Before a new hull takes water, the hull-wrights walk it once empty, from stem to stern, and do not speak. They will not say why.

**The road-mend.** They fill a hole, clear a ford, and shore a culvert. The stretches they keep are the Near Mile, the inland walk through the Motherland, the spur that stops at Nelath, and the days along the Down-Bank.

They are not Road-hands. A Road-hand carries living wood, and a mender carries a shovel. They do not ask a traveler for a name, a last hearth, and who stands for them. Those questions belong to the watch, which holds a person until morning and does not keep the answers past dawn.

A person on a named walk is still vouched as a walker by the road, which means the road itself is what says they may walk it, and the mend is why the road is still there to walk. At Nelath the road ends at the boughs, so the mend stops with it. They do not cut the thorns back to make the spur go on. The town already decided where the road ends.

The Walled Book can give a turn on the road to a person who lives inside its walls. A mender on that page still knows the work. The assignment is the Book's.

At the first ford after a town, they leave the shovel standing in the bank overnight after a mend. They will not say why.

## The work people hire

When the work needs doing, people hire a craft. They do not enroll in a school of magic to get it.

A Daggerheart domain card is a job the town already pays someone to do, and the nine domains below are those jobs. The cards stay on the SRD, and this chapter does not copy them, because a class on a sheet is how the table plays the jobs. A domain card does not Turn anyone, which means it does not make a person into a Condition. If the same person has both, each one does what its own card says: the Tithe ticks only from the Condition, and the domain card still does what the SRD printed. A licence is still a licence. None of those papers comes off a domain card, and the fellowships still do not stamp a body.

| Domain | The job |
|---|---|
| **Arcana** | Shaping heat, water, stone, air, or a light a workshop already charges for, the same family of work as an Answered coax. The card is the craft. The Condition is the element that answered a person for life. |
| **Blade** | A weapon in the hand, a watch holding someone until morning, a pruning knife or a kitchen knife, or a cohort walking children to a sound Hand, a sound Tree. |
| **Bone** | Knowing where to stand, who is tired, and which bank will go, which is reading a fight the way a road-mend reads a ford. |
| **Codex** | Paper, minutes, a copy, and a count, the work of a Book-hand's table. Reading a page is not Speaking a colour. |
| **Grace** | A guest-meal, a factor's terms, or a voice that asks. The hazardous Cry stays on the hall-keepers' ticket. This job is the ask, not that Cry. |
| **Midnight** | A shut door, a night's work, or a trade that does not want a square. The Slide keeps its own note for that trade. |
| **Sage** | Ground, herd, weather, and the bark of a young graft, which is a greens-keeper's eye. The Taken-In remain their own card. |
| **Splendor** | The room after harm, the wash and the light and the pot and the table once a stiller's gaze is done. Phoenix fire stays with Phoenix. |
| **Valor** | Standing a gap, whether that is a flood-wall or a child walked to a sound Hand. The watch, and the cohort it sends, is the civic hold. |

An Answered person who also carries an Arcana card has a Tithe and a craft, and what it means for that person to be idle is already said with the Answered: an element left unused will speak whether or not the shop is paying. A Kept person with the same card has the craft, and no element that lets go in a crowded room. The town can tell those two people apart by the work, and by whether the workshop has a plan for a day off.

At Leaf-Fall, the week the door is open and a child can stand under a Tree, a warden says the colour, or says that the child was held. The square hears what the leaf did. That sentence is the Speaking. It is not a spell, and it does not use a Spellcast trait, the trait a spell would call on. Hope does not buy a colour. A warden cannot Speak a person into a Condition who is not standing under a sound Tree in the week the door is open. When a colour is misspoken, it is corrected, and the week is not run again.

## The long-house (novelty without a new order)

A Long-Lived house that has endowed a library is already this sector. What that house furnishes is something genuinely new. The money goes out into the stacks, and a deathless member of the house still sits the chair and names the scholars, which is how an endowment keeps the steering after the coin has left. The Pourers, a circle of Long-Lived who meet at a shelf in a deathless library, keep one such shelf on the Ledger Coast. Staff are often the Kept, people with no Tithe of their own, able to fetch and to stay. What the house will not furnish is a page at a time to a person who cannot walk in. They will not carry a rumour to a fade shut in a room. A junior who extends a page, the way a clerk extends a novelty subscription one more week, has started the Slide's week beside the long-house.

## The gate (the sector's shared courtesy)

Every official door stops at a line the town has agreed to call *enough*. The greens-keeper keeps the lot, and does not walk the room where someone is already wilting. The hall-keeper keeps the hour, and does not open the side door after the lamps for a voice that missed its slate. The long-house is open to a person who can walk in, and it will not carry news to a Long-Lived shut in a room. The ticket-house trains the child who was Given the colour, and stamps the adult who has become competent. The raw Struck person is the Intake's problem. The guild is glad to forget the shed if the catching-year, the in-house word for that week, stays quiet.

The Slide begins where that line is. The two houses can meet, and they do not merge. A hall that runs late on purpose is already arguing with a week-rent that still opens. The worst of it is a public wilt or a Cry in the market that the official door caused by closing.

The other houses touch the gate without becoming it. A tree-warden points a newly Given child at a lot or a hall, and does not licence anyone. The lot and the hall decide who gets the bed or the hour. A Book-hand's term may assume a greens-share, or an hour on the seventh day. If the lot or the slate is full, the term is unpayable that day, and the book records it as owing. The Holding Desk is not this sector. A Bound person's civic year may assume a hall. The desk downstairs does not furnish one. A Taken-In door-keeper, one of the people who keep the older doors a later Strike can come through, may live on a lot. The courtesy of that door does not give anyone a bed.

Under the Tallied Crown the hall and the green *are* the channel, the share the roll can open or shut. Videm *(VEE-dem)*, the channel-clerk, runs the counted hall and the slate that is supposed to match the tended green, and will say the channel held while someone three streets off is already wilting. Sirtal *(SEER-tal)*, the warrant-hand, writes the warrant that withholds the share until a name sits still. A lot that still has beds, and a share Sirtal will not open, are the same street.

*What follows is a closing-notice, nailed to a lot-gate and, in the same hand, to a hall door. The back of the sheet is last week's notice, not taken down.*

> *Share is enough. Hour is enough. After close we do not walk the rooms. After the lamps we do not open the side door. If you have already wilted, the lot is still the lot — come when we open. If you have already cried in the street, the hall is still the hall — come at open, not now. If you cannot wait, we do not know a pot. We do not know a room. The town is glad. File that as weather if you must. It is a door.*

The keepers do know a cousin's pot, and they do know a room that still shuts. The notice says they do not, so that neither the lot nor the hall is blamed for what the pot and the room do. The town is glad of both, the official gate and the week that opens when the gate has shut.

## How they live in the three corners

In the Waiting Lands the works are commons-minded, and the purse is thin. A cousin's field stands in when the lot is short. You were helped, so the valley expects you to spend the gift as it asks, and when the share fails the Slide is a private pot they call the last pot, and also gift-credit.

On the Ledger Coast the provision is posted and charged by the season. You can be priced out of staying whole, and after the fee fails the Slide is week-rent.

Under the Tallied Crown the crown furnishes, and the crown holds. Videm holds the channel. The Slide is the late week that channel will not admit failed. Videm knows the week is there, and Sirtal files the leak as the person's own lapse.

Kumbaan, the Sundering Isle, has no greenhouse and no ael-hall. A wreck walked uphill is a guest. The storm-wall is weather, current, and reef, and a graft never crossed it.

A bed-list is plants, a hall-slate is hours, and a guild-roll names the practitioners of a dangerous art. Only two crowns write persons and the gifts they carry on the same paper. Threnmaieth calls its roll holy and names it the Threnhael. Trenledd, the Worn Count, keeps a roll of persons too, and does not call that roll holy. Road-hands carry living wood, greens-keepers keep the soil, hall-keepers keep an hour, and the ticket-houses keep a licence.

## The Greens-Keepers

Greens-keepers keep the garden-commons, the greens, and a leaf-lot, so that a Taken-In person in a stone town has living ground to lie on and does not wilt on a parlour floor. They are a jurisdiction of the furnishing, not a house with a college of its own. They keep the lot, and they do not go looking for anyone who is not at the gate.

Everyday people say **greens-keepers**, or **lot-keepers**. In the liturgy they are the **Saelhael** *(SAEL-hael)*. *Sael* is sowing, the first green, said "sayl," and *hael* is whole, said "hayl," so the name is the sown-whole. The Waiting Lands' second word is **Mother's-green hands**. The Ledger Coast says **lot-keepers**, as ordinary as saying gardener. The Tallied Crown files them as **the Tended**.

### Shape of the house

The house is the lot. A town has a strip along the wall, or pots in a yard, or, in the Waiting Lands, a cousin's field held in common. Whoever waters it, and says who sleeps on which bed tonight, is the house. There is no Green Seat over other towns.

A share gives a night on living dirt, a season on a bed, or a commons-hour. The person should leave able to work. Someone who cannot leave is the Slide's work, a private pot after the share fails. The keepers will not follow you home with that pot, and they will not open the gate again because the wilt has started in a room they do not walk to.

A bed-slate is a list of plants, rows and tired beds. A person's name gets onto it because that person is *on* a bed, not because the lot has started a roll of people. In Threnmaieth the crown already has another list. Videm, who holds the counted channel, wants the beds to match that channel. The lot will not invent a bed to close someone else's book.

### If you meet one

You meet them with dirt under the nails, a key to a gate in a wall, and a slate of rows, and if you are let far enough in you meet last year's leaf already buried under a new bed. Ask where a Taken-In person sleeps when the rooms are stone. A stone town cannot keep its Taken-In without a lot. Green is free on a farm, and in the city it costs a gate.

### Who comes, and when

A Taken-In person who works in town comes because the rooms are stone and the Tithe is due. The keeper gives the share the lot has, a bed or a night or an hour, and writes the bed if anything is written. They will not walk the three streets to the room where the wilt has already started.

A Book-hand comes when a settling assumes a greens-share. The keeper honours the term if there is a bed, and marks the slate full if there is not, so the book becomes owing and the lot does not tell a lie to close it. A crown-clerk in Threnmaieth comes because the tended green is supposed to match the channel. The keepers give the beds they have. They will not pretend a wilt off the lot is not happening, and they will not walk to it.

Almost no one else comes for the share. The week that is not a queue is watering, and turning the rows the slate already names. Grain feeds the town by another door. This gate is the living ground that keeps an exposed person off a parlour floor.

### An ordinary week

They open the gate, and that opening is the kindness. A Taken-In person lies down on living ground until the tokens fill, the count of the upkeep coming back up, and then leaves, after which the bed is turned and the next row is watered. Last year's commons-leaf is buried under a new bed. No one will explain the leaf to a guest. A first-year who asks is told, by whoever is on the gate, that this is how the lot remembers.

When the queue is longer than the wilt, they still open for the share they have, and they close when the share is gone. They know a cousin still has a pot. They do not say the cousin's name. A child who carries the water can name that cousin. The slate cannot, and the notice on the gate says the lot does not know a pot, so the lot is not blamed for what the pot does.

They do not steal a scion, the living wood a Road-hand carries to plant a Tree, and they do not send a lot to Kumbaan. What they give, they call a share.

### Who may keep a lot

The house does not ask what Kind a person was born. You are of the house when you can keep a bed, when the lot is still alive in the dry month, and when the town still sends its Taken-In to you.

### How they live in the three corners

In the Waiting Lands, where they are called Mother's-green hands, the commons is a cousin's field. They will say they are tending a gift. The quiet obligation is the one the valley keeps: you were laid on the Mother's green, so you will spend the green as the valley asks. When the field fails, the last pot does the Slide's work, and they will still call it tending.

On the Ledger Coast, where they are called lot-keepers, the leaf-lot is posted by the season, with coin on the gate. A Taken-In person who cannot pay the season is priced out of this lot. The lot that still opens after the fee has failed is doing the Slide's week-rent.

Under the Tallied Crown they are filed as the Tended. The crown furnishes the tended green and can withhold it, and the beds are supposed to match the channel. A wilt off the lot is a leak the roll does not want to file. The keepers look like gardeners. What the crown can shut off is the water.

Kumbaan has no greens-keepers, and no lot is sent there.

### The gate

Whoever furnishes a provided-for Tithe holds power over the person who needs it. The greens-keepers hold that power and refuse to carry it past the wall. The refusal is the kindness, because they will not own you, and it is the cruelty, because they will not walk three streets. The town is glad either way, because a person who wilts quietly off the lot is not a public hazard. A devout town can say the gift was offered, a Lestrand quay can say the season was posted, and the Tallied Crown can say the channel held.

The need for living land is the Tithe. People built the gate. A lot can be full of beds and still have a queue, and the keepers are then choosing who sleeps on stone.

### A bed-slate

The slate inside the gate is plants, which rows are taken and which beds are tired. The words the town is meant to read are the closing-notice nailed on the gate itself, the same sheet a hall door wears in the same hand. Last week's notice is still on the back.

> *Share is enough. Hour is enough. After close we do not walk the rooms. After the lamps we do not open the side door. If you have already wilted, the lot is still the lot — come when we open. If you have already cried in the street, the hall is still the hall — come at open, not now. If you cannot wait, we do not know a pot. We do not know a room. The town is glad. File that as weather if you must. It is a door.*

The notice is a commons keeping itself to the share, and a little cold about it. The keepers do know a cousin's pot. The child with the water can name the cousin. The slate cannot, and the leaf under the new bed is not on the notice at all.

## The Hall-Keepers

Hall-keepers keep the official outlet and the Far-Voiced ticket in the same building. A Far-Voiced person needs both the scheduled hour and the licence. In the ael-hall, the airing, from *ael*, to let feeling out, they empty a voice on that schedule, and the building is the town's playhouse. They furnish enough voice that a person can walk until tomorrow. After the lamps, a room that still shuts belongs to the Slide, and it is not this hall's side door.

**Hall-keepers**, or **ael-hands**, is what people say in every register, from the street to a docket. There is no church-name over them. The Waiting Lands' second word is **gift-hall folk**. The Ledger Coast says **release-hands**, as ordinary as saying box-office. The Tallied Crown files them as **counted-hall clerks**.

They sell you an hour in which you may feel in earnest, and they will not open after the lamps.

### Shape of the house

One building does two jobs. The hall furnishes the outlet, and it stamps the Voice-ticket. The keeper who says your hour is the third is the keeper who says you may stand in a crowded room. The hour is enough: a song, a weeping, a relay-call, a laugh that empties the chest, feeling on purpose where a neighbour paid to hear it. The person should leave emptied and able to work. This house wants you gone until tomorrow.

Each town keeps its own hall, and no seat over the other towns writes to it for leave.

### If you meet one

You can tell them by the ink or the chalk on a finger, by the slate of hours, by the stool at the door where the ticket is stamped, and by the one bench nobody sweeps. Ask where a voice can be loud without becoming a Cry in the market. A town with Far-Voiced people and no hall leaves a Book-hand writing *owing*, and leaves the street waiting for the feeling to land where everyone can hear it.

### Who comes, and when

A Given child, after the sea-blue leaf, is apprenticed here. The voice has to be trained before it is a hazard, in breath and projection and venting on purpose, and the ticket comes when the town can stand next to them. The hall does not Speak the colour. The warden already did that.

A working Far-Voiced person, a performer or a crier or a sworn witness, often pays the Tithe by the vocation itself. The hall gives them the stage, the relay-post, or the stand, and stamps the year if the ticket is due, and it will not invent a crisis so they can vent off the slate. A person who has lost that honest stage, posted somewhere isolated or set to dull labor, can buy or be granted a scheduled hour, be emptied, and be sent out. The hall will not open after the lamps. A room that still takes a voice after that belongs to the Slide, and the week it sells is never quite enough for the person to get free.

A Struck Far-Voiced person, raw from the drowning that brings the voice later and untrained, is handed to the Intake. If the Cry is already in the room, a hall-keeper may sit the first hour with the shed. The hall will not stamp a ticket on a raw voice, and it will not leave them in the market.

A Book-hand whose settling assumes a seventh-day hour is honoured when the slate has a hole, and marked full when it does not. Greyhull's release-house will take that seventh day when the term names it. The hall will not pretend a full house has a bench. In the eighth week, when the passage-money fails, the slip can still be fair, and the person can still owe a book that does not close.

Anyone who came for a play is sold a bench. The working voices pay their Tithe as the show. The hall will not stop a performance to process a ticket. The show is the Tithe for the people on the boards, and a night out for the people who paid to hear it.

### An ordinary week

Morning is the slate, with the hours named, the relay-posts listed, a child's breath-lesson in the cold hall, and the ticket-desk a stool by the door. Evening is the house, feeling in earnest and on purpose, where a neighbour paid to hear it. One seat is never swept. The line people use for it is, "That's the first Cry's seat." No one will explain which Cry.

When a voice misses its hour, the keeper does not open the side door. They know a room that still shuts, and they do not say the room's name. A raw Struck person is the Intake's, and the hall will not stamp that ticket. They do not run a mission-hall on Kumbaan.

### Who may keep a hall

The stool does not ask what Kind a person was born. You are of the house by practice, by who can keep the hour, and by whether the town still comes when the slate says it will.

### How they live in the three corners

In the Waiting Lands they are called gift-hall folk. The venting happens at worship, the purse is thin, and a person who was given the hour is expected to spend the voice as the valley asks, as crier, witness, or hymn. When that hour is missed, the room that still shuts is the last pot.

On the Ledger Coast they are release-hands, and the playhouse is the hall. Hours are posted, the ticket is a master's papers, and a sweep-hour is sometimes the only hour a person can afford. After that, the week is week-rent.

Under the Tallied Crown they are counted-hall clerks. The crown furnishes the hour and can withhold it, and the ticket is filed as a crown-warrant. Videm will say the hour held while a Cry is still unspent at the door.

Kumbaan has no ael-hall, and the hall-keepers do not keep a mission-house there.

### The scheduled hour

The hall sells, or grants, the right to feel in public on time. Off that time the voice is a ticket problem, and a missed hour stays missed. A performer who lost the honest stage still has the hour when the slate has a hole, the season-fee was paid, and the crown still lists them. The town is glad of an emptied voice, because that voice is not a Cry in the market. A devout town can say the gift was aired, a Lestrand quay can say the bill was posted, and the Tallied Crown can say the channel held.

The same hour is some people's Tithe and other people's night out. A devout barn and a priced quay will both tell you that is simply how a hall works. The first Cry's bench stays unswept. No one will say which Cry it belongs to.

### An hour-slate

The morning slate names the hours. The words nailed beside the bill are the same closing-notice the lot-gate wears, in the same hand, with yesterday's sheet still on the back.

> *Share is enough. Hour is enough. After close we do not walk the rooms. After the lamps we do not open the side door. If you have already wilted, the lot is still the lot — come when we open. If you have already cried in the street, the hall is still the hall — come at open, not now. If you cannot wait, we do not know a pot. We do not know a room. The town is glad. File that as weather if you must. It is a door.*

The notice is a playhouse being practical. The sweep-hour, when the house keeps one, is the nearest thing on the slate to kindness. The keepers do know a room that still shuts. The notice says they do not, so the hall is not blamed for it. The first Cry's bench is not on the notice. Everyone in the house knows which seat it is, and no one will explain it.

## The Stillers

The Stillers licence the Stilled and run the infirmaries. One of the Stilled can hold a moment still. A wound that is bleeding stops bleeding, a poison stops where it is, and a blade already swinging hangs in the air a beat longer than the arm meant. The same look, misused, can leave a person helpless in a fight, which is why this ticket is the strictest in the world. The guild issues it. The state watches the guild. It does not keep a list of every Stilled person, and it does not police a people.

Everyday speech is **stillers**, or **the Stillers' Guild**. In the liturgy they are **the Stelhael** *(STEL-hael)*, from *stel*, to still, to hold from moving, and *hael*, whole. The name says they keep you hale by holding you still. The Waiting Lands say **blessing-surgeons**. The Ledger Coast says **ticket-surgeons**. The Tallied Crown files them as **crown-stillers**.

They will save you on the table, and they will make you drill until the Grey is gone. The Grey is the stillness that collects in the surgeon each time the gaze is used. Left in the body, it locks one of the surgeon's own limbs stone-still. The seizing stays on the Stilled. It does not turn another person to stone, and it does not take anyone's will. The ticket marks competence. The infirmary's pace follows the surgeon's Grey as much as it follows the patient.

### Shape of the house

If you understand this guild, you understand a Voice-ticket and an element-ticket, because both are the same shape of licence. What the house keeps is a roster of people who practise a dangerous art, not a list of a people. The pool of those who might be asked is large. This roll is stillers who Gaze, not every pale-stone person in the city. Pale stone, marble-white, is the leaf a Given Stilled child draws.

The infirmary and the yard are one guild and one clock. On the table they hold a wound. In the yard they spar, patrol, and hold the riot-line, because using the Gaze fills the Grey and the yard is what empties it. The guild's best work creates the guild's Tithe, and that is the job, not a puzzle they have solved. They see themselves as a surgeon's college, apprentices and a code and a ticket like a master's papers. The frightening part is the Gaze that saves you, and the unlicensed Struck person the town will meet with a rope.

There is no Stillers' Seat over other towns. A rich infirmary in the core is rich, and a frontier stiller is one tired person, because the charter is local. The state watches that charter, not the body of the person who holds the ticket.

They live beside the other houses the way any craft lives beside its neighbours, and the hard edge is where the jobs must not be swapped.

Everyday is how the two houses share an ordinary year. The hard edge is where that sharing breaks.

### If you meet one

You meet a calm person, flat-eyed when they are working, whose hair stirs when something nearby has gone too still. You meet them in an infirmary, on a drill-floor, or on a line that has not yet become a massacre. You would not know which hour was the guild's unless you already needed a ticket. They are useful the way a surgeon is useful. A town without one makes do with a rope and a prayer.

### An ordinary week

The theatre is the pleasant horror outsiders remember. A gaze stops blood, and the surgeon then has to go and move. Between patients they drill, they spar, and they dance, badly, because the dance works the Grey out of the body that spent it. No one in the core calls this therapy. They call it the job.

They touch a doorknob before they look at a patient. If the knob has gone still under an unwatched Gaze, the room is already wrong. A first-year who forgets is sent back to the yard. The yard is exertion on purpose, the Tithe paid as the work they already understand themselves to do. The ticket-stool is a master's mark, and the person holds it. The guild knows its members the way any craft does. On the Ledger Coast the ticket can start to behave like property, a place and a chair and a name on a door. That is this house's live argument. It is not a rule the rest of the world has locked, and other towns do not have to keep it.

When a raw Struck person arrives, the paying floor loses a master for a week, and the guild is not grateful. That week is the Intake.

What they carry is a ticket, a code that is spoken, this world being late-medieval, chalk for the drill roster, and hands that have learned to keep moving. The roll they keep is stillers. It is not a census of the Stilled, and it is not a book of weeks.

They cannot still a will, and they will not pretend they can in order to frighten a street. A raw Struck person goes to the Intake rather than leaving the yard with a stamp. If they can still drag a person to the yard, they will not let the Grey run to a public seize, because a seize on the floor ruins the charter. A roll of stillers is not Threnmaieth's register of the Stilled, and they do not export a college across the storm-wall.

### Who may hold a ticket

The Gaze does not care what you were born. A child of one of the common ancestries and a Kitsune apprentice, one of the fox-born, can stand in the same yard. You are of the house when you can still a wound and then move, and when the town has watched you do both. The house leans toward the Stilled for the work that matches the Tithe. It does not gate the yard by Condition.

Often is who usually does this work. The last column is who else is welcome to do it.

Skeptics are welcome, because the Gaze does not require a Mother, and believers in the Waiting Lands will still say a blessing-to-practice, the devout name for the ticket, over the same paper.

### How they live in the three corners

The Waiting Lands keep blessing-surgeons, often one tired stiller whose riot-line is also the only theatre, with a blessing-to-practice in place of a college. You were trained, so the valley expects you to stand every line it has. If that stiller is three valleys away, a raw Struck person is still met with a rope.

On the Ledger Coast they are ticket-surgeons. The infirmaries are superb, and the guild-ticket sits next to property, so you can be priced out of the chair. The charter is a commercial good, and unlicensed practice threatens that charter as much as it threatens the street.

Under the Tallied Crown they are crown-stillers. The crown wants the ticket as a crown-warrant and the roll as a column. Some masters cooperate and call the cooperation stewardship. Some refuse, because a guild-roll is not a Threnhael. The Gaze that saves you can run through a channel that withholds the bed from a household the crown dislikes. It is the darkest infirmary in the world, and it is still a real infirmary.

On Kumbaan a travelling stiller is a guest. The guild keeps no chapter there.

### The Grey as labor

The guild's best work fills the clock that will lock a limb if the surgeon does not then labor. So the house needs them on the table and in the yard, and the charter needs them never to lapse in public. The infirmary's pace is set by the surgeon's Grey, not only by the patient. This is not a queue for a living Tree, and it is not a week rented from the Slide. It is a hospital that runs on its staff's Tithe.

The argument about the ticket as property, which is the Coast's and which leaks into other towns, is the second face of the same house. Competence lives in a person. The chair can start to live in the guild.

The Motherfaith says she gave a gaze that costs. The roster is people's work, and nobody expects the Tree to make the drill optional. A grudging extra drill-master is the most the guild will grant at the end of any story. The paying floor still comes first.

Theatre, paid surgeries, and charter-fees are the living, together with whatever stipend a town puts on a civic infirmary. Intake is badly paid. The raw Struck people who most need a week in the shed are the week the floor can least spare, which is why the shed stays the underdog and never becomes the rotation anyone wants. The stillers do not furnish green, and they do not furnish novelty. A stiller who is also Taken-In uses the lot like anyone else.

The shed is not on this wall. If you were stone last week, you are not on this wall. Come to the door that does not face the street. We do not write that door. The roster is a college keeping its people hale, with the code written as a work-order. It also shows the paying floor refusing to write down the door to the Intake. "A Grey we file as lapse" is how a tired surgeon becomes a charter problem instead of a person who needs a longer run. The knob is not explained.

## The Element-Guilds

Ember, Tide, Root, and Gale are four ordinary craft charters that share one safety rule. They keep separate fees, masters, and work, and they share a ticket standard because an idle Answered person is a workshop hazard before they are a theological question. The ticket is what lets that element be used in a crowded yard.

Everyday speech is **the element-guilds**, or the **Ember-house**, the **Tide-house**, the **Root-house**, and the **Gale-house**. The umbrella in a formal charter is **the Crae** *(kray)*, from *crae*, craft, the working of an element. A single Answered person is *of the Crae*, and *of Ember* if you need the door. The Waiting Lands' second word is **gift-shops**. The Ledger Coast says **the craft-houses**. The Tallied Crown files **the Reckoned Craes**. People say the element-guilds. *The Crae* survives mainly where a charter is being written, and the umbrella name is not a separate liturgy for each door.

The shop is how an Answered person pays the Tithe. Leaving the shop leaves the element idle, and idle is the hazard.

### Shape of the house

Four doors share one way of licensing. Ember keeps smiths, glass, and firefighting. Tide keeps hulls, dyers, and any work that needs water to behave. Root keeps masons, potters, and the carrying-earth a Road-hand may buy, living earth coaxed so it will travel with a scion. Gale keeps sails, mills, and instruments. An Ember-master does not set Tide's fees. The shop is what the charter covers. Journeymen sit under a door, which is not a census of every lamp-amber leaf in the city. Lamp-amber is the shade that names an Answered child, and the shade names the element. The mill-share, the hull-wrights, and the road-mend keep the season of work beside these doors, and they do not stamp this ticket.

Earnest work, the element used at the craft and not in play, slakes the Tithe. The guild is therefore the upkeep. That is why the monopoly has teeth. A benched Answered person is not merely unemployed, because the element will speak whether or not the shop is paying them. A late-medieval town already needed smiths and masons. The Condition made those guilds necessary twice, once for the work, and once so the work does not let go.

The paper is ordinary on purpose. An apprenticeship term, a supervised Reach, a crowded-yard endorsement, a night rotation, and a transfer are the boxes a clerk already understands. A Reach is the element answering through the hands. The danger enters when one of those boxes is hiding grief, or coercion, or a master protecting a fee. A Condition does not hand the house a personality. An even-tempered Root potter and a short-fused Ember smith share a ticket standard and almost nothing else.

Everyday is how the two houses share an ordinary year. The hard edge is where that sharing breaks.

### An ordinary week

A week moves from the shop to the rotation, the fire-crew and the escort and the watch that is also the Tithe, then to the ticket-stool, and it ends at the shop-rule no one explains to a guest.

When a master is pulled to Intake, the paying floor loses a Reach for a week. Ember hates this during a dry month, when the fire-crew can least spare a pair of hands, and Tide hates it when a hull is mid-bend. That hatred is why the shed stays the underdog.

They will not switch an element to suit a labour shortage, because the shade of the leaf named it. Benching someone "for their own good," with no earnest use waiting, is how a room lets go, so they will not do it. A raw Struck person goes to the Intake, and leaves without a stamp. They are not a fourth clergy. They have no colour to Speak, no graft to carry, and no drowning-stone to keep, and they do not export a Crae-college to Kumbaan.

A Tengu, the crow-born of the high country, lean and sharp-featured, with dark feathers through the hair and down the arms, who is also Gale-Answered, is a common lean of the same kind. The element is not reserved to either people. You are of the house when the element answers your work and not the rafters. The house keeps those leans. It does not keep a gate.

### How they live in the three corners

The Waiting Lands keep gift-shops, one mixed yard and four tools and a blessing-to-practice. You were answered, so the valley expects you to spend the element as it asks, on the mill, the wall, or the fire-line. Idle is rare, because the valley will not let a gift sit.

On the Ledger Coast the craft-houses are deep, the tickets are posted, and the monopoly has teeth. You can be priced out of the chair and still need the work, which is how a person slides from self-paying to exposed overnight. The charter is money and safety in one paper.

Under the Tallied Crown the Reckoned Craes put the element and the person on the same column of the roll. Workshops are channels the crown can hold, so a benched Answered person is a withheld Reach, a use the crown has not released. The shop looks like a gift-works. What the crown can withhold is the fire, the slip, the stone, or the wind.

On Kumbaan a wrecked Tide-Answered person is a guest. The guild keeps no Tide-house on the sand.

### The shop you cannot leave

Self-paying holds until you want to stop. The Tithe does not care that you buried a child, that the yard burned, or that you are tired of glass. Idle, the element speaks for itself. So the guild's kindness, a shop and a crew and a rotation, is also how the guild holds you. Leaving is a Tithe-crisis, the upkeep failing because the work has stopped. The Waiting Lands, in the devout way of speaking, call that a vocation. The quay, which is how the Ledger Coast talks about the same paper, calls it a contract. The Tallied Crown calls it a channel.

The Motherfaith says she answered. The shop-rule is people's work, written by the house and not by the leaf. The complaint a person can bring is about the exit. The constraint is the element itself. The charter is local, and it has no authority to make another town take an idle journeyman.

### A shop-rule

*What follows is a shop-rule from an Ember-house in a Lestrand glass-yard, nailed above the last coal. The back of the sheet is a Tide-house rule from the next quay, used as backing.*

> *The last coal stays. A Reach used in play is not a Reach used in earnest — the ticket will not have it, and the room will. If you cannot work, you will still work: the fire-crew, the night bellows, the watch. We do not bench. We do not rent a spark. The shed is not this door; raw hands go to the door that does not face the street. If you have already let go, this is still the house — come to the coal, not to the square. Gale will not take you. Tide will not take you. The coal will.*

The rule is a craft keeping its people steady, and it is a little fierce about it. "We do not bench" also means "you may not leave." The Tide-rule on the back says the same thing about a cup of water. The two houses did not copy each other. Why the coal stays, and why the cup is yesterday's, they will not say.

## The Intake

The Intake is a shed, and it works to a plain rule. It gets a newly Struck person, someone the change took outside the childhood ceremony and who is not yet safe to stand next to, into a state where the town does not have to reach for a rope.

Everyday speech is **the Intake**, or **the shed**. The in-house word is **the catching-year**. The Waiting Lands call it **the late-catching**. The Ledger Coast calls it **the emergency ticket**. The Tallied Crown calls it **the unwitnessed warrant**, because the crown treats a change nobody witnessed as a thing that still needs a name. A person has a week. In that week the hazard is live and the town wants the person gone. If the week works, the slip is burned, and the story told afterwards is that the guild simply trains its own.

They have a week to make a live hazard into a person the town will stand next to, and if they succeed, no one writes it down.

### Shape of the office

The Struck person is brought to the shed. A runner arrives, or a master is pulled off the paying floor. The fear is a rope already in someone's hand, a runner still three days out, and a loud week that becomes a charter hearing. Road-hands already walk the long road. If the Intake walks, it walks toward one particular person. It is not a circuit.

Three ticket-houses share the shed, and each hazard still has its own door. A raw Gaze goes to the Stillers. A raw voice goes to the hall. A raw Reach goes to the right element, not the nearest fire. The wrong house is how a Cry gets stilled quiet, as if a voice were a thing the yard could hold still, or how an Ember is put on a bench to sing. The parent yards pretend the shed is not on their wall, even in a week when they have lent it the master. Success is silence. A quiet catching-year is a week the floor lost. A loud one is a hearing. Nobody changes the guild's mind about the shed. A grudging extra pair of hands is the most the yard will grant, and they will take them back.

### What the catching-week requires

The shed has four things, and no more. It has a place, the door that does not face the street, which is not the theatre, the boards, or the paying shop. It has hands: one master the floor can spare, often the one it can least spare, a Kept person who carries no Condition and will sit, and whatever the yard had left from last year. It has a narrow authority. They can take the person if the person will come. They cannot overrule a frontier rope, and they cannot stamp a ticket this week. It has a thin reputation. Leadership has forgotten them on a good day and will blame them on a bad one, because the charter is at risk if the person harms someone, and at risk again if the guild refused the Struck and the town later says it left a hazard in the square.

What they are racing is time. The Gaze, the Cry, or the Reach is already live, and the town wants the person gone. The task is to begin the apprenticeship before that rope, to help the person make a first honest payment of the Tithe that is not a public hazard, and to find someone who will vouch for them as what they are now, which means someone who will stand up and say they know the person as they have become. Intake does not sell that vouch, and it does not take the vouch's place. At the end of the week the person is still raw and beginning to be safe to stand next to, or the person is gone. Gone means the rope, the road, or a week-keeper who will keep them by the week and will not make them safe.

What counts as success is that the person is still alive, still here, and beginning to be safe, and that the town pretends this was always going to happen. The guild does not apologize.

### If you meet one

You meet someone tired, chalk on a sleeve, a key to a door the slate does not name. You would miss them if you came in the front of the infirmary or the playhouse. Ask where the one who was stone yesterday went. Or you are the runner, and you already know the door. They are who a town has when a becoming has already happened and the town may prefer it had not.

### Who comes, and when

| Who | Where they go | What the Intake will not do |
|---|---|---|
| A raw Stilled person, who was stone | A first drill. The hold does not outlast the scene. | Stamp a ticket, or still a crowd as a lesson. |
| A raw Far-Voiced person, who drowned | The back room of the hall, so the person can vent on purpose. | Open that room after the lamps as a policy. |
| A raw Answered person | The right element door, for earnest use that is not the crowded shop. | Put them on a hull the same day. |
| A family, a door-keeper, or a Book-hand | The Intake takes the person and asks who will stand for them as what they are now. | Sell a vouch, or call the person Given. |
| The watch | The Intake bargains for the shed instead of the rope, and sometimes loses. | Fight the town. They have a key. They cannot command the street. |

The paying floor wants its master back. The shed finishes the week or fails it, and the master goes back to the floor.

Bound Terms are a table, not a body the shed can catch, and a Taken-In upkeep is a lot, so neither of those people is this door's work. A Returned purpose cannot be furnished here, a Two-Bodied person uses civic land, and a Long-Lived person uses a long-house. A bystander who was only standing nearby gets no ticket from the shed.

### An ordinary catching-year (it is not ordinary)

There is no ordinary week, only the week in front of them.

They get the person off the square, out of the water, and away from the shop that is already wrong. They name what is already true, that the person carries a Gaze, or a voice, or a Reach, and then they help that person make a first honest payment that is not a public hazard, a drill or an hour or a coal that is not the public forge. They find someone who will stand for the person anew. At the end of the week the person is handed to the real apprenticeship, still raw and beginning to be safe, or the person is gone.

The slip is written on the inside of a ticket-blank, and the face stays empty until a real ticket is earned. A first-year who writes on the face is made to strike it out. They will not stamp a ticket this week, because competence is not a week old. If they must walk, they walk toward this Struck person. The Road-hands already have the road, and a Bound person's Terms are another table's paper.

### Who may sit the shed

The shed does not choose sitters by Kind. A person sits the week so the Struck person does not become a public story. Whoever sits is a ticket-holder of that hazard, there for the live Tithe, or a Kept person who can stay because they carry no Condition of their own, or a Struck person who remembers the door, or a runner who can run.

### How they live with the other houses

The Stillers, the hall, and the element-guilds pretend the shed is not written on the wall. If the week fails, the Slide takes the overflow. The Intake wants the person safe enough to leave. The Slide wants the person not fallen enough to stay. A Book-hand may send someone, and a hall that was never there still counts as owing. A door-keeper may name the person kin the same week. Kinship is not a ticket. They are two courtesies, and either one can happen without the other. The Standing Trade will sell a witness who says they watched. The Intake will not.

### How they live in the three corners

In the Waiting Lands the rope is real, and the shed may be a barn. Once the person is caught, the valley will spend them.

On the Ledger Coast there is a fee if anyone can pay it, and the hazard in the street still takes the person when nobody can.

Under the Tallied Crown the crown wants the person named as they are caught, so a slip written on the inside of a blank, where the face stays empty, is already a defiance of that naming.

On Kumbaan a wreck walked uphill is a guest, and there is no shed on the sand.

### The week no one writes

The floor needs the master back on the paying work, the town needs the hazard gone, and the Struck person needs a year, while the shed has a week and is blamed for all three. If the week succeeds, the story told afterwards is that the guild trains its own. If it fails, the story is the shed. The pay is whatever the parent house has left after the paying floor, the shed and the food and the master's lost week, and what they do with it is catch a person rather than keep a commons or sell an hour.

### A catching-year slip

*What follows is written on the inside of a blank ticket, in a stillers' shed in a core town. The face of the blank is empty on purpose.*

> *Inside only. Gaze, raw. Third night. Stood for by the sister and by N. who will not give a family name. First drill held. The hold does not last. No ticket. If the week ends quiet, burn this. If it does not, the charter already knows. We do not face the street. If they will not come, we cannot make a town take them.*

The empty face is deliberate. When the slip is burned, the success becomes the guild's ordinary story, and the person's becoming was never written down in public. The sister stood, and N. would not give a family name, and both of them are someone standing for the Struck.

## Three corners: Vaethorn, Lestrand, Threnmaieth

Every polity in the Turning sets three things, and sets them apart from one another. **Theology** is how far the gift is taken into ordinary life, from an ardent Motherfaith, the faith of the Leaf-Mother, to a place that is frankly secular. **Reach** is whether the place stands among healthy Turning Trees, where the Given are dense, or at a thin edge, where more children come away Kept and more people are Struck later and alone. **Governance** is how far the state reaches onto a person, from light guild self-rule, the ordinary arrangement, to a crown that writes its subjects down. Law, livelihood, and daily life stay the same from power to power, because they come from the Trees and from the Conditions. What changes is the combination.

Say **the Waiting Lands**, **the Ledger Coast**, and **the Tallied Crown**. Charters, rites, and learned maps write *Vaethorn*, *Lestrand*, and *Threnmaieth*. Each pair of the three shares exactly one setting and differs on the other two. Three further corners have notes of their own. Ornled is a secular frontier. Maiethvael is a devout, lightly governed, Tree-rich core. Trenledd is a cold surveillance-state with no faith to sanctify the roll. The other twelve powers sit between these.

| Name | Theology | Reach | Governance | The one-line feel |
|---|:---:|:---:|:---:|---|
| **the Waiting Lands** *(written Vaethorn)* | high | low | low | Faith is ardent, the Trees are thin, and almost no state reaches a person: a warm, poor, half-lawless pilgrim edge |
| **the Ledger Coast** *(written Lestrand)* | low | high | low | Faith is worn lightly among dense Trees, and guilds do the governing a crown would do elsewhere: a rich, cool, transactional merchant power |
| **the Tallied Crown** *(written Threnmaieth)* | high | high | high | Sacred and surveilled together: a wealthy, pious core where keeping the census is a holy duty |

The Waiting Lands and the Ledger Coast share low governance, and that is the only setting they share. Both keep a light-touch state, and everything else swings the other way, so that one is warm, poor, and faithful while the other is rich, cool, and secular.

The Waiting Lands and the Tallied Crown share high theology, the same ardent faith, lived in wildly different circumstances. One is devotion at a starving edge. The other is devotion in a sanctified core that watches its people.

The Ledger Coast and the Tallied Crown share high reach, the same wealth of Trees, and they spend that wealth in opposite directions, the Coast in free markets and the Crown on a census of persons held as holy.

The three settings are independent, which is why two devout lands can be this far apart, and why two lightly governed lands can be this far apart, and why two Tree-rich lands can spend the wealth as enemies of each other's idea of a good life. Theology is not a measure of good or bad. High theology produces both the kindest corner to a whole neighbour, the Waiting Lands, and the most inescapable control in the world, the Tallied Crown. Devotion multiplies whatever else the polity is already doing. Each corner owns its injustice, and the harms come through different institutions. Warm faith, a free market, and a sanctified order do not fail in the same way.

The name a polity gives a shared thing shows the stance. A devout land's gift-hall is a surveillance-crown's counted hall. It is the same building. The three written names come down from the Old Tongue, the root speech the rites still use.

### Vaethorn: the Waiting Lands

*(formal VAETH-orn; spoken Wetorn · theology high · reach low · governance low)*

Vaethorn is a broad, thinly peopled land at the world's Tree-poor margin, where the faith burns hottest because the Trees are scarce, and where the ruling mood is waiting. People wait for a graft, young wood set in this ground, to take. They wait for a distant Tree to be reached. They wait for a gift that may never fall. This is a frontier with theology turned as high as it goes, and the two facts feed each other. Where the Trees are few and young, more children come away Kept, more people are Struck later and alone, and the Motherfaith answers that scarcity with more devotion, not less: pilgrimage, longing, and a theology built around waiting for a gift that may not come.

**Law here.** Governance is almost absent. No crown reaches the individual, and custom outruns written law the way it does on any frontier. Citizenship is witnessed in the oldest, most literal way, a settlement small enough that everyone was there when you Turned. The Lands' thin reach makes that fragile. With few Long-Lived to remember and few Bound to vouch, the three pillars that make proof-by-people work are stretched thin. Those pillars are a Long-Lived memory, a Bound vouch, and a Far-Voiced voice. The unvouched Struck, a person who changed alone, with no guild to catch them and few witnesses to stand for them, is a constant ache, and the Lands meet it with faith rather than machinery. A feared Condition here is more likely to meet a rope or a blessing than a licence, because there is often no guild to issue one. Someone who stands for you in a new place does it at worship, and they call that standing-for *the speaking*. They call the ticket *the blessing-to-practice*. No Condition can command another person's will. That rule is the Inviolate Will, taught to every child as "the will is one's own," and here they call it *the Mother's own line*. The warden is most of the state a valley can see, and the Lands call wardens Hands-folk.

**Economy here.** There is little formal furnishing and there are few specialists, but the theology reads the frontier's poverty as charity. Where a purely secular frontier would improvise the upkeep as barter and favor, the Waiting Lands do it as alms and congregation. The venting a Far-Voiced person needs happens at worship, and they call that worship the gift-hall. Neighbours furnish the green a Taken-In person needs and count it a sacred duty, and they call that green the Mother's green, held in common. The Taken-In, whom rich cities keep at arm's length and still need on the lots, are here the honoured backbone of the land. Working the living earth is the holy economy. It is commons-minded generosity with almost nothing to fund it: real warmth, real want, and a thin edge where the people whose Tithe must be furnished slide with only prayer for a net. Time spent as the animal, on open land, they call the wild-leave. The long-house of books they call the deathless gift. When the commons run out, they call the Slide the last pot, and also gift-credit.

**Daily life here.** Ceremony is thin, and devotion makes what there is of it enormous. A child's Turning at a small, hard-won grafted Tree is the event of a decade, a joyous, anxious sacrament, and the Kept child is felt most sharply here. In a land starved of the gift, to be passed over reads, wrongly and cruelly, as being passed over by the Mother herself. People call the Kept the Waited, or Mother-kept, and the valley pities them for it. They call the Struck the late-come, met at a later door. Medicine is a travelling stiller if the settlement is lucky, and a rope and a prayer if it is not. The gap between a rich core and this edge is at its widest here. Families are warm, mixed, and self-reliant. A Condition lapsing with no infirmary to reach is a frontier tragedy the faith can console and cannot cure.

**Its injustice.** The harm is a guilt. The scarcity of the gift becomes a theology of unworthiness, so the Kept wonder what they lacked, and the Struck wonder why the Mother came to them at the wrong door and alone. A hard, quiet piety can shade into treating want as a spiritual failing. The Waiting Lands are the kindest place in the world to a whole neighbour and the loneliest to an unvouched one.

**The seat.** Eolvaeth *(EOL-vaeth)* is a pilgrim-edge vale behind the coast that faces Strandoren. The name sets *eol*, a hinge, beside *vaeth*, the waiting, and folk-speech shortens it to Elvaeth. People walk here to wait, and the Tree at the end of that wait is not a certainty. The seat is not Harrow's Green, the Hand that grew up and that a neighbour can already count on, not the three hamlets that can see that canopy from a ford, and not Saelvaeth's march, further along the same wave of grafts.

**What they remember.** The wave of wood arrived late, and the First Cut is the name of that arrival, the first time living wood was carried out from the Awakening Tree and set in new ground. The Hinge Hush, the peace that still charges the Old Crossing once, says the Waiting Lands may bless a landing on Heskoren and may not levy that landing as if the peace were a gospel. Ornled, the secular frontier on that same far coast, has no hymn on the clause. Saelvaeth may take a pot of living wood without paying the Waiting Lands' price for the wait. The guilt-theology wants both of those neighbours unworthy. The history kept in the Other Count, the dated years that are not the spread of grafts, does not agree.

### Lestrand: the Ledger Coast

*(LES-trand · theology low · reach high · governance low)*

Lestrand is a dense, Tree-rich merchant power, a merchant city of the kind a person means by Bruges or Florence, carried as far as markets can go. The Trees are mature and many, the Given are dense, and the faith is worn lightly over deep markets and thin welfare, so that the whole business of becoming is read as a transaction. It shares the Waiting Lands' light-touch governance, and no crown writes anyone down. Where the Lands' light state leaves a gap the faith fills, the Coast's light state leaves a space the guilds fill, completely.

**Law here.** Guild self-rule is carried as far as it goes. The state is a charter-granting council of merchant houses and craft-guilds. It charters the Stilled's guild and the element-guilds, and it watches the guild, never the individual. Citizenship-by-witness runs smoothly here because reach is high. Long-Lived notaries are abundant, Bound oaths close the contracts, and Far-Voiced voices stand on every dock and in every court. A city this size nests that witness as hearths, a quay-gang or a courtyard, rather than as twenty thousand people sharing one memory. The three pillars are not reverence here. They are the machinery that lets a huge commercial city run on trust without a register. Travelers clear on a road-word, a traveler's vouch, or on a guild-mark. The gate asks three questions, a name, a last hearth, and who stands, and any two will do. It does not ask your colour, and it does not keep the answers past dawn. The Struck are vouched in briskly, not as a grace but as efficiency, because an unvouched person is a transaction that cannot clear. Here they call the Struck *the turned*. They call standing-for *warranting*, a commercial guarantee. They call the ticket *the guild-ticket*. The Inviolate Will is cited as sound commercial law, *the free-will clause*. A Bound contract binds property, not persons, and the Coast likes that line because it makes the Bound the witness a written contract wants. They call the Motherfaith *the old rite*, and they wear it light. The Fair Hand, the faith of honest accounts, they call *the Settling*, and on this coast that is ordinary. Its clerks are the Settling-hands.

**Economy here.** This is the richest corner, and the coolest. Deathless banking is fullest here. Deathless houses keep trusted notes that other houses measure their paper against, the nearest thing the world has to a gold standard, and they keep century instruments, loans and endowments on horizons no mortal lender would risk. They endow fortunes rather than leave them as inheritances, at a scale the other lands do not match. The great endowed libraries and universities cluster here, and that money funds the Long-Lived novelty-Tithe, the need for something genuinely new. They call the long-house *the endowment*, or simply *the house*. Self-paying Conditions prosper, the Answered crafts, the Two-Bodied haulage and courier trades, the Stilled surgeons, because their labor is their upkeep and the market pays for the labor. Tithe-provision is a private market, not a public good. You buy your green, and they call the plot *the leaf-lot*. You buy your outlet at the release-house, and that house is also the playhouse. If you can afford it, you stay whole in comfort. The injustice is the market's. The people whose Tithe must be furnished get thin welfare in a rich city, the starkest have-and-have-not gap in the world. The shadow economy, smuggled green, black-market novelty, sold vouching, and off-book Bound contracts feeding the Given-Over, a Bound person bargained down until the counterparty effectively owns them, runs biggest here because the legitimate market is biggest here. The risk the deathless pose is at its most acute. A handful of houses that never die quietly steer the wealthiest polity through patronage that never disperses. After a fee fails, someone rents the week, and they call it week-rent. They call the Kept *the free*, labor with no Tithe to furnish, and they say it with approval. They call the ranging *the beast-yard*, and that yard can be hired. They call tree-wardens *tree-tenders*.

**Daily life here.** The Condition-medicine is superb. The great Stilled infirmaries and the deathless-endowed hospitals are here, in the rich core, for those who can reach them, and the queue outside is the longest here too. Family life shrugs at theology. A marriage market reads Conditions as assets. A Two-Bodied bloodline is a trade dynasty. A Long-Lived match is a century of credit. The aching Long-Lived marriage, grief against advantage, gets weighed as coolly as any other balance. The Turning is a civic and commercial milestone more than a sacrament. A family's Leaf-Fall is watched for what guild the child's colour opens. The life is free, and it is prosperous. An attic-host of the Open Table, the faith that sets an extra place, is a curiosity and a good supper. Sailors print "no one is a stranger twice" on a card, and the Settling will not enforce the card.

**Its injustice.** This is the coolest of the three. Your marriage, your gift, and your faith stay your own, and nothing here is policed, while everything is priced, so a person who cannot pay slides with a whole rich city stepping around them. The Coast will not file you and will not conscript you. It will let you fade if you run out of coin, and it will feel that it owed you nothing.

**The seat.** Orentel *(OREN-tel)* is a salt-quay city on the Old Crossing, the narrow water between the Motherland and this coast. The written name is a worn *oren*, to witness, set beside the Tree, the *th* eroded to *t*. The White Note House sits on the third quay, north side. It keeps terms. It does not govern the coast, and it is not the crown.

**What they remember.** Paper written on the Salt Walk, the old sea-and-land road through this continent, outlived the gate of the Closed Seat, the power that tried to pull the copied Trees back up and then went behind the walls of the Inner Close. The notes outlived the Grey Summer, the quay-fever on the Old Crossing in Cut-year 171, an epidemic and not a Tithe. The rate the first quay still charges is the Hinge Hush. Sirtol *(SEER-tol)* spoke for a pile of Coast charters in that peace, one charter-holder among many, and did not sit a throne. Brenledd, the hearth-league, piled its hearths into one compact rather than be priced one hearth at a time. The White Note is one desk-house, not the polity. The chronicle of that memory is the Other Count.

### Threnmaieth: the Tallied Crown

*(THREN-mai-eth · theology high · reach high · governance high)*

Threnmaieth is a pious, Tree-rich core kingdom that keeps a census of persons and their Conditions, in the wealthiest, most devout, most Tree-dense land in the three corners. The written name sets *thren*, sacred counting, beside *maieth*, the Mother, so the name is the Mother's Reckoning. Here the roll is kept as a holy duty. The Mother's gifts are precious and must be accounted for. That makes it the hardest of the three to resist. The other two have injustices you can name and resent. This one puts its injustice in the two things the world most trusts, faith and plenty.

**Law here.** The Tallied Crown keeps a record held on people. Every subject and their gift is written down, not as the guild rolls and tax ledgers everyone has, but as a census of persons. At home that census is reverent stewardship of the Mother's blessings. Elsewhere it is felt as surveillance. They call it the **Threnhael** *(THREN-hayl)*, *thren* set beside *hael*, whole, so the name sounds like keeping the realm hale. Its clerks say they keep subjects whole. Outsiders hear the Counting. Trenledd keeps a roll of persons too. The difference is that the Tallied Crown calls its roll holy, and Trenledd does not. Other cities nest the witness as a hearth, the neighbours who stood, and then stop. Threnmaieth copies that stand onto the Threnhael, and the copying is the whole difference. On that roll the second name is the house, where other lands would say a surname.

Because reach is high, there is a great deal to count. The Given are dense, and the crown calls them *the marked*. The guilds are dense. Long-Lived memory is abundant, and the crown distrusts it, because that memory is not the crown's. What gives the crown away is that it will not rely on the three pillars the rest of the world runs on, because living memory answers to itself and a ledger answers to the crown. The unvouched are hunted, and the Struck are pursued so they can be catalogued, a pursuit the crown files as *the unwitnessed*. The Bound are watched far past their real hazard. The roster's own clerks know the Inviolate Will means the Whisper cannot command, and the watching is stigma kept as if it were safety. Devotion supplies the justification a census without faith would lack. A steward will say you would not hide a gift from the Mother, so why hide it from her stewards. They call standing-for *the naming*, because you are named to the crown, and they call the ticket *the crown-warrant*, the Inviolate Will *the crown's assurance*, and the Motherfaith *the Reckoned Faith*.

**Economy here.** Tithe-need is turned to leverage, and consecrated. The crown provides the upkeep efficiently, even generously, because a pious, rich state can afford to, and then it holds what it provides. It holds the green, which it calls the tended green, the novelty, which it calls the crown archive, the outlets, which it calls the counted hall, and the Stilled medicine, all of them running through channels the crown can withhold. A population whose staying-whole runs through the state cannot afford to refuse it. That is the most complete control in the world, and here it wears a halo. To receive your provision is to receive the Mother's bounty through her appointed hands, and to be cut off is framed as your lapse, not the crown's lever. They call the furnishing the counted channel. They call the Slide the late week, the week the channel will not admit failed. Videm knows that room is there, and Sirtal files the leak as the person's own lapse. A Two-Bodied person's time as the animal is filed as the ranging-roll. Deathless finance here is superb and centuries deep, but the Long-Lived are courtiers and crown-archivists as much as bankers. Their memory, which cannot be bribed into forgetting, is bent to the census rather than left free to check it.

**Daily life here.** The sacrament and the census are the same hour. A child's Turning is a joyous holy day that is also a state registration. The colour that falls is celebrated with a hymn and recorded by a clerk. Marriages of heritable Conditions, Two-Bodied bloodlines especially, are noticed, encouraged or discouraged, and filed. The medicine is excellent, and it runs through the crown's channels. A household whose bloodline and upkeep are both on file cannot easily refuse the crown anything. That is a blessing said over a house the crown can already see. The Kept are pitied here the way a devout land pities them, and recorded the way this crown records them. The roll says *the unmarked*, or *the passed-over*. They were passed over by the Mother, and the crown notes them as such. Tree-wardens are filed as the Reckoned Hands. The crown wants them named to the Threnhael as officers of the Reckoned Faith, the colours written down and the Speakers working as stewards. Some Speakers cooperate, because they believe they are keeping the realm hale. Some refuse. The Tree witnessed, they say, and a ledger is a second, lesser seeing. Nearness to the First Seat is pressure. The crown does not own the Seat. The college can refuse the roll, and some Speakers do.

**Its injustice.** This is the worst of high devotion and a state that reaches every person, each excusing the other. The devotion makes the surveillance feel like reverence, so that being counted is piety, and the surveillance enforces the devotion, so that refusing the count is impiety. What you meet is a wealthy, faithful, orderly people who genuinely believe that accounting for the Mother's gifts is the highest form of tending them, and that belief makes the control almost impossible to resist. Elsewhere the roll reads as chilling, because only two crowns keep one. Here the faith is what lets it feel like grace.

**The seat.** Maiethlir *(MAI-eth-lir)* stands on the Core-thaw, the snowmelt river in the Sacred Core. *Maieth* is the Mother, and *lir* is the thaw. Its children can Turn at its own Tree, which makes it a Hand, a town with a Tree of its own. Pilgrims still walk on from here to the Awakening Tree, whose name is Thaeloren, and the crown's roll is kept in this town. The seat does not own Thaeloren. The First Seat of the wardens sits in the Motherwood beside the town, and the crown does not own that college either.

**What they remember.** The Two Papers, in Cut-year 67, are what this crown calls the year the reckoning stood. Limrae *(lim-RAY)* opened a desk in a river-town and wrote persons down, because, as they said, a gift you cannot find again is a gift you have already failed to tend. The crown-count starts in that summer. A devout clerk may write Cut-year 387 and Crown-year 320 on the same slate, and the sixty-seven years between them are the split. Maiethvael is the cousin-country that would not be named, the same hymns on the opposite paper. The Inner Close keeps another list, of rank and not of care, and will not share ink. The chronicle is the Other Count.
