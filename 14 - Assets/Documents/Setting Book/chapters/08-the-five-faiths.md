# The Five Faiths

The Motherfaith is the dominant faith of the Turning lands, and it is not the only one. Five lived faiths each answer a different experience the Trees, or the lack of a Tree, actually produce, and what follows is how people live those answers, not a list of gods.

| Faith | Home | A Condition is… | Everyday name | Own name |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **the Leaf-Mother** | everywhere the Trees stand; born on Maiethorn | a **gift chosen** | the Motherfaith | *Maieth* |
| **the Watching** | Maiethorn's oldest districts | a **seeing**, later named a gift | the Watching | *Haelthael* |
| **the Fair Hand** | Strandoren's ports and houses | a **bargain struck** | the Fair Hand | *Leddoren* |
| **the Old Ways** | Heskoren's groves and doors | the **land making its own** | the Old Ways | *Vaeloren* |
| **the Open Table** | Kumbaan, the Sundering Isle | a mainland hunger **they never had** | the Open Table | *Ndeyaan* |

A Condition, in all of these arguments, is a becoming: something a person becomes, set beside the Kind they were born. The Watching, the Fair Hand, and the Old Ways live where the Trees stand, on Maiethorn, Strandoren, and Heskoren, so the friction is inside the settled world, among people who already live with the wood. The Open Table grew on Kumbaan *(koom-BAHN)* with no Tree and no Mother-name. Sailors call that place the Sundering Isle, and a storm-wall of weather, current, and reef keeps it, which is why the grafts never crossed.

Smaller household rites, quay-charms, and Kind-customs stay local, especially the household customs of the Kitsune, the Selkie, and the Tengu, peoples a person is born as. Those charms are not what the five faiths stand on. A household can keep a charm the next street has never heard of, and the five faiths are still the five.

The people who do the daily work are the tree-wardens, who keep the Motherfaith at the Tree; the Watchers, who keep the vigil; the Book-Hands, who witness a signing; the Door-Keepers, who walk the rivers, the stones, and the groves; the Table-Keepers, who keep the supper; and the Shore-Sitters of the isle, who sit the storm-beach and wait, and do not swim out. A fringe of the Long-Lived, the people whose aging has stopped, call themselves the Pourers and keep a cup they call the Poured God, and that cup is not a sixth faith.

## The argument (what a becoming *is*)

Every faith in this world has to answer the same fact, that most people become something they were not born as, and the faiths do not agree on the verb for that becoming.

The people a Tree gives a colour are the Given, and even that name is already an argument: the Motherfaith says the child was chosen, the Watching at the same wood says the child was seen, the Fair Hand says the child was signed, and the Old Ways say the Tree is a late guest at a door the land already kept.

The Struck are the people who change later, at a threshold, often with no Tree nearby, and the faiths do not hear the same door. The Motherfaith is still arguing whether the Leaf-Mother reached that threshold, while the Watching says the threshold was always a place of seeing, the Fair Hand hears a later contract struck in extremity, and the Old Ways call this the oldest becoming, the land's own children.

The Kept are the people who get the hug and no colour, and on the mainland that same hug has four readings: a gift of wholeness, a person passed over, no bargain taken, or a year the land had no door for you. The Open Table does not offer a reading of the hug, because on their account a Condition is a mainland hunger they never had.

The Tithe is the upkeep a becoming still asks, and the faiths read that same upkeep four ways: the Motherfaith calls it the honest weight of a gift, the Watching calls it the cost someone once paid, the Fair Hand calls it the term of the contract, and the Old Ways call it the land's due.

None of these is a villain-theology. Each is a sincere answer to a seam people actually live with: gifts that fit, a reach that runs thin at the edge, the written terms a Bound person carries, the Struck who change with no Tree nearby, and a whole isle that never Turned, that never brought its children under a Tree to become. The Motherfaith's own schism already taught the courtesy the others use. What you do at the rite is what binds, what you believe is what gets argued, and a household that keeps two practices is ordinary rather than a scandal.

## How they live together

People belong by the rite they keep, and the creed can wait. Because the Motherfaith counts you by what you do at the Tree, a Watcher who brings their child, a Settler of the Fair Hand who stands as witness to the colour, and a Kin-speaker of the Old Ways who also walks the rivers, the hills, and the stones can all belong at the same Leaf-Fall. Doctrine fights, and the festival usually does not.

Each fight stays in its own land, inside a district or a port, and it does not become a march of one faith upon another.

On Maiethorn the fight is at the centre. The Watching and the Motherfaith share the same Trees and argue over the blessing spoken there. Threnmaieth, the crown that writes its people down, files the Watchers on the Threnhael *(THREN-hayl)*, the roll whose name means the keeping-whole, as a known, counted dissent. The file calls that dissent the Grown-Over. What follows from the filing is pressure, not a pogrom.

On Strandoren the faith is worn light, so the fight is over the Bound and the books. The Bound are the people who chose a grant and named the terms of it. The Fair Hand and the Motherfaith compete for the same oath-keepers in the same port. Lestrand, the great merchant power of that coast, which people call the Ledger Coast, prices both and polices neither.

On Heskoren the fight is over the grove. The Old Ways were there before the grafts, the Trees set into older ground, and the Motherfaith burns hottest where the gift is scarcest. A dying Tree has two claimants. Vaethorn, the devout power of the Waiting Lands, makes the quarrel tender and sharp, not holy-war loud.

On the Sundering Isle the wall still holds. A Yumboe on the mainland, one of the good people born to that isle, is a guest at everyone else's rite. They keep their supper, and they find the noon-day faiths, the mainland rites kept in the daylight, loud the way a guest who will not sit is loud. The quarrel is already in the port. A host counts a guest as family after two suppers, a clerk wants to know where the person was Turned, and a host will not spend a child's tenth-year week on a Tree.

Most years what actually happens is a household keeping two practices. The hard edge is the hour when the two stop agreeing.

| Pair | Usual mix | Where it breaks |
|---|---|---|
| Motherfaith + Watching | One Tree. Two blessings. | The roll wants one name. |
| Motherfaith + Fair Hand | Festival and signing-watch in one week. | Gift and debt, over one Bound person. |
| Motherfaith + Old Ways | Pilgrimage, and the old doors. | A sick Tree: warden or door-keeper. |
| Mainland + Open Table | A shared supper. Rare. | Watched Turning, or two suppers. |

All five houses tell the Child at Four Doors, and the last line changes. That is how the world argues without a council.

The Leaf-Mother remains a belief in public life, and the other faiths stay beliefs with her. The Watching says it remembers a cost, and what that cost was the house will not agree, so the claim stays unsettled.

## The Watching

The Watching is Maiethorn's heartland heresy, an older Tree-faith the Motherfaith grew over, and the dissent sits at the centre rather than out on a frontier. They keep the Tree and refuse the Mother-name, holding that the colours fall because the Tree sees, and not because a she chooses.

In everyday speech a neighbour says **the Watching**. Their own name is **Haelthael** *(hayl-THALE)*, from *hael* and *thael*, the Tree kept whole. They mean the Tree before anyone split it into a Mother and her hands. The Motherfaith's soft name for them is **the First Watching**, and Threnmaieth's file-name is **the Grown-Over**.

### Shape of the faith

This is a witness-faith, and the founding fact is that the Tree looks, so a colour is a seeing made visible, and not a present wrapped in a leaf. To live well is to name what was seen, rather than to thank a giver you cannot prove.

Their practice is older than the Motherfaith's. They stand at the same Leaf-Fall, and they will not say the Tender's blessing, the line the warden speaks over the child, as the warden says it. The rite still binds them to the town, and the withheld word is the whole dissent.

They will not give the Tree a divine character, neither a mother nor a bargain-partner nor a land-spirit in a new dress, because for them it is the Tree. Naming it *Maieth* is, to them, the overlay, a comfort the sacred centre invented when the seeing needed a face.

You know them by the name they keep for the wood. They keep **Thaeloren**, the Tree that witnesses, and they refuse **Hand of the Mother**, so the wood is the same and the title is the schism.

### Doctrine (as believed)

They share the Motherfaith's picture of the Before, a beginning in which the world was Kind and Kind alone, people as they were born and nothing besides. They part company on what came next: the Tree, they say, was already there and already watching, the Giving came after that Watching, and the Mother-name came last, so that each layer is a story laid over a seeing.

"She chooses well" is, to them, a later sentence. Fittingness is what a witness produces: the Tree sees what you can carry and what the town will need, and the leaf matches the seeing. They call that intent, and they do not call it a mother's. To a Watcher, the skeptics of the Motherfaith are almost right, and still too quick to call the Tree a phenomenon with no mind. There is a mind in the seeing, and it does not owe you a lap.

Haelthael teaching remembers a cost, and holds that the Giving was not free: something was spent, someone was lessened, and the Tree, or whatever stands behind it, does not reach twice by chance, because reaching costs. They will not name a Mother who pretends otherwise. What the cost was, they will not agree, though they agree the cost was real.

The Kept were seen: a hug and no colour is a seeing of wholeness, or a seeing that did not reach, and they argue this as fiercely as the Motherfaith does, without the consolation of a mother's lap. The hug is still a witness, not an oversight, and not, they insist, a slight dressed as love.

The Struck were seen at a harder door, so a drowning, an oath, or a death is an older place of seeing, and the Tree is the ordinary one. The Watching does not need the Wide-Hand and Narrow-Hand split the Motherfaith keeps, the quarrel over how wide a giving hand reaches, because seeing was never confined to a grafted scion, a living cut of the origin planted in a town.

### Practice

The Night Watch is kept the night before Turning-Week. Watchers keep a silent vigil at the town Tree. They speak no blessing and read no colour. They keep watch on the Tree all night, as they believe it watches the town.

The withheld blessing is what they do at the Leaf-Fall itself. They attend, because the practice still holds and their children still Turn, and when the warden says the Tender's line a Watcher household speaks its own, quietly.

> *"What falls, was seen. What is kept, was seen. The Tree does not reach twice by chance."*

The cadence is the same as the Motherfaith's blessing, and the Mother is not in it. Towns that have lived with both wordings for a long time barely notice the difference. Threnmaieth notices.

Name what you saw is the devotion after a Turning. A Watcher does not say "tend what you're given." They sit with the child and describe the fall: the colour, the way the leaf came, and what the year will ask. Devotion, for them, is accurate speech.

In Haelthael liturgy the origin Tree is only Thaeloren. "First Hand" is tolerated as poetry. "Hand of the Mother" is the overlay they will not say.

Watchers are not priests. They keep the Night Watch, they teach the withheld blessing, and, where a town will have it, they offer a second reading of a rare or mixed fall. That reading is a witness alongside the tree-warden. It is not a reading over the warden, and the warden's word stays the town's. In Threnmaieth they are a known column on the roll. The Watching has no mother-church and no seat in the Awakening Tree's shadow, because that seat was grown over by the First Seat. Their oldest rooms are under older chapels, or in houses that never took a warden's blessing on the door. What they keep as offices are the Night Watch and the withheld blessing.

### The live schism

The Masked say the Mother is a later face the Witness learned to wear, or a name people needed and the Tree allowed. The reading sits close to the Motherfaith, and it is common in mixed families. A Masked Watcher will stand for the Tender's blessing and add the withheld line under it.

The Bare Tree says there was never a she. The overlay is a comfort the sacred centre invented, and comfort is not the same thing as truth. This is the harder and smaller reading, and it is the one Threnmaieth files most carefully.

Both branches agree the Giving cost something, and they do not agree what: a wound in the Tree, a year the watching failed, or a someone who became the wood. This is the question they will not close. It is also the one the Motherfaith most wants left unasked.

### An inscription on a door-beam

The inscription was cut on a door-beam, then recut, and found when a warden-chapel on Maiethorn was re-floored. The new beam faces the Tree. This face was turned to the stone.

> *We kept the watch before the name. The wood was already looking. If a mother came after, she came to a work begun. Do not thank the latecomer for the seeing.*

It reads as heresy, stubborn and local, and it claims that the Tree is older than the Mother-name.

## The Fair Hand

The Fair Hand is Strandoren's fortune-and-contract faith. It has no catechism, and it distrusts anyone who offers one. What follows is assembled the way Settlers, the people who keep this faith, assemble what they hold: from working books, settled margins, and clauses copied often enough to become prayer. The faith reads a Condition not as a gift given but as a bargain struck. The world, on this account, is a ledger of deals. The Tithe is a term, not a devotion. When the book is closed you are even, and you do not owe gratitude forever. It is the direct rival to the Motherfaith's gift-language, at home on the trade continent, and especially among the Bound.

In everyday speech the name is **the Fair Hand**. Their own written name is **Leddoren** *(LEDD-or-en)*, from *ledd* and *oren*, the witnessed account. Along the Ledger Coast the slang is **the Settling**. The Motherfaith calls them **the Bargainers**. A Bound person, who chose a grant and named the Terms of it, will say they have **a closed book** once that year's Terms are paid.

Open a quay-factor's book, a factor working the quay, and the line reads: *A grant without a term is a debt whose number the giver keeps.*

Open a Bound mason's book and the line reads: *Asked. Answered. Owed through rain-month. Closed.*

Open a Kept daughter's book, beneath a blank colour-line, the line where a colour would have been entered, and it reads: *Nothing granted. Nothing owed. My mother still thinks this means nothing happened.*

No one entry speaks for the Fair Hand. Together they are closer than an official creed would be.

### Shape of the faith

This is a settling-faith. Nobody is being rescued, and nobody is being given something they cannot refuse, because a gift you cannot put down is, to them, an imposition. The founding ethic is fair terms. Know what you owe, pay it, and walk.

What makes you of the Fair Hand is that you keep accounts honestly: the Tithe, the contract, and the witness. You do not have to deny the Mother in the street. Many Settlers still bring a child to the Tree. They attend as witnesses to a signing.

The faith splits on whether anyone sits across the table as a counterparty, the other party to the deal, or whether the far side of the table is empty, and either way of keeping the book agrees that the table is real. The Leaf-Mother, if she exists, is a giver who will not show her terms, which, to a Settler, is the one unforgivable clause.

The mark of a Settler is a small personal book, and it is not a census, because a census is the sin they lay at Threnmaieth's door. In the book a Settler writes colour, Tithe, and what they are owed. The Bound did not invent this book. They are simply the ones whose book is also a contract.

### Doctrine (as believed)

The Fair Hand holds that nothing is given, and that the Leaf-Fall is a striking of terms. The colour is the grant, the Tithe is the price, and the town's need is the other side of the deal. "Tend what you're given" is how a creditor teaches you to be grateful for the interest.

The Bound are how the faith proves the model. A Bound person chose, named a grant, named Terms, and named a Pledge, the piece of themselves the contract would collect. The Fair Hand says this is not a special horror. It is the same grammar as every becoming, written large enough to read. The Motherfaith's fear of the Bound is, to them, embarrassment. The Bound make the ledger visible.

The Kept, on this reading, declined, or were not offered. No bargain was taken, nothing is owed, and nothing is owed to them, which the faith holds as a cooler dignity than spared or slighted. On Lestrand the same life rhymes with calling the Kept the free. The Fair Hand did not invent that slight. They gave it a theology.

The Struck, they say, signed later, in a storm, because extremity is when people accept bad terms. A drowning, an oath, a death: of course the book opens then. The Struck are not suspects. They are people who bargained without a clerk. The faith's kindness is to settle the year fairly after the fact, by a hall or a warrant: someone vouches, and the civic year is taken up in place of the bargain that had no clerk. A Bound person's Terms are the exception. Those belong to the Counterparty, the other party named in the Bound's own bargain, and not to a clerk. The cruelty is the same motion. A Struck person who cannot settle fades, and the quay steps around them.

What they mean by grace is a closed book. It is not forgiveness, and it is not a mother's lap. The sentence they trust is the one they can say in a breath: you paid, you are even, walk.

### Practice

The signing-watch is what Settlers make of the Leaf-Fall. They attend as witnesses, not as petitioners, and they leave the Tender's blessing to the warden. They speak, if they speak:

> *"Grant and term, seen and spoken. The book is open. Keep it fair."*

Public settling is the Tithe paid where a neighbour can see it, and the act is a clearing, not a confession. A Bound person's oath-day is the high holy day. The Terms are recited, and the year's book is closed or marked owing. The Motherfaith calls this showing off. The Fair Hand calls it the only honest worship.

Quay-blessings are short, worn, and said in a tongue the use has rubbed down. A Settler says "fair wind, fair term" over a hull, a contract, a marriage. The sacred and the commercial use the same sentence, and that is the teaching.

The personal book holds the colour, the Tithe, the debts, and what the year owes you. It stays private. It is not the Threnhael. A Settler will die to keep a crown-clerk out of it. That refusal is the one fury they share with every other faith on the subject.

Book-hands, whom neighbours also call Settlers, are notaries who are also theologians. They witness signings, they teach children how to keep a book, and they settle a Struck person's civic year by a hall or a warrant. They recite a Bound person's Terms on oath-day, and they do not rewrite those Terms, because that clause belongs to the Counterparty and not to a clerk. On Lestrand they are often the same people as the commercial clerks, so the faith is the profession, worn lightly. Two book-hands may refuse each other's settlements and still borrow ink.

The Fair Hand has no seat and many tables. It meets in guild-rooms and quay-shrines, and a high house in a rich port is the oldest book-room. There is no membership roll, because a roll would be a Threnhael.

### The live schism

Settlers do not form two branches, because they write over one another in the same book.

> **Recto:** Something sits across the table — a mind, a power, a hand that is fair rather than mothering. The Bound prove that an answer has an address.
>
> **Verso, in another ink:** A book does not require a reader on the far side. Rain settles downhill without a creditor.
>
> **Lower margin:** Then whom did you pay?
>
> **Trimmed edge:** Paid is paid.

What unites the inks is a practical fury at a hidden term. That is why Settlers cannot join the Motherfaith even when they share a festival. "The Mother does not reach twice by chance" is, to them, a clause with no number on it.

### From a Bound's working contract

The page still in use is a preamble copied from a Bound person's working contract, the form-words a Lestrand book-hand uses when the grant is ordinary and the Terms are not.

> *I was not given. I asked, and I was answered, and I named what I would pay. If a mother had wanted me she would have sent a leaf, not a clause. I prefer the clause. I can read it.*

It is a Bound person's pride, a little cold, and it is the Fair Hand's whole case against gift-doctrine. The Bound person who prefers the clause is sincere. They have felt the cost and named it a price.

## The Old Ways

The Old Ways are the ancestor-and-land faith of Heskoren. The faith predates the grafts. It says the Mother's reach is new here, and the land was already making children of its own, the Struck and the Taken-In, at rivers, drowning-pools, oath-stones, and groves. A Taken-In is a person the wild took partway into itself and gave back changed. The Trees, on this account, are guests. They are not the source.

Everyday, and in the house's own common speech, the name is **the Old Ways**. The liturgical name is **Vaeloren** *(vay-LOR-en)*, from *vael* and *oren*. They use **vael** in an older sense. It is not "a Condition Given," a becoming handed down as a leaf-colour, but **what the land yields**. They say the Motherfaith narrowed the word until it meant only a leaf-colour. Pious speech in Vaethorn calls them **the Before-Faith**. The Taken-In among them say **the Green Kin**.

### Shape of the faith

This is a land-and-kin faith, and the founding fact is that place makes people. A river that has drowned someone and returned them, a hill that took someone in, and an oath-stone that heard a vow are **the old doors**. The grafted Tree is a new door, and it is welcome if it behaves.

You keep the Old Ways by walking, not by watching a colour fall. You go to the doors, you feed the kin who stayed, and you stand for the Struck when a town will not. Belief about the Mother comes second. Many Kin-speakers also bring a child to a healthy graft when they can reach one.

What they hold as divine is many, and the dead stay. It is not one mother, and it is not a ledger. It is hills, waters, old trees, and the people who went into them. The old trees are the wild kin of motherwood, the wood of the Turning Trees, and the grafts are the planted ones. Ancestors here are not ghosts to be feared. The Returned, people who died with something unfinished and got up walking, come through a different door. The ancestors of this faith are kin who remained in the ground.

You know them by a worn token from their door: a river-stone, a grove-twig, or a pinch of hill, carried the way a Settler carries a book. No two tokens look alike, and that difference is the theology.

### Doctrine (as believed)

The land was first. Before grafts, and before pilgrimage, Heskoren's people already became things other than the people they were born. The Struck are not a problem the Motherfaith has failed to place. They are the oldest rite, the land doing what the land does, and the Taken-In are the grove's own children rather than a frontier embarrassment.

Vael meant yield: grain, game, a child returned from water, a person the hill kept. The Motherfaith's vael, a Given Condition, is a special case they mistook for the whole word. To call a leaf a vael is fine. To say that only a leaf is a vael is a theft of speech.

Grafts are guests. A healthy Tree is a new kin-house. You may tend it, and you may Turn under it, and you may not pretend it invented becoming, or that a town without one is empty of the sacred. A sickening Tree is a guest who is dying in your grove. The fight with the tree-wardens is who is host.

The Kept are a year the land had no door. The Old Ways will not call that spared, or slighted, or free labour. Some years a door does not open for you, and you are still the land's, whether the opening comes next year, in the drowning-year, or never. The waiting that Vaethorn sanctifies as pilgrimage is, here, just weather.

The Mother is a late name. Either the land is wearing a face the newcomers needed, which is the Guest-reading, or the name is a claiming, which is the First-reading. Both readings agree she did not arrive first.

### Practice

Threshold-walking happens at the year's hinges, the turns of the year, and not only at the High Solstice. People walk the old doors of their district. They greet the water, the hill, and the stone, they leave food, and they do not ask for a colour. They ask to be known.

Ancestor-meals set a place at the board for the ones in the ground. The meal is quiet and domestic, easy to miss as religion if you came from Maiethorn looking for a chapel, and the courtesy is the rite.

Standing for the Struck is what they do when a person changes later and alone. The Old Ways do not take that person to a Tree to be acknowledged as Given. They take them back to the door that made them, if it can be found, and name them kin. The Motherfaith's quiet receiving-rite and this one sometimes happen in the same week, for the same person, and the argument is which name goes first.

The contested grove is an ailing graft standing in an old Taken-In green. That is the fight everyone knows: warden against door-keeper, guest against host, the scarcity that sends people on pilgrimage against the sentence "we had ways." It is a quarrel between neighbours who both love the wood.

Door-keepers, whom neighbours also call Kin-speakers, know which doors still open. They speak at ancestor-meals, and they stand for the unvouched Struck, the ones no one has yet stood for, when they can. The standing is a reputation more than a hierarchy. A famous door-keeper is the one three valleys will walk to, and the two names often belong to the same person. The land is the seat, so there is no mother-church to sit in. Vaethorn's tree-wardens find this infuriating and, in a dry year, quietly useful. The door-keepers walk the doors. They do not carry scions.

### The live schism

The Guest-reading says the Mother is the land wearing a face the newcomers needed, the land in a new dress, and the grafts are kin. It is common in Vaethorn towns that finally got a Tree and did not want to throw their grandparents out of the rite. They will make the pilgrimage, and they will walk the doors.

The First-reading says the grafts are a claiming, and the old doors were enough. It lives in the thinner-reach districts, the ones still waiting on a scion that will not take. They will tend a dying graft as a guest in their house, and they will still refuse the Tender's blessing.

Both branches revere the Struck and the Taken-In, and that shared reverence is what the faith stands on. A reading that started pitying them as unreached has already become Motherfaith.

### Said at a drowning-stone

Said at a drowning-stone on Heskoren's wet margin, the year after a graft sickened and a child came back from the river Changed, Struck by the water and carrying no leaf:

> *The tree is young. The water is not. If a mother sent the water she sent it before she sent the leaf, and she has not told the wardens. We will feed both. We will not let either pretend it was first.*

It is frontier courtesy, a little barbed, and it is the Old Ways' whole case. They have correctly felt that becoming happens at doors where there is no healthy Tree.

## The Open Table

Kumbaan's faith grew with no Tree and no Mother-name. The good people keep the night, set a place for hands that have no bodies, and hold that a stranger at the table is the holiest thing in the world, holier than a colour, a graft, or a bargain. The Yumboe, which is the mainland's name for them, are the hill-folk of that isle, born into the shape rather than Turned into it. They stand about waist-high to a mainlander, pearl-pale, silver-haired, and they call themselves the good people. The bodies are warm. The ancestral idiom lives in the hands, not in the people.

On the mainland a neighbour says **the Open Table**, and a sailor says **the Moon-Feast**. The house says **Ndeyaan** *(ndey-AHN)*, the night-keeping. In full courtesy the name is **Bakhna Ndeya**, the good night. The opening of every meal that matters is already their creed. Someone at the table says "No one is a stranger twice."

### Shape of the faith

Ndeyaan is a hospitality-faith, and the founding fact is the table. To feed and to be fed is how the living sit with the staying-kin, the hands that remained, and with anyone the storm-wall did not keep out. There is no Leaf-Fall, because, as they say, there was nothing missing to give.

The rite is the meal. Ndeyaan has almost no clergy and no chapel, because the rite is supper under the moon, a place set for hands you can see and for a body you cannot, with daylight reserved for sleep. A Yumboe who keeps this on the mainland is still of the Open Table. A mainlander who sits once is a guest. A mainlander who sits twice is kin.

What they hold as divine is the hill, the moon, and the ones who stayed. It is not a mother, and it is not a ledger. The dead do not leave. They stay as hands. Whether those hands are the dead, which is the Staying, or the hill's own courtesy, which is the Serving, is the house-argument, and neither reading makes the good people into ghosts.

You can know a house of the faith by an extra place laid, even when the household is poor. The unseen hands are what make that place possible. That is not a metaphor they use. It is the furniture.

### Doctrine (as believed)

Wholeness is the default. The mainland invented a hunger and called it a gift. On Kumbaan you are born Yumboe and that is enough. If the good people become anything else, they do not become it at a Tree, because there is none. How any other becoming happens is not a doctrine they owe a stranger. It is a family matter, and they are not in a hurry.

The dead stay to serve. Folklore on the mainland calls Yumboes spirits of the dead. They find this rude and, in a sideways way, nearly true, because the hands are the staying-kin. The living host, and the staying pour. A feast that outshines its means is a theological statement, because the household is larger than the bodies you can count.

The moon wakes both, so night is the shared day of the living and the staying, and daylight is a Sabbath of sleep, a day kept for rest, and not a fallen hour. Mainland noon-faiths are loud the way a guest who will not sit is loud.

A stranger is the sacrament. The storm-wall keeps the world out, and anyone who crosses it has already been through drowning-water, so you do not test them with a colour. You feed them. The second supper is the rite of belonging. This is why a lone Yumboe on Strandoren becomes a host wherever they land, still keeping Ndeyaan with whatever table they can make.

The Mother, as they hear her, is a story about a quiet table, told too loud. They do not hate her. They have never met her. They do not understand why a people would need to be chosen in order to be wanted.

### Practice

The moon-feast is the ordinary high rite: night, a hill-hall or open turf, the extra place, and the unseen hands fetching and pouring. There are songs. There is no colour-reading, and there are no terms recited.

The place is laid even in a poor house. To omit it is the one real impiety, not because the staying starve, since they do not, but because you have counted the household too small.

"No one is a stranger twice" is spoken at the second supper. The first night you are a guest, and a guest is already sacred. The second night you are of the table. Mainlanders who hear this as a proverb have missed that, on Kumbaan, it is a legal sentence in all but parchment. It is spoken, and it is not written down.

Daylight sleep is rest. The glare is not holy, and it is not cursed. It is when the good people and the staying-kin rest. A Yumboe abroad will still take their long rest when the moon is down, if they can, and they will not make a scene about it.

Table-keepers, whom neighbours also call hill-hosts, are almost not clergy. The office is the person whose hall the valley comes to on the bright nights, and the standing is a reputation rather than a title. They have one job, which is to keep the table.

Shore-sitters, whom neighbours also call wall-waiters, are the other isle job. They sit the storm-beach and wait. Swimming out is outside the job. The living go uphill to a table.

A Yumboe abroad is a table-keeper by necessity. One person can keep Ndeyaan in a Lestrand attic, and that is how the faith travels, when it travels at all. It recruits by who will lay the extra place, and not by Kind. Kumbaan sends no mission.

### The live schism

The Staying says the unseen hands are the dead: grandparents, the drowned, the ones the hill kept. The reading is ancestral and literal, older in the tellings, and still common in the deep-hill halls. It is not a ghost-cult. The dead are family who took the serving-work.

The Serving says the hands are the hill's own courtesy, the good people's oldest wonder, and not a roster of names. This reading is gentler. It is the majority in the halls that face the surface, and it is easier to explain to a mainland guest who has gone pale.

Both branches set the extra place. A Serving host who will not say the dead's names, and a Staying host who will not stop saying them, will still pass the same cup. The argument is for after the dishes.

### Two suppers, or a Turning-witness

On Kumbaan, "no one is a stranger twice" is how you belong. On the mainland, you belong because a town watched you Turn. A host of this faith counts a guest as family after two suppers. A town clerk wants to know where the person was Turned.

This is the faith's edge on the mainland. It does not need a mission, and it does not need a chapel on Strandoren. It needs a room, a tenth year, and a clerk who counts belonging a different way.

The everyday collision is citizenship. A guest sits a second night in a Lestrand attic. The host says the sentence and means it. A quay clerk, a Book-hand, or a warden passing through wants a Turning-witness, someone who saw the person Turn, before they will treat that person as of a place. Inside the room the guest is kin. Outside the door the same person is still a guest. Neither office is cruel, and the sentence does not mean the same thing outside the room.

The week that can close is the Given-door. A child of the table, whether isle-raised, convert-raised, or a sailor's tenth summer in a port, reaches Turning-Week. The host will not walk them to a Turning Tree, because in the host's telling they were never lacking, and a neighbour or a town-warden will. The Given-door is one week. If the argument outlasts Eolthael *(ay-ohl-THALE)*, the Turning-month, the child is unTurned. Struck doors still open later. That is not a comfort the host asked for, and it is not a kindness the warden can file. This is not a crusade on Kumbaan. The child is already on the mainland, and the storm-wall stayed where it was.

When a tenth year is in the house, the extra place is set slightly back, and the host says the sentence or refuses to take the child down to the Tree in the morning. A warden, a clerk, or a neighbour is in the street with a list of this year's children. In Hale-month, the worn name of the spring month before the Turning, the same list is manners. In Eolthael it is a child's life.

**Mbela** *(MBEH-lah)* keeps an attic off a Lestrand salt-stair and will not spend a child's week on a colour. **Sorel** of the quay will not file a second supper as belonging, and will walk a tenth-year neighbour to the Tree if the host will not. They are not enemies. They count belonging differently, and they share a stair.

Romanticizing the Moon-Feast is itself a slight. Printing the sentence on a luck-card is how Lestrand files the whole faith. The woman in the attic still sets the place.

### A place-setting blessing

Said to the empty plate before the hands arrive, taught to children, and heard once, badly copied, by a Strandor sailor who did come back.

> *We have counted you. Sit. If you have a name we have not learned it yet; if you have a colour we have no tree to read it. The moon is enough light. Eat, and be the household.*

It reads as hospitality, almost twee, and it also says that being wanted never depended on a colour. The sailor who copied it thought it was a grace. What was copied is a claim that being wanted was never a gift.

Kumbaan keeps the moon, and not the solstice. The Yumboe reckoning is nocturnal and lunar, and it owes the Trees nothing, because they have none. The High Solstice, the mainland's holiest week, is not a hinge on the isle.

## The Child at Four Doors

A teaching-story told in every house. The bones of it stay the same, and the last line does not, so the story carries the argument of the five faiths in a shape that will not resolve. A Turning, in this telling, is the year a child comes to the one door the town said would be there. Children hear the ending that belongs to their own house first. Travellers hear the other endings, and they do not sleep well.

### The story (as told)

A child came to the year of their Turning and found **four doors** where the town said there was one.

The first door was a Tree, and a leaf hung over it, coloured, waiting.
The second door was a book, open, a line already drawn, a space for a name.
The third door was a river-stone, wet, older than the road.
The fourth door was a table with a place set, and no one sitting in it.

The child asked which door was theirs.

No one would say. Each keeper only said: *this one opens.*

The child sat down in the middle and waited to be chosen, or seen, or signed, or fed.

They waited a long time.

When they looked up, all four doors were open, and the child was still in the middle, and the year had gone on without a colour, or with one — the tellings do not agree — and the child had to stand, and pick, and did, and the story ends before you see which.

### The last lines (by house)

The bones above are common. What a house adds, once the bones are done, is the faith that house keeps.

- **Motherfaith (Tender):** *The Mother had opened the Tree before the child sat down. The waiting was the hug. They were wanted either way.*
- **Motherfaith (Wanting):** *The Mother opened one door and the child was looking at the other three. That is how a person is passed over: by having too many doors.*
- **The Watching:** *The Tree had been looking the whole time. The other three doors were people talking. The child was seen. That was the Turning. The leaf was optional.*
- **The Fair Hand:** *The child signed by sitting. Waiting is a term. Someone should have told them the price of a year in the middle.*
- **The Old Ways:** *The stone was wet before the Tree was planted. The child was already the land's. The waiting was weather.*
- **The Open Table:** *The place was set. The child was the guest, then the household. There was never a test. The other doors are how hungry people decorate a supper.*

Skeptics of every faith tell a seventh ending, which is not kind and is very short: *There were not four doors. There was a child, and a year, and a town that needed a story.*

### Variants

On Maiethorn the four doors are carved on the underside of old door-beams, and the wardens say the carving is decoration. On Strandoren the story is a quay-song, and it has a chorus the Bound like to sing: *name the term or get up.* On Heskoren the tellers put the river-stone door first and the Tree third, and some of them leave the book out entirely. On Kumbaan the only door in the story is the table. The other doors are "mainland verses," and people tell them as a curiosity after the dishes, if a guest insists on hearing them. A school-version told in Threnmaieth adds a clerk who writes all four doors down on a roll. The children are not meant to laugh. They do.
