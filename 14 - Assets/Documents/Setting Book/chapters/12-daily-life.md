# Daily Life

Most families in the Turning are ordinary, and that is the first thing to understand about them. Two people marry. Some of the household carry a **Condition**, the change that arrives across a life, and some do not. The children have not Turned yet, and will not until their tenth year. A **Kind** is what a person is by birth, the ancestry they arrived in, and a Condition stacks onto that Kind. It can arrive years later. A **Tithe** is the upkeep a Condition keeps requiring, and it shows up in a house as a cost, a noise, or a door left waiting. A wife born a Selkie, one of the seal-people, a husband Returned from a drowning, a Kept grandmother, three children who are simply children so far: this is not a remarkable household. It is the household. Because nearly every family is mixed in that way, mixed families are not a category anyone thinks about. There is no unmixed household to marry away from, so nobody treats the mix as a departure, and nobody keeps a separate name for it.

### Family, the whole spectrum

Family life spreads along a range, and the spread itself is the point.

At the warm, mundane end, which is most families, a Condition is a texture of the household the way a trade or a temperament is. A Two-Bodied parent's other self has a name, and the children know it. A child says, "That's Bramble, mother's otter," and grows up with that other self as much a part of the family as a pet that is also a person. A Far-Voiced household is a loud one, because the Tithe means feeling must be aired each day. You do not get to sulk quietly in a home with a Far-Voiced in it, and families learn to treat the evening's singing, or weeping, or laughing, as ordinary weather. A Taken-In parent keeps the windows open and the rooms green. These are quirks rather than tragedies, the small daily accommodations of living with people who are also in the middle of what they have become.

In the middle, the Tithe becomes a shared family labor. Where a Condition is provided-for, kept whole by something the household has to furnish, keeping a family member whole is a household expense and a household duty. The green for the Taken-In, the books for the Long-Lived, the outlet for the Far-Voiced. Families organize around it the way a household organizes around a member's chronic need, mostly without drama, sometimes with strain, and with the quiet knowledge that to let a loved one's Tithe lapse is to watch them slide toward the harm that Tithe was holding back. A family too poor to furnish a Tithe carries a particular grief. They can see exactly what their kin needs, and they cannot buy it.

At the aching end sits the marriage ordinary life keeps returning to: loving someone whose span of years is not yours. Chief among these is marrying a Long-Lived. Their aging simply stops. Their spouse's does not. A Long-Lived can have children and a full family, but the Condition is not reliably inherited. Most children age and die like anyone, so a Long-Lived does not build a deathless dynasty. They outlive it. They watch a spouse grow old across a marriage that stays, on one side, unchanged. They bury their children. The old saying is that the cost of the Long-Lived is measured in funerals, and it is a cost paid inside the family, at the dinner table, over decades. A Long-Lived who has done this once faces the choice their whole existence turns on. They can stop, live on alone, and keep no more households. Or they can begin again, marry again, love again, and outlive that family too. Neither choice is cowardly, and neither is free. The grief lives in the most ordinary place there is, which is a marriage.

A few threads run through that range, and each of them is a way a house already knows its own people.

A Long-Lived never crosses another's home threshold uninvited. It is held as the deepest good manners, the mark of someone safe to let in. Inside a family this softens into something tender. To be welcomed across a Long-Lived's own threshold, or to welcome them across yours without a thought, is a real intimacy. A door held open is how this world says you are mine and I am yours. A door refused is felt more than a Long-Lived will ever say.

A person who died and Returned comes home, and contrary to what an outsider expects, they are not cold. They are calm, deliberate, unafraid, and quietly respected, and their loved ones get them back. The chill comes only if the Purpose lapses. The Purpose is the unfinished thing that holds them here, and a Returned who neglects it runs cold. The living can feel the wrongness at a touch. So a Returned's family learns a hard and intimate literacy. You can tell, by an embrace, whether the one you love is tending what holds them here. A warm embrace means they are well. A cold one is a family emergency dressed as a hug.

Two-Bodied bloodlines are family lines. The Condition runs in blood, and children of a Two-Bodied parent usually Turn the same animal, so wolf-folk, hawk-folk, and otter-folk are real extended families. The family animal, the Condition, and often the family trade descend as a single inheritance. Marrying into such a line is marrying into a shape your children will likely wear.

Every parent lives the same open question until the child's tenth year: what will they become, or will they be Kept? In a devout home a parent will say the Mother will give what the child can carry. In an anxious one the decade is quiet dread, or ambition. Parents who carry a heritable Condition, Two-Bodied especially, can half-guess. Most cannot. The Leaf-Fall, the rite inside Turning-Week when the child stands under the Tree, is therefore not only the civic hour of the year but the emotional hinge of a family's life, the day you finally meet who your child has been becoming all along. In the Walking Years, when a Giving still required the walk to Thaeloren, the Awakening Tree, the question had a prior, which was whether the household could get there at all. A household that could walk only one child still lives in the present as two siblings, one Given at the origin and one UnTurned, who never stood under a Tree. Folk still call that UnTurned child Kept. The law does not.

Mixed-Kind children are allowed. Two mainland Kinds may sit in one body, the way the rules already allow a mixed ancestry, and a fox-and-seal child, Kitsune and Selkie together, is as ordinary as a Returned husband. Nobody files the marriage. Nobody calls it marrying out. The live question is whose kitchen the summer before they Turn, not whether the marriage was permitted. Yumboe, the people of the Sundering Isle, are the exception. A Yumboe at the table needs the GM's leave, because they are mythic-rare off the isle, and if one is seated they are the full Kind, never a mix. A child of a Yumboe and a mainlander is a full Yumboe, under that same leave, or the mainland Kind, with at most a story people tell about the child. There is never a half.

### Medicine: miraculous, and strange to outside eyes

Medicine in the Turning is astonishing for a late-medieval world, a world of guilds and bone and herb with no anaesthetic worth the name, and it is astonishing for one reason. The work a Condition makes possible shows up hardest at the sickbed and the deathbed. Two Conditions turn a clinic into something a world of this technology should not have. To anyone standing outside it, the room holds wonder and wrongness together.

The Stilled are the surgeons. A stiller's Gaze arrests motion. It holds a wound from bleeding, slows a poison's spread through the blood, and keeps a patient perfectly still under the knife without holding them down. It does not petrify, and it does not harm. It simply stops what is moving, for a beat. In a world that cannot halt a hemorrhage any other way, this is the difference between a survivable operation and a death. The best surgeons in the world are Stilled, and a great infirmary is built around them. Sit in that room as an outsider and watch what it is. A surgeon's flat, patient gaze freezes the blood in the vein on command, and the surgeon works fast because the Grey is filling as they work. The Grey is the stillness the Gaze leaves in the surgeon, the clock of their Tithe, and a Grey left full locks their own limb to stone. They drill and spar between patients to burn it off. The most gifted healers in the world are also the most tightly licensed and quietly feared. The same gaze that saves you on the table can, misused, hold a person helpless, and it is the one the law watches hardest. Wonder and dread, one person, one room.

The Returned do the work that kills the living. Plague wards, mine collapses, the recovery of bodies from places the living cannot safely go, and the tending of the dead: this is Returned work, done by those death cannot take a second time until their Purpose is met. A Turning mortuary is staffed by the Returned, people who have been dead, calmly and well. A plague ward that would empty a town's living physicians is walked by Returned who are unshaken by rot and contagion. To an outsider this is ghoulish. Inside, it is simply respected. The Returned are the reason a plague does not take the doctors too, and everyone knows it. There is a real tenderness in it as well. No one speaks for the dead, or tends them, as gently as someone who has been dead.

The rest of medicine is ordinary, and ordinarily unequal. Midwives, bonesetters, herbalists, and the Answered's craft turned to apothecary and instrument-making do the everyday care. More of the population carries a useful becoming, so that everyday care is better staffed than a town of this technology would otherwise manage. The care that makes the world exceptional is not evenly available. The great Stilled infirmaries and the deathless-endowed hospitals stand in the rich, Tree-dense core. A frontier town makes do with a travelling stiller if it is lucky, and with cloth, a rope, and a prayer if it is not. The deepest inequity is the one the economy already names. You can be too poor to stay whole, and the medicine that treats a lapsing Condition is the medicine the poor can least reach. The wonder is real. So is the queue outside it.

### Living in a Turning town: city design

A city here is shaped by a fact no other world's city has to build for. Most of its adults have an upkeep, and some of those upkeeps are dangerous or expensive to meet. So the town builds for the Tithe the way it builds for water and waste, as civic infrastructure, visible and load-bearing.

Every town is organized around its Turning Tree. It is the civic and sacred heart, the site of the year's most-attended week, the thing the oldest quarter grew up around. You can read a town's age and standing in its Tree. A mature grafted Tree, grown from a cutting of an older one, means an old town where the giving runs thick. A young Tree, or none, means a new settlement or a frontier one. Geography and the street plan both point back to it. Older uses sit in the same square, a stone, an upper room, a road that ends at the boughs. If you want one of those squares under your feet, Harrow's Green is the neighbour's canopy on Heskoren's live front, and new towns still say they Turned at Harrow's Tree before they had their own. The three hamlets past the ford can see that green from the water, and they still have to send their ten-year-olds on. Further out, the Third Hearth is the road-house on the Near Mile that stayed an inn, the bed still made, and Ornsael is the well-town on the Rain-Shadow, where the drink that made people stop is leaving. When the question is a whole polity rather than one square, the seats are Eolvaeth in the Waiting Lands, Orentel on the Ledger Coast, and Maiethlir under the Tallied Crown.

A village watches a Turning as one circle. A city of tens of thousands does not pretend to be one. Turning-Week is still the civic festival, and the legal witness is the hearth that brought the child, the street or the quay-gang or the courtyard. The square is full. The memory is nested.

Where the polity treats provision as a public good, the town furnishes it in the open, and these buildings are as ordinary in the street as a well or a market cross. They are wells, not granaries. Grain still feeds the city. The lots and the halls keep a minority whole, and they keep a Cry, a Far-Voiced voice breaking loose, out of the market.

Everyday speech calls the venting-hall the **ael-hall**, pronounced AYL, "the airing." It is part concert hall and part a place to let the voice out, and it often is the town's performance venue as well. In Vaethorn, the Waiting Lands, the same building is the gift-hall. On Lestrand, the Ledger Coast, it is the release-house, and it is also the playhouse. In Threnmaieth, the Tallied Crown, it is the counted hall.

The greens are the potted groves and the garden-commons, the living plants a Taken-In in the city rests on so they do not go to seed indoors. Everyday speech just says **the greens**. A devout town in the Waiting Lands calls the same ground the Mother's green, after the Mother the devout name, and holds it in common. On the Ledger Coast it is the leaf-lot, rented by the season. Under the Tallied Crown it is the tended green, because the crown is who furnishes it.

The long-house of books is the endowed library. It feeds a Long-Lived's need to learn something genuinely new, and a Long-Lived is often the person who built it. In the Waiting Lands that gift is called the deathless gift, because the patron does not die. On the Ledger Coast the money set aside is the endowment, or simply the house. The Tallied Crown calls the shelves the crown archive, and the books sit with the crown.

Out at the edge, **the ranging** is the commons where the Two-Bodied can be their other selves. The Waiting Lands call it the wild-leave, which is leave to go out into the wild. The Ledger Coast calls it the beast-yard and hires the yard. The Tallied Crown calls it the ranging-roll, because someone writes down who went.

The night-watch and the ward-watch are part of the same street. They hold a person until morning, and at a gate they ask three things: a name, the last hearth, and who stands. They are not police sent after a Condition.

The work that can harm a bystander sits where such work always sits, at the margins. Stilled infirmaries and Answered workshops, fire and forge and tide, are guild-quartered and set slightly apart. The Returned's mortuaries and plague-work sit at the town's edge, near the dead. They sit there for the same reason a tannery sits downwind, and not from disgust.

Ordinary houses bend quietly to what the people in them have become. Windows open, and rooms stay green, for a Taken-In guest. An evening's rhythm is built around a Far-Voiced's vent. A threshold is a place a Long-Lived waits to be asked across. You can read the household in the house.

Core and frontier show in how much of that provision is there at all. Dense, Tree-rich towns are thick with it. Frontier settlements have little of it and improvise. How much of a town's Tithe-provision is public, private, or simply missing tells you where that town sits.

### How polities vary (domestic faces)

Everyday life varies on the same three choices as the law and the economy: how gift-devout the place is, how many healthy Trees it has, and how far the state reaches into a house. Three stances recur.

**The Warm** *(high theology).* In a Warm house the gift-theology is simply how the family talks. A child's Turning is a joyous sacrament, a mixed household is read as blessings compounded, the Tithe is furnished in the open, and the sick are tended as people whose gifts must be kept whole. The soft injustice reaches the same hearth. The Kept child is pitied for being passed over, marrying "beneath your gift" is quietly frowned on, and the family is expected to spend its members' Conditions as the community sees fit, so the love and the pressure arrive in the same kindness.

**The Watchful** *(governance at its extreme).* A crown that keeps a roll of persons, as the Tallied Crown and the Worn Count do, does not stop at the door. Marriages of heritable Conditions may be noticed and then encouraged or discouraged, a child's Turning is an event the state records, and the medicine that furnishes a Tithe runs through channels the crown controls. The cold injustice is that domestic surveillance. A household whose upkeep and bloodline are both on file cannot easily refuse the crown anything.

**The Frontier** *(low reach).* Infrastructure, medicine, and ceremony all run thin, so families improvise their own Tithe-provision or do without, and a travelling stiller is a rare blessing. The Taken-In, whom core cities keep at arm's length, are neighbours and kin here, the backbone of the place. No one polices your marriage or your gift. A lapsing Condition may have no infirmary to reach, and an untrained Struck child, someone the change came to outside a guild, may have no guild to catch them. The feeling runs warm. The net under a lapse is thin.

A place's daily texture is set by all three choices at once. A devout frontier holds joyous Turnings with almost no infirmary behind them. A secular Tree-rich republic has superb medicine and a marriage market that shrugs at theology. A Watchful-and-pious core turns the sacrament of a child's Turning into a state census with a hymn over it.

### When the town buries

A funeral is the hour a street has a body to carry. It is a walk, and then a meal, and then a door. It is not a Turning. Nobody asks the Tree what the dead person was, and nobody stands the body under the branches for a colour.

The body is washed in the house, often by someone who is Returned, because that labour is already their street. The family still chooses the cloth. Then the neighbours carry the body. In a town with a Tree, the carry goes past the canopy, along the street the person used when they were alive, and the body is not stood under the boughs. A tree-warden may walk with the house. If the house wants a name said, the warden says Maieth, the short name the devout use for the Leaf-Mother, and says it the way a neighbour would. If the house has asked for silence, the warden walks without speaking. Both of those walks are the same office.

Neighbours bring food to the door, and the door stays open through the meal. The person who is going to sleep in the house that night is the one who shuts it, or else the person the house has asked.

A Long-Lived who loved the dead waits on the step until someone says come in. That waiting is already how a household treats a deathless life, a life long enough that the funerals in it have to be counted, and this hour is one of them. It is still a door and a loaf.

Because a mixed house is the usual house, the meal is mixed, and each person does their own small rite at their own bowl. Nobody stops the eating to argue which name ought to come first. That argument can have the time after the dishes. A Motherfaith sibling says Maieth over their own bowl and means the life that has ended. A sibling of the Watching does not thank a giver. They talk about the dead as they were at the stall in Gift-month, the summer month after Turning-Week, when the colours have settled: the coat, the argument, the way they counted change.

If the dead kept a book, a Settler of the Fair Hand closes it on the table, where the room can see the last line, and does not write a new one. A kin-speaker of the Old Ways sets a little of the meal at the threshold, and if the dead kept those ways, the rest of the food goes to the door once the street has finished. Ancestors in that rite are kin who stayed in the ground. A return is a different matter, a Returned person still living a neighbour's life, and you do not summon one. The food at the threshold is the funeral. It is not a calling.

Nobody Speaks a fall. To Speak, at a living child's Leaf-Fall, is to say aloud whether the Tree gave a colour or a hug. Speaking a fall over the dead would mean naming a colour as if the Tree were being asked to answer, and the room does not do it. A neighbour who tries is walked outside by whoever is nearest the pot.

On a dock, if the dead person worked the water at Orentel, the gang carries the box along the quay. The box does not go under Tallo, the quay's name for its Tree, to be read. Some of the gang say "fair wind, fair term," because that is the sentence they already have for a leaving. Some of them will not say it, because they keep that sentence for a hull that is still going out. Both of those things happen in the same hour, and the gang does not stop the carry to settle which is right. If there is a book, it is closed where the gang can see that it has been closed. Salt sits on the Tree's roots in Eolthael, Turning-month, the month of the High Solstice. People say it is the tide, and the tide does not reach the rise. Orentel will not say why the salt is there. The salt is not part of this hour.

Eolvaeth does not send off to a Tree-town to borrow a warden it does not have. If the warden is already in the vale, that warden may walk with the house. If the town sent its children somewhere else to Turn that summer, the town buries its dead with the people who stayed. The pot that feeds the mourners is the gift-hall's, and the food goes to the door the dead person kept. A wet leaf lies on the spring, or too many, or none. The leaf is the spring's business. It is not a funeral token. They will not say why it is there.

At Ndenjoo the body is carried above the tide, and the hall does not lay a second empty plate for the dead. The dead person's own cup is set in the place that was already laid. It sits there for one night, and then it goes back onto the shelf. Whether anyone speaks a name is the argument the valley already knows how to have, and that argument waits until the dishes are done. The shore does not come up the hill to invent a rite on the sand.

### The guest-meal

The bowl is set down before anyone asks the guest who they are. That order travels from town to town. Everything after the bowl is the stance the street was already keeping.

In Maiethlir, in a kitchen on a thaw-street, there is bread and there are greens, and the river is loud enough that people can talk over it. The guest is a walker come from the vale, with a letter and with wet shoes. They sit, and they eat, and only then does someone ask, "Of where?" The guest says the vale. Nobody asks what colour they are, or whether any leaf fell for them at all. The host says Maieth over the host's own bowl, and does not hand that name to the guest to say. A Watcher in the same kitchen tells the guest what is in the pot, greens and a bone and too much salt, and then stops. A Settler at the end of the bench does not open a book. The only thing waiting to be settled is the letter, and the letter can wait until the shoes are dry.

At Orentel the first bread is eaten standing, at a barrel, where the crane can be seen. If you sit down before that bread, you are claiming a berth you have not paid for. After the bread, if no paper is open, there is a chair, and they ask which berth the guest holds. The guest has none, and the guest still eats. If a filing house has a paper open, the house-name is spoken before anyone offers the chair. A house-name is an old given name that stuck to a family line, that place's own word, and it is not a surname brought in from somewhere else. While the paper is open, a clerk gets the house first and then the given name. Shut the paper, and the quay goes back to asking where you are from. The bowl does not wait for the house to finish its paper. A book-hand who wants the guest's affairs written in ink is told, by the person who cut the bread, to come back and do that writing on the quay in the morning.

The guest who turns up in Eolvaeth is often the visit turned around: a clerk from the core who came to see a week and found a pot. If the house is thin, that clerk is fed out of the gift-hall. Nobody promises the guest that the wood will speak. If the clerk asks about the leaf on the spring, someone passes the bread instead of answering. The leaf stays unexplained, and they will not say why. A walker who arrived with a cohort, the group the town sent on to another Tree, eats with that cohort. The town does not lay a separate table for people who stood somewhere else this summer.

On the first night at Ndenjoo, the guest does not serve the food, and does not sit in the place that is set back from the board. That place is already kept. On the second night a plate is put into the guest's hands to carry, and someone says the sentence the hall already lives by. The words of that sentence belong to the hall. What this hour has is the plate. That second supper is how the hall counts you as kin on the isle. It does not become a last hearth you can name to a mainland watch, and it does not become papers. On the one night of the moon sailors call Yoltal, the hall eats the heel of the loaf first. Every other night the heel is last, or it goes to the goats. A guest who asks why is given more bread. They will not say why.

If someone asks what the guest is before the bowl has been set down, the room goes quiet. The person nearest the pot puts the bowl down anyway. Talk may start again when the guest takes a second bite, or it may not start again at all. There is no speech kept in reserve to repair the asking. A host who wants a doctrine can have one after the dishes, taken from whichever faith that host already keeps.

### What a house keeps

When a town needs work done, it hires a person. The work people expect from a made thing is already someone's job. An Answered coaxes stone, water, fire, or air, and then goes home. A Stilled holds a body still for a knife. A mill-hand sets the stones, a hull-wright walks a hull, and a road-mend leaves a shovel in the bank. Those are people. A Clank is a Kind, a person born a Clank, who mends flood-walls, hulls, mills, and lofts alongside everyone else who does that work. A made thing is a thing, and a Clank is a person.

Most towns do not have one. If a scene has a thing that keeps working with nobody in it, a crane left running, a mill someone swears turns with nobody at the stones, or a figure of clay, then it has a maker and a problem. The maker is a person, or was one. The problem is why the thing is still doing the work: the stop broke, the maker left a debt unfinished, or a story got ahead of the mechanism. Name both before the scene goes on. It has no Kind, no Condition, and no hearth, and it owes no Tithe. It has no domain, because a domain is a job a person does. If the work needs doing, send the person whose job it is. A town without that problem does not keep one on a shelf.

Ordinary kit is whatever that ground already needed, clothing and tools, and it is not a ladder of better kit. Nothing here is rarer than the next town's version of the same need. A coat from this list keeps the weather off. It is not armor unless the wearer is also wearing armor. If someone fights with one of the tools, it counts as the smallest one-handed weapon in the rules, and the town that made it adds nothing.

| Place | What they keep | Why it is there |
|---|---|---|
| Rain-Wall shelves, the passes above Lirorn | An oiled wool coat | The shelf is wet and cold, and a quay shirt does not finish the walk. |
| Orentel's quays | A short hook, and a coat waxed against brine | The quay is lines, crates, and salt. The hook pulls. The coat is for the spray. |
| A young graft on the Heskoren live front | A pruning knife, and a wind cloak | The wood is new and the weather is not. The knife is a tender's tool, and it is not the One Knife. |
| Rain-Shadow herds, toward the Dry Stair and Ornsael | A goat-hair blanket, and a water-skin | The days are dry and the well is shared. The blanket is the herd's hair, felted. |
| Kumbaan halls and shallows | A peat cloak, and a short knife for mussels | Smoke, rain, and the rocks. The cloak is for the hall and the terrace, and it does not cross the storm-wall. |
| Mother-core orchards | Beeswax | They keep it for the hives, and for the halls that are owed wax when the blossom fails. |

Motherwood is not on this list. A scion, a cutting of a Turning Tree carried for planting, is a warden's cargo, not a material a person buys. The Spent Leaf and the Closed Lamp are not craft stock.

The pruning knife is not the blade people mean when they say "the knife." One house on the Near Mile hangs a kitchen knife on a ribbon beside a scorch on an old pot-ring. The people in the house know which summer that was. It was the Closing, the war that ended when the riders went home, and they do not turn out for it. The Low Wall is another stone, from another dig, and a knife-ribbon left at that wall was left in error. Strangers call the Near Mile knife the nameless knife, because they want the first blade to have a handle they can buy. The house will not sell it as that. It is their kitchen knife. The summer is theirs, and the scorch is on their pot. A person can take it down and hold it. In a fight it is a small one-handed blade, the smallest such weapon in the rules, and the ribbon adds nothing. The single blade people mean when they say the knife has not been produced. Nobody has named who made the First Cut.
