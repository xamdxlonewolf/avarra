# Language and Naming

You need a person who sounds like they grew up somewhere, and a hall that sounds like the land that built it.

**Speak the common name, and write the liturgical name.** The common name is what a person says. The liturgical name is the one that belongs on a charter, a rite, a learned map, or a scholar's tongue. A traveller asks for the *hall-keepers* or sails to *the Night Shore*. The paper may say *Orenhael* or *Netstrand*. At the table, the common handle comes first. The Hinge Shore has no current liturgical form. *Eolstrand* is retired, and a few old dockets still carry the crossed-out form.

**A person is named by the place that raised them.** If they are Kitsune, Selkie, Tengu, or Yumboe, they also keep a hearth-name from their Kind, the ancestry they were born into. Everyone else uses the local speech of the Old Tongue, which the devout call Maiethren, Mother-speech. Two names is ordinary. You introduce a fox-born broker on a quay that does not file people as **Raki of Hallowquay**, the given name from the fox register and the second name from the quay.

This chapter is the how-to: how names work, how to pick one, and how to make one that is not on a list. Under each Kind, the sound table and **To make another** are how you build a name that could sit next to the list without anyone blinking. **How to Name a Place or a Hall** names towns and offices from the old-word table. The lookup tables are what each land calls the same building. The map of the continents waits for **The Four Continents**. The deeper sound-system, and the words linguists use for it, live in Book X under **GM only: Language**. What you need in the room is here.

## How to Name a Person

1. **Where were they raised?** That sets the second name, and the sound of a common-tongue first name.
2. **Are they one of the four custom Kinds?** If yes, pick the given name from that Kind's list. If no, pick from the place-list. Stock ancestries have no private tongue, so a human neighbour draws a given name from the town.
3. **Add the second name that place uses.** A **byname** where people are witnessed. A **house-name** where a list has to find them again. The civic name carries one of those, not both, and it is never a name taken from the Kind.
4. **Say it once with the stress marked** (CAPS), then use it normally.

Most people have two names, and the civic line uses only those two. The first is a given name, chosen by the family. Custom-Kind families often draw it from the hearth-register, the sound that Kind keeps for given names, for kin, and for talk inside the group. Everyone else draws it from the local Maiethren drift, the regular way that place has worn the old tongue. The second is the second name the raising-place uses. You can often hear both homes in one introduction. The place that did the raising also decides whether that second name is a byname or a house-name.

A byname is how a place names you when someone there saw you. You are *of a town*, *of a Tree*, *called such for a deed*, or *of the house you actually sleep in* when that house is not itself a written list. This is the habit anywhere a witness still does the naming and a roll does not: on the road, on the live front, in the waiting towns, and under every power that refuses to write persons down. A human at the next desk might be **Valen of Hallowquay**. They share the street and the civic shape of the name, and the first sound is what tells you which home the given name came from.

A house-name is inherited. Children wear it. A neighbour says the given name, while a clerk says the **house first**, then the given name. The house-word is still from that place's drift, often an old given name that stuck to a line. It is not an English family name, and it is not a town. You do not hand over every piece you own. The place that asks decides which piece, and in which order.

A neighbour gets the given name. A clerk in a list-land, a place that writes people down so it can find them after the witness is gone, gets the house first, then the given name. That order is the file, and a given name alone is an incomplete row. A witness-town gets the given name, and *of the place* if you are a stranger, and leading with a house-name there sounds filed, or cold. Kumbaan, the far isle, gets the given name, and a mainland house, or a mainland *of-the-town*, is not your name there.

The rows below are the pattern in use. Maiethlir is Threnmaieth's counted river-seat, and Threnmaieth is the Tallied Crown. Trenledd is the Worn Count: it keeps a roll of persons and does not call that roll holy. The Inner Close is the household of the Walled Book, and the line is said there when rank is in the room. Brenod is a worn folk hearth-name, and it keeps no house-list. The Held bed is a bed that is never let. The White Note is a desk on the third quay at Orentel, and it is a house only while the paper is open. The Feeding Hill does not add a town to Njunda's name.

A marriage in a list-land chooses which house the new household is written under, and the children wear that house. Refusing the writing is a fight with the clerk, not a blank where the surname should be. In Trenledd, where the gate already says the house first, the column may file you under the house that pays, including one you dislike. In the Inner Close, the line can cast you out and keep the name on you anyway. *Closed* on a roster is that rank, not a second name. The Walled Book keeps its own heir-roll inside its walls, and that roll is not a list of everyone outside them.

Harrow's will not use a roll-name unless it means to be cold. Maiethlir, which already keeps a house when it has one, will invent a house for a walker who stays, and the walker will feel filed.

A mixed mainland child answers to one register if either side is custom, usually the kitchen they spent the summer in, or else the name that stuck, and still takes the second name of the place that raised them. A Selkie-and-Tengu child might be **Lavo of Highgate**. The perch is in that byname, a perch or a pass rather than a harbour, and the child does not wear two hearth-registers at once. A Yumboe keeps the one register and is never a mix.

| Who is asking | What you give |
|---|---|

| A clerk in a list-land | the house first, then the given name |
| A witness-town, and you are a stranger | the given name, and *of the place* |
| Kumbaan | the given name |

The list-lands are Threnmaieth, Trenledd, the Inner Close, and a filing house while the paper is open. Shut the paper, and the quay goes back to the witness way. A witness-town asks *of where?* because the name that stands for you is the place that saw you. A given name with no place on it has not been stood for. Leading with a house sounds like a column on a roll, and that town does not keep the column. A list-land asks *what house?* because the paper has to find you after the witness is gone. The house is the row the paper looks up, and the given name alone is an unfinished line. Kumbaan asks neither question. The given name is the name.

A Tengu's byname is often a perch or a pass: **Teku of Highgate**, **Hosen of the White Stair**. Raised on a roll, they wear the house-name like anyone else, and the perch stays the name the ridge uses.

Yumboes are the exception you can hear. They are too rare on the mainland to have grown a local byname-layer, so they keep the one register, and a Yumboe introduction sounds foreign the instant it is spoken.

### Keep the cast distinct

Before you lock a name for a player character, or for anyone else at the table, say it next to the names already in the room.

**Raki** and **Ranyu** in the same party are easy to mix up, because the shared opening and the shared cadence make the table hear one person twice. **Teku** and **Kukra** sound like twins, so they belong together only if they are twins.

A Selkie named **Wishi**, or a Tengu named **Maethaem**, is a tell. It says the person was raised away, or adopted, or is passing. Use that on purpose, or do not use it at all.

## Names by Place

Everyday names are the Old Tongue worn down to something you can shout across a yard. The warmth is in the liquids and the nasals, *l* and *r* and *m* and *n*, and the weight is in a soft *th* and the long vowels. A drift is one regular way that sound has worn. There are three of them, the same three that institution-names travel, and you push a person-name through the drift of the place. You do not invent a fourth human language.

| Drift | Place | What happens to a person-name | Pick from |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Conservative** | **Threnmaieth**, pious Maiethorn | The old church sound, kept whole. You hear the *th*. | **Maethaem** (MAY-thaym), **Reimaethe** (RYE-may-theh), **Thilim** (THIL-im), **Rithnali** (rith-NAH-lee), **Valein** (vah-LAYN) |
| **Worn** | **Vaethorn**, Heskoren folk | The *th* softens. Endings simplify. | **Nathdo** (NATH-doh), **Saelid** (SAY-lid), **Tora**, **Seine** (SAY-neh), **Nethiro** (neth-EE-ro) |
| **Eroded** | **Lestrand**, Strandoren docks | Clipped. The *th* has gone to t or d, or dropped. | **Valen**, **Tilim**, **Mataero** (mah-TAY-ro), **Ledan**, **Sorel** |

Devout places keep the old *th*, the thorn, which is the *th* of *thin* and never the *th* of *the*. In worn speech, when that thorn sits between two vowels or right after a vowel, it commonly becomes *t*, endings simplify, and unstressed vowels flatten. Written Vaethorn is spoken **Wetorn**. Careful prayer puts the thorn back for as long as the prayer is open. On the docks the thorn becomes t or d, or drops, long vowels clip, and word-ends wear off. A name that still has the *th* comes from a devout place. A name that has dropped it comes from a dock.

To make a new common-tongue name, start from one of the lists above and keep that drift's sounds. A named power uses the same roots and the drift that matches how it stands. It does not get a new language unless it refused the Tree. Vaelhesk keeps the older *vael* and the frontier *hesk-*, and that is this same speech, worn further. The isle of Kumbaan already has its own speech, and it is the exception.

Stress follows the Old Tongue rule under **How to Say It**. A compound you can still hear as two roots stresses the first root. The stress marked on first use is the one to trust.

## How a place speaks

A traveller can say **Turning Tree**, **the Mother**, **a hug**, **a colour**, **a tree-warden**, and **a graft**, and be understood on every mainland road. The Turning Tree is the shared name for the Tree. The Mother is the one the pious core calls Maieth in the street and the docks call the old rite. A colour is what a Tree speaks when it can already speak one, and a hug is the no-colour, the embrace that leaves a child without a Condition. A tree-warden is the person named with that Tree, under a different word in each column below. A graft is wood that has been brought and set and cannot yet Turn. Use the common words and you will be understood. The table is what a place says when it is being itself, rather than reaching for the word a stranger would already understand.

The pious core keeps the thorn, waiting towns soften that thorn to *t*, and the docks drop it. Kumbaan speaks a language of its own. Mainlanders call that isle the Sundering Isle, the old liturgical tongue belongs to the mainland, and the hill was never part of it.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| the Tree | **the Mother's Hand** | **the Thallow** | **Tallo** *(TAL-oh)* | **the far tree** |
| the Mother | **Maieth** *(MY-eth)* | **Mait** *(MAYT)* | **the old rite** | **the loud story** |
| a hug | **a keeping** | **a hug** | **a clear** | **the place** |
| a colour | **the colour** | **the gift** | **the fall** | **a test** |
| a warden | **the Reckoned Hands** | **Hands-folk** | **a tree-tender** | **the loud guest** |

A neighbour in Maiethlir or the Tallied Crown says **Maieth** in the street, the way another town says the Mother, and the polity's own street-name is still the Tallied Crown. A polity is a land with its own way of standing. **The Mother's Hand** is the Tree that can already speak a colour. Wood that has been brought and set, and cannot yet Turn, is **a Hand carried in**, and at the rite they call the no-colour **a keeping**. The Tallied Crown's clerks file that child as unmarked. That word belongs to the desk, and the square does not use it. Maiethvael shares Maieth and the Mother's Hand, and calls the warden a **tree-warden**. The Reckoned Hands is the crown's word for the same person.

Careful prayer in a waiting town puts the thorn back, and Maieth returns for as long as the prayer is open. The kitchen says **Mait**, which is Maieth with the thorn tired, and **the Thallow** is their name for the Tree. A colour is **the gift**, and a no-colour is still **a hug**, which they do not dress as a prize. Young wood on the road is **a carried Thallow**. On the live front, a young graft or a neighbour's canopy such as Harrow's Green, people say **the wood** until that wood can Turn.

**Tallo** is how a quay says the Thallow once the thorn is gone. Say it on the water, and keep it off the charter, because the written name stays the Turning Tree, or the old liturgical name when the paper is a rite. They call her **the old rite**, and they call a no-colour **a clear**: the child came up clear, and is free to be hired. A warden there is **a tree-tender**. A scion in the hold, their word for a graft being carried, is **a carrying**. Trenledd is this speech with a list behind it. The gate asks the house, and the gate will not put the thorn back to be polite.

Ndenjoo, a hall on the hill, has a table, and it has no Tree and no graft, so it does not grow a word that would put one in the valley. **The far tree** and **their cutting** are mainland things, said of someone else's country. **The loud story** is what they call the Mother when a guest brings her up, a tale about being chosen, told at noon-volume. **The place** is what they offer where a mainlander expects a hug. **A test** is their word for a colour, and they will not use one on a guest. A warden who climbs the hill is **the loud guest**, and is still fed.

A scene uses one of four ways of talking. Each one is a sentence length, the thing they say again, and the thing they will not say while mixed company is present. Each mainland drift still has one phrase it says whole, and the hill has its own. Nobody stops to translate them.

In a devout square, which is the speech of Threnmaieth, of Maiethlir, and of the Inner Close when the room is a household rather than a file, people use two sentences, and the first one finishes. If that first sentence left **the Mother's Hand** or **Maieth** out, the second puts them back. A fragment sounds like the dock, and they will hear it. What they say again is Maieth, and the Mother's Hand. While the room is mixed they will not say *Tallo*, *the old rite*, or *a clear*, and they will not say *unmarked* while the rite is still open, because that word belongs to the desk. They will not lead with a house-name to a neighbour, and they will not joke a colour. While the room is mixed, they greet a neighbour by the given name. A player who needs the sound can say the line below, and then stop. The last words are the phrase, left whole.

> "The Mother's Hand kept her. We say Maieth before the square moves. Lei thulen." *(lay THOO-len)*

On a dock, which is Lestrand, Orentel, and the Ledger Coast quays, and Trenledd when the list is what is speaking, people give about six words to a breath, and they take another breath only if the number or the berth needs it. Then the phrase, and the quay lets it go. What they say again is **clear**, and the number, and the berth. What they repeat is the last hard fact, not a person's feelings about it. They will not say *Maieth* or *the Mother's Hand*, they will not restore a thorn to sound devout, and they will not call a clear a sadness. They will not ask *what house?* while the paper is shut. While a filing house has the paper open, they will, house first and then the given name, and they still will not hymn it. They speak the dock form, with the old *th* gone.

> "Clear. Berth five. The fall's on Tallo. Ka doven." *(kah DOV-en)*

One tone covers the waiting towns and the live front, and the two of them repeat different words. Waiting towns, the Waiting Lands and Eolvaeth among them, say again the week that has not come. The live front, Harrow's Green or a young graft or a neighbour's canopy, says the wood again. They use one sentence, and that sentence does not close the week. Twelve words is already long for them. They reach for the old word and let the thorn thin. Waiting speech says **not this week**. The live front says **the wood**, and then **the neighbour** if the wood will not speak. They call their Tree the Thallow. They will not promise the week, they will not say *the old rite*, and they will not offer a house-name. They will not call the Thallow *Tallo*, because that is the coast, and on this road it sounds like a slight. Prayer puts the thorn back. The kitchen keeps the tired form to itself.

> "Not this week, and the Thallow is still the Thallow. Len soret." *(len so-RET)*

The live front uses the same length and the same phrase, and the other repeat.

> "The wood first, and the neighbour if the wood won't. Len soret."

A hill-hall on Kumbaan, Ndenjoo and any attic that is still keeping the table, keeps a night pace. The welcome is the whole of what they are willing to say about it with guests in the room. They spend three short sentences on that welcome, a welcome, then a pause, then one practical thing, and they do not explain a Tree inside it. What they say again is **sit**, and **the place**. They will not read **a test**, they will not point to a graft on the hill, and they will not ask *of where?* or *what house?* as the price of a seat. They will not say *Maieth*, and a mainland house-name, or a mainland *of-the-town*, is not the name they use at the table. A newcomer is seated on that welcome. The hill's own phrase stays inside it, untranslated.

> "Sit. The place is set. Aman ndo." *(ah-MAHN ndoh)*

## Names for the Four Custom Kinds

Kitsune, Selkie, Tengu, and Yumboe keep a hearth-register for given names, kin, and in-group talk. On the street they still wear the local second name. You can often hear both homes in one introduction. The own-names, the names the peoples use for themselves, are settled. The common-tongue names outsiders say, Kitsune, Selkie, Tengu, and Yumboe, stay.

Each register is a naming-language: a sound, a short recipe, and a list, rather than a grammar. Nobody writes sentences in Kusawe, Sakoa, or Gonan. If a scene needs an in-group word, coin one word from that Kind's sounds and say what it means in the same breath.

The tables are how you check a new name. A beat is one syllable. C is a consonant and V is a vowel, so CV is a consonant and then a vowel, the way *Ra* is, and CVC closes on another consonant, the way *Tek* does. When the table says F, that is the consonant allowed to end the beat, and it names which ones. A prenasal is the hum on the front of a stop, *mb* and *nd* and *nj* and *nk*. *Ai*, *ea*, and *oa* are glides, two vowels spoken as one. An onset is whatever consonants begin the word. A sound in the forbidden row already belongs to another home, and the register does not take it.

The lists are what you pick from. **To make another** is only for when you need a name that is not on the list. Make one that could sit next to the others without anyone blinking.

### Kitsune: Kusawe (koo-SAH-way)

A Kitsune hearth-name sounds light, slipping, and a little sibilant, full of *s* and *sh*, the sort of name that slips round the corner of a sentence.

**At the table**

- **Consonants** k s sh n r m t w y f h
- **Vowels** a i u e o ai
- **Syllables** CV · CVF (F = n/m/r) · 2–3 syllables
- **Forbidden** *th* (Maiethren sacred), prenasals *mb/nd/nj* (Yumboe), *kr/gr* (Tengu)

**Pick from:** Raki, Nenyu, Wishi, Kiraye, Suti, Hatu, Ranyu, Sinro, Foryo, Heka, Sofan.

The hearth-name is the one they answer to when someone is trying to pin them down. They consider this funny. A fox raised devout in Threnmaieth, where the clerk says the house first, may still be Raki at home and Maethaem, Raki on the roll.

**To make another:** two or three short beats, using k, s, sh, n, r, m, t, w, y, f, h, and the vowels a, i, u, e, o, plus *ai* as in *eye*, and you can end on n, m, or r. Leave *th* to the church, *kr* and *gr* to the Tengu, and *mb*, *nd*, and *nj* to the Yumboe.

### Selkie: Sakoa (sah-KOH-ah)

A Selkie hearth-name sounds liquid, rounded, and coastal. You can say it with cold air in your mouth.

**At the table**

- **Consonants** l r n m s v f d b k w
- **Vowels** o u a ea oa i e
- **Syllables** CV · CVC · 2–3 syllables
- **Forbidden** *th*, *sh*, *kr/gr*, prenasals

**Pick from:** Lavo, Fesa, Moka, Dovo, Neson, Foaro, Veakea, Didel, Roadoa, Woanun, Laknea.

The day a child is first given the run of their own skin is when the hearth-name is spoken to the water. The civic second name comes later, from the town that watched them Turn, a byname, or a house-name if that town writes people down. Two homes, two names, and they do not consider this a conflict.

**To make another:** two or three beats, using l, r, n, m, s, v, f, d, b, k, w, and the vowels o, u, a, i, e, plus *ea* and *oa*. Leave *th*, *sh*, *kr*, *gr*, and the prenasals to the homes that already use them.

### Tengu: Gonan (GO-nan)

A Tengu hearth-name is clipped, windy, and high-country. The names are shorter, and they start hard, on a single consonant or on an onset such as *kr*, *gr*, and *hr*, said as if the wind might take the end. This is ridge-speech, and it does not sound like Kusawe.

**At the table**

- **Consonants** k t g n r h s b d · onsets kr gr hr
- **Vowels** a o u e *(few i — that vowel lives in Kusawe)*
- **Syllables** CVC · CV · 1–3 syllables, prefer 2
- **Forbidden** *th*, *sh*, prenasals, long *ai/ea/oa* glides

**Pick from:** Teku, Kukra, Gobak, Hosen, Kotak, Buka, Rekren, Kuhos, Hrarat, Gonan.

Gonan is also the people-name. Reusing it as a given name is a hearth-honor, like naming a child after the ridge.

**To make another:** prefer two beats, using k, t, g, n, r, h, s, b, d, with *kr*, *gr*, and *hr* at home at the front, and the vowels a, o, u, and e. Few *i*, because that vowel lives with the foxes. Leave *th*, *sh*, the prenasals, and the long *ai*, *ea*, and *oa* glides to the other homes.

### Yumboe

Yumboe is its own tongue. Open vowels, a drum-like rhythm, and *mb*, *nd*, *nk*, *nj* at the start of a beat. A Yumboe name sounds foreign the instant it is spoken. The Trees never crossed the storm-wall, so the speech stayed the island's own, and it owes nothing to the Leaf-Mother's liturgy.

They call themselves **Bakhna Rakhna**, the good people, and their home is **Kumbaan** (koom-BAHN), the hidden home, which mainlanders call the Sundering Isle.

**Pick from:** Ambaa, Njeela, Kumbo, Sambanka, Ndeya, Mbaraan, Njunda, Rakhilo, Sonkaa, Mbindaa, Yumbaka, Ndoolu, Sambiyo, Lunji, Yaaba, Saalo, Mbela, Soonke, Njili, Yendo, Waandi, Koriwaa.

**To make another:** keep the open vowels and the *mb*, *nd*, *nk*, and *nj*. No *th*, and no clipping it down to a Lestrand dock-name. They keep the one register on the mainland.

## How to Say It

CAPS is the stressed beat. Mark it the first time, then drop it.

- **ae** = "ay" as in *day*
- **ai** and **ei** = "eye"
- **eo** = an "ay-oh" glide
- **a** as in *father* · **e** as in *bed* · **i** as in *machine* · **o** as in *go* · **u** as in *moon*
- **th** as in *thin*, never as in *the*

The Old Tongue feels warm and weighty, a hall-and-hearth tongue. The liquids and the nasals carry the warmth, *l* and *r* and *m* and *n*. The thorn and the long vowels carry the weight. The consonants in use are m, n, l, r, w, j, p, t, k, b, d, g, f, v, s, h, and *th*. The vowels are a, e, i, o, u, plus the two-letter sounds ae, ai, ei, and eo.

A word may open on a consonant, and it may open on one of a small inherited set of clusters, consonants that already begin an old word together: *br*, *kr*, *st*, *str*, and *th* followed by *r*. Those are the shapes in *bren*, *crae*, *stel*, *strand*, and *thren*. A word may close on a single consonant, and the inherited closings include *sk* and *nd*, which is how *hesk* is licensed. Do not invent a three-consonant opening except *str-*, and do not invent a four-consonant knot. A new root should prefer the simpler shape. If you cannot say it at the table, it is the wrong name.

A simple root keeps the stress listed for it. A compound you can still hear as two roots takes its main stress on the first root, with a lighter beat on the second: **THAEL**-oren, **VAETH**-orn. Old ceremonial forms, and personal names, may keep a stress of their own, the stress that word already had before anyone compounded it. When that happens, the pronunciation given on first use is the one to trust.

## How to Name a Place or a Hall

The Old Tongue is **Maiethren** (MAI-eth-ren), Mother-speech, the church language of the Awakening Tree. It is this world's Latin. Nobody grows up speaking it at the market, and it is still the language of the Tree, the rite, and the law's oldest words. Everyone who is not speaking as the devout calls it the Old Tongue. Place-names, crowns, and old offices are built from it. Everyday talk uses plain compounds. People say *the Kept*, *the ranging*, and *tree-wardens*, and you should hear those as worn Old Tongue rather than as a separate language. The common handle is the name spoken at a dock, on a road, in a market, and at the table. It is not a translation sitting after the "real" name. Liturgical forms stay real. They belong to writing, to ritual, and to the moment someone deliberately uses the older form. No new liturgical names are being made.

To name something old or official:

1. **Pick one or two old words** from the list below.
2. **Glue them together.** Thaeloren is *thael* + *oren*. That is Tree and witness.
3. **Wear it the way the land wears it.** A devout land keeps the *th* and the long vowels. A frontier town softens them. A dock town wears the *th* and the long vowels away. The most worn name belongs to the least-devout place.
4. **Mark the stress** on first use.

Same building, three names, and the name shows the stance. A devout land calls the Far-Voiced hall a *gift-hall*. A merchant coast calls it a *release-house*. Threnmaieth calls it a *counted hall*. The lookup tables below are that fact, row by row. Kumbaan's names are Yumboe, and Kumbaan is not in this box of old words.

### The old words

These are the blocks names are built from. The meaning is what the word meant in the rite, even when a dock town has kept the sound and let the meaning slide. A suffix is an ending, and *ren* used as a suffix means "the speech of" whatever stands in front of it. A month-root is a root the calendar already took. *Strand* is a loan, a word that arrived from another shore and was made ordinary before the daughter speeches divided. Where the notes contrast *thren* with *ledd*, *thren* is sacred counting, a stewardship reckoned before the Mother, and *ledd* is the commercial twin, the ledger and the sum. The list is small and settled. No new roots are being added.

One name carries the setting. The Awakening Tree's liturgical name is **Thaeloren** *(THAEL-or-en)*, *thael* for the Tree set beside *oren* for witnessing, so the name means the Tree that witnesses. To witness, in this tongue, is to speak truly, and citizenship-by-witness is that witnessing folded into the name of the Tree. Everyday, people say **the Thallow**.

Each daughter speech is the Old Tongue worn by distance from the Trees and from the faith, a later speech descended from this root, and the changes are regular enough that any old word can be pushed through them. Conservative speech, in Threnmaieth and the pious core, barely moved, so it still sounds like the rite: archaic, formal, and holy. Worn speech, in Vaethorn and on the devout frontier, is rough-warm folk-speech that still reaches for the old holy words. Eroded speech, on Lestrand and the secular coast, is smooth, clipped, and mercantile. The most-drifted sound belongs to the least-devout place.

Closer to the Tree the old sound stays, and farther out the *th* wears away.

**Vaethorn** *(VAETH-orn; spoken worn **Wetorn**)* joins *vaeth*, the waiting, to *orn*, land, and means the Waiting Land. Careful writing and prayer keep the sacred *th*, ordinary speech wears it down to *t*, and people say the Waiting Lands, or simply the Waiting.

**Lestrand** *(LES-trand)* joins *ledd*, to reckon, to *strand*, the shore, and then wears the compound smooth, so the name means the Reckoning Coast. It is the most worn of the original three. People say the Ledger Coast, and formal records keep *Lestrand*.

**Threnmaieth** *(THREN-mai-eth)* joins *thren*, sacred counting, to *maieth*, the Mother, and means the Mother's Reckoning. The pious core keeps the full sacred sound, and it names its census as reverent stewardship of Her gifts. People say the Tallied Crown, and the crown writes *Threnmaieth*.

The other twelve are built on the same lever, so the written name is the stance you can hear.

On pious Maiethorn the old sound stays. **Maiethvael** *(MAI-eth-vael)* keeps *maieth* and joins it to *vael*, so the name is the Mother's Gift, gift rather than count. **Orenbren** *(OREN-bren)* is *oren* and *bren*, the witnessing-hearth, and it stays clear of *brenhael*, which is an inn. **Saelthael** *(SAEL-thael)* is *sael* and *thael*, the sown Tree, and it stays clear of *Saelorn*, which is a month, and of *Saelhael*, the greens-keepers. **The Hinge Shore** is the common name that replaced the retired *Eolstrand*, a compound that had crowded *Eoloren*, *Eolthael*, and *Eolvaeth*. Current speech and current writing use the common name. **Lirorn** *(LIR-orn)* is *lir* and *orn*, thaw-land, and it stays clear of *Liren* and of *Ornthael*.

On the eroded shore the sacred count will not be said whole. **Brenledd** *(BREN-ledd)* sets *bren* beside *ledd*, hearths of the ledger, and that name is the compact. **Leddvael** *(LEDD-vael)* is *ledd* and *vael*, the gift reckoned, and it stays clear of *Leddoren* and of *Leddhael*. **Trenledd** *(TREN-ledd)* is *thren* worn down beside *ledd*, and the reconstruction *Threnledd* goes unused, because they will not say the holy word. **Netstrand** *(NET-strand)* is *neth* worn down beside *strand*, the reconstruction *Nethstrand* goes unused, and people there will not say why the name means night.

On worn Heskoren the sacred *th* survives in careful writing and thins in the mouth. **Ornled** *(ORN-led)* is *orn* and *ledd* clipped. On this continent, dropping *th* is a neglect, so the reconstruction *Ornledd* goes unused. **Vaelhesk** *(VAEL-hesk)* sets *vael*, in the older sense of what the land yields, beside the frontier *hesk-*, and it stays clear of *Vaeloren* and of *Vaethorn*. The fight is in the first syllable, *vael* against *vaeth*. **Saelvaeth** *(SAEL-vaeth)* joins *sael* to *vaeth* and means sowing into the waiting, and it shares *sael* with Saelthael on purpose. Kumbaan is not a sixteenth name on this list.

Faiths are named from the same blocks, so a faith sounds like the land that keeps it. **Haelthael** *(HAEL-thael)* sets *hael*, whole and hale, beside *thael*, the Tree, and means the Tree kept whole, which is what the Watching calls the Tree from before anyone gave it a mother's name, and its people are the Watchers. **Leddoren** *(LEDD-or-en)* is *ledd* joined to *oren*, the witnessed account, because the Fair Hand takes the Tree as a signing-table, and its people are the Book-hands. **Vaeloren** *(VAEL-or-en)* is *vael* in the older sense plus *oren*, the land's witnessing, which the Old Ways keep, and its people are the door-keepers and the Kin-speakers. **Ndeyaan** *(ndey-AHN)* owes this liturgy nothing. It is the Yumboe night-keeping, not Maiethren, and it is the Open Table's own name, whose people are the table-keepers and the hill-hosts.

The houses that work the faith are named from the same box, and no new ones are being made. Tree-wardens write themselves **Orenhael** *(OREN-hael)*, witness beside whole, because they speak the fall truly and keep the Tree hale. Road-hands are **Thaelvaeth** *(THAEL-vaeth)* when the rite names them, the Tree gone to the waiting. Watchers are **Nethoren** *(NETH-or-en)*, the night-witnesses. They are not written *Haeloren*, which would sit too close to Orenhael. Book-hands are **Leddhael** *(LEDD-hael)*, the accounted-whole. *Orenledd* was not used, because it collides with *Orledd*, the Counterparty, the other party named in a Bound's bargain. Door-keepers are **Vaelbren** *(VAEL-bren)*. Here *vael* keeps the older sense, the land's yield, and beside *bren*, the hearth, the name means the yield-hearth. It is not *Vaelorn*, which belongs to the Taking. **Njaalo** *(NJAH-lo)* is a Yumboe coinage, not Maiethren, and it means the hall that is kept, the own-name of the table-keepers. The isle's name for the shore-sitters, also outside Maiethren, is **Njawaal** *(njah-WAHL)*. The Slide's cant is **Vaethledd** *(VAETH-ledd)*, the waiting reckoned. A cant is the house's other name, neither the everyday word nor the compound a charter writes. Vaethledd is not *Orledd*, and it is not *Nethledd*, which was retired with the Back Table, a Slide name that stayed unused.

Greens-keepers are **Saelhael** *(SAEL-hael)* in the liturgy, the sown-whole, and that name is not *Saelorn*, which is a month. **Aeloren** is retired, because it collided with *Vaeloren*, *Leddoren*, and *Eoloren*, and it never carried information that hall-keepers or ael-hands did not carry better. Old rosters may preserve it as a crossed-out form, and no living house claims it. Stillers are **Stelhael** *(STEL-hael)*, the held-whole. **The Crae** *(kray)* is simply *crae*, craft, and it is the umbrella over the element-guilds, one name set over the houses rather than a separate liturgy for each. There are four doors under it, and those four do not each receive a liturgy. The Intake has no liturgical name, and its cant is *the catching-year*. Hall-keepers have no current liturgical form.

Era-names come from the same old words. **Brenvaeth** *(BREN-vaeth)* is the Walking Years, the hearth gone to the waiting, and the compound is a deliberate inversion of *Thaelvaeth*. People walked in, and later the wood walked out. **Eoloren** *(EOL-or-en)* is the First Cut, the witnessed hinge, and the same name serves the clerical year-count, the *Eoloren-count* or the Cut-years. It is not *Eolthael*, which is a month. **Ornthael** *(ORN-thael)* is the Years of Hands, Tree-land. That name is not *Maiethorn*, the Motherland, and it is not *Haelthael*, the Watching. Before the Walk stays mostly unnamed on the mainland roads. The Watching keep **the Before**. Kumbaan keeps moons rather than this count.

A Walking-era road-house has the cant **brenhael** *(BREN-hael)*, the hearth kept whole, the inn that kept you hale through the waiting, and the everyday word is *road-house*. The cant is not *Brenvaeth*, which is the era, and it is not *Brenorn*, which is a month. The three walks stay in the common tongue on purpose, the Near Mile, the Salt Walk, and the Long Mile, because they were logistics before they were liturgy.

The nameless knife and the nameless green are folk objects of the First Cut, and they have no liturgical form on purpose. Naming the green would pick a hearth, and a hearth would pick a hand, so the everyday name comes first and stays. The generation of argument is the run of summers from the first taking to the first local Leaf-Fall, the stretch when the First Seat could not undo the graft and had not yet written permission for grafting. It stays in the common tongue because it was a quarrel, not a rite. It is not *Eoloren*, and it is not *Brenvaeth*.

The extra mile, the First-Hand year, and the neighbour's week are the walk's three jobs in the Years of Hands. They stay in the common tongue because they are jobs, not a fourth age. They are not *Brenvaeth*, which is the requirement, and they are not *Ornthael*, which is the era. *The old mile* still means the Long Mile when the neighbour is a continent. The stone in the square, the upper room, and the Held bed are what that walk left sitting in a Hands town. The everyday name comes first. *Brenhael* remains the inn's cant, and it does not become the loft's name.

**Ornsael** *(ORN-sael)* sets *orn* beside *sael* and means the sown-ground. It is the Rain-Shadow walk-hold on Saelthael, and it is not *Saelthael* itself, not *Saelorn*, not *Saelhael*, and not *Ornthael*. **Brenthael** *(BREN-thael)* sets *bren* beside *thael* and means the hearth-Tree. It is the neighbour Hands-town of the Third Hearth, and it is not *brenhael*, not *Brenvaeth*, not *Brenorn*, and not *Orenbren*. **Brenod** *(BREN-od)*, **Vaelun** *(VAY-lun)*, and **Ornath** *(OR-nath)* are the worn folk hearth-names of the Three Hamlets Past the Ford. They have no liturgy on purpose, because the Seat's leave did not write them, and they stay clear of *Vaeloren*, *Brenvaeth*, and *Ornled*. The Mill-hold and the First Bowl are two more squares. The First Bowl's folk hearth-name is *Lonasir*, the common tongue comes first, and there is no liturgy. They stay clear of *Brenthael* and of *Vaelun*.

**Eolvaeth** *(EOL-vaeth)* is *eol* and *vaeth*, the hinge-waiting, and it is Vaethorn's pilgrim-edge seat. Everyday worn speech says *Elvaeth* *(EL-vaeth)*. The name is not *Vaethorn*, not *Thaelvaeth*, and not *Eoloren* or *Eolthael*. **Orentel** *(OREN-tel)* is eroded *oren* and *thael*, the witnessing-Tree, with the *th* worn off. It is Lestrand's salt-quay seat, and everyday speech also calls it the Salt Quay. It is not *Thaeloren*, not *Orenbren*, and not *Lestrand* or *Netstrand*. **Maiethlir** *(MAI-eth-lir)* is *maieth* and *lir*, the Mother's thaw, and it is Threnmaieth's counted river-seat. It stays clear of *Threnmaieth*, *Threnhael*, *Thaeloren*, *Maiethvael*, *Maiethorn*, *Lirorn*, *Liren*, and *Brenthael*.

No new roots are spent on ground. The everyday name comes first, and these stay in the common tongue: the Old Crossing, the West Water, the Rain-Wall, which Lirorn calls the Thaw-Wall, the Noon Pass, the Shelf-gate, the Core-thaw, the Well-wash, the Rise-water, and the Chart-run.

## What Each Land Calls the Same Thing

Every land has the same institutions. They flow from the Trees and the Conditions, and both are universal. What each land calls them comes from how it stands on the faith, on whether it sits among healthy Trees or at a thin edge, and on how far the state reaches onto a person. A devout land calls the release-hall a gift-hall, a merchant coast calls that same building a release-house, and a surveillance-crown calls it a counted hall. It is the same building under three worldviews.

Use the **common tongue** column unless you need the local word. Use the local word when a Vaethorn pilgrim, a Lestrand factor, or a Threnmaieth clerk is speaking, or when you want the table to hear the difference. When a cell pauses in parentheses, that parenthesis is the stance in miniature.

The three walks keep their common-tongue names. People say the Near Mile, the Salt Walk, and the Long Mile when they plan the road.

### The Cut and the spread

These are the lived names of the First Cut. People say the common-tongue name. The cutter is unnamed. The nameless knife and the nameless green have no liturgical form. A liturgical name for the green would name a hearth, and a hearth would name a hand.

### Residues

These are the lived names of the Years of Hands, and the everyday name comes first. An origin-walk you take when you already have a Tree is the extra mile. A walk you take because the Tree is missing, young, or sick is the neighbour's week, and people still say *the old mile* when that neighbour is a continent. The prestige origin-winter is the First-Hand year. The Years of Hands are the present.
