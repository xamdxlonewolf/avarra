# The Ages

People in this world do not live in a fourth age stacked on three earlier ones. They keep two clocks at the same time, and the clock a person reaches for tells you where that person stands.

The first clock is personal, and a family keeps it the way it keeps a grandparent's stories. It counts how you were Turned. Turning is what the Awakening Tree does for a child in the tenth year: a Condition, a change that comes to a person rather than a people they were born as, can be Given, or the Tree can hug the child and leave them without one. A colour at that giving means a Condition. A hug means no colour, and a person left with none is called Kept, and also UnTurned. Did you walk to Thaeloren, the Awakening Tree itself, or to a neighbour who had already made that walk, or did you stand under a town graft, a cutting of that Tree planted at home and grown as the same plant? An ordinary family will tell you they are a Hands-house, Hands being those planted town Trees, and that grandmother still talks of the road. A Long-Lived person, whose aging has stopped and who forgets nothing they have witnessed, who says "I walked" is saying when they were young, because the Long-Lived live long enough that the walk can have been their own childhood.

The second clock is geographic, and it has not finished. The Grafting is the carrying of living wood outward from the origin, Hand by Hand, and it is not a golden age that opened and then closed. The wood started at the Awakening Tree, filled Maiethorn, crossed the sea to Strandoren, and is still arriving on Heskoren, which people also call the Sundered Reach. Kumbaan, the Sundering Isle, stands entirely off that spread. Where the wood is still thin, that thinness is not a scar history left behind. It is the front of a process that has not finished.

Both clocks sit on a preface that was never given an age-name, Before the Walk. The Tree was already there. People were already born into Kinds, the peoples a person is from birth and does not become later, and some were already Struck, which is a Condition arriving later at a threshold such as a drowning, an oath, or a death, before anyone had called the Tree a mother. No count that is being honest begins with the Tree.

This was not a civilizing mission, and the Walking Years were not a golden age of pilgrimage that the grafts then improved. The Walking Years were the work of getting a child to one Tree in time, across weeks of road and water, and that work killed children. The Grafting was horticulture, the cutting and planting of living wood, and people sanctified it afterward. The First Seat, the college of tree-wardens at the Awakening Tree, now tells both the walk and the grafts as the Mother's will travelling. Folk memory is less tidy, and holds the romance and the graves in the same sentence.

## The ages at a glance

Everyday is the name people use. Liturgical is the old name. Says is what that old name means.

The pair of liturgical names turns inside out the name the Road-hands use for the work of carrying wood, and the Road-hands are the tree-wardens who walk that wood. In the Old Tongue, Thaelvaeth means "the Tree gone to the waiting," which is wood carried out to the hearths. Brenvaeth *(BREN-vaeth)* means "the hearth gone to the waiting," which is people walking in to the Tree. Eoloren *(EOL-or-en)* is the liturgical name of the cut, and Ornthael *(ORN-thael)* is the liturgical name of the years when a town has its own Tree. The Grafting is the moment the world stopped sending hearths to the Tree and started sending the Tree to hearths.

## A history in order

This section tells the history in order, from before anyone counted years to the present. The years are given the way the First Seat and the devout clerks count them, from the First Cut, the first time a piece cut from the Awakening Tree was planted in another town and grew. That year is Cut-year 0, and the present year is Cut-year 387. Many people count another way, and "How years are numbered," below, says who they are. The later sections of this chapter tell each part at length. What follows here is the order of events, and what each one left behind.

### Before anyone counted years

Before anyone counted years, the Awakening Tree already stood on Maiethorn, in the country now called the Sacred Core. It was the only tree in the world a child could be Turned under, and nobody planted it in any summer that anyone remembers.

People were already born into their Kinds. Some were already Struck into a Condition at a threshold, far from the Tree and with nobody to witness it. Under the Tree, children in their tenth year were already brought at the solstice and either Given a colour or hugged. Nobody knows how long any of that had been going on.

The Watching, the faith that keeps a night of attention and will not start history at a mother's name, call this time the Before. They teach that the Tree was already watching people before anyone called it a mother. The Old Ways on Heskoren, a long way from Maiethorn, say their people were already being Struck at the places they call doors, a drowning, a grove, or a stone, before any road to the Tree existed. Kumbaan is not part of this history. Its people have their own past, and they do not count it by the Tree.

Nobody wrote down a king, a war, or a founding from this time. A few stone places from it survive, and three of them are described near the end of this section. More of the thin preface is in "Before the Walk."

### The years of the walk

The time before the First Cut is called the Walking Years. Nobody knows when it started, because it started when families began to organize the trip to the Tree, and nobody wrote that year down. The road, the inns, the graves, and the gate are told at length in "The Walking Years."

In those years there was one Tree to Turn under, and one week in which to do it. A child who had turned ten since the last High Solstice had to stand under the Awakening Tree during Turning-Week, the week around the High Solstice in the month of Eolthael. A child who arrived after that week was not Given a Condition, that year or any later year. They could still be Struck later in life, but no Tree would ever Give them a Condition. Families who lived far away left months early. The richest families, and the most frightened, sent a nine-year-old to spend a whole year near the Tree, wintering in a road-house, so the child could not miss the week.

People in Maiethorn walked the Near Mile, which took between three and twelve days. People in Strandoren, the country reached by ship, took the Salt Walk: three to five days on a ship across the Old Crossing, the sea between the two shores, and then the Near Mile, usually one to three weeks in all. People in Heskoren, and in the far inland parts of Strandoren, walked the Long Mile, which took six to twelve weeks. A family on the Long Mile had to give up a year's spare food, or sign a debt at a road-house, or not go.

Many families could not afford to send every child. A family with three children near ten might send one. That child might come home with a Condition, while the brothers and sisters who stayed home grew up UnTurned, or were Struck later. Because so many children never reached the Tree, the far lands were full of UnTurned people, whom folk called Kept, and of people Struck later in life.

Inns along the roads gave travellers a bed and let them eat on credit, to be paid back on the way home or years later. Many of the innkeepers were Long-Lived, and they forgot none of what was owed. After the walk stopped being necessary, some of those inns kept their records and became banks. The White Note House in Orentel is one of them. The Third Hearth is an inn that stayed an inn.

Children died on the road, and so did the adults who carried them. They drowned at river crossings in the thaw, caught fever in crowded inns, were lost in late storms on the Old Crossing, and went hungry on the long stretches of the Long Mile. Travellers buried them where the group stopped and set a stone there. The next year's travellers used the stone to find the water. Those stones are the mile-shrines that still stand along the old roads, often with a ribbon tied on.

Reaching the Tree was not enough. A group of families with no Condition lived around the Awakening Tree and decided who was allowed to stand under it. People now call them the Closed Seat, and in ordinary talk they are the Grove-Sitters. A family needed a paper called grove-leave to pass their gate. A family could arrive with good shoes and a paid ship's berth and still be turned away. Some children walked the whole Long Mile and went home UnTurned because the gate refused them.

The Closed Seat ruled the country around the Tree, and the rule stopped at that country. Strandoren's families paid for the crossing, but the Closed Seat never ruled Strandoren. Heskoren was too far away to rule, and Kumbaan never came.

Some Long-Lived alive today walked one of these roads as children, and they remember every day of the walk. They do not agree about what it meant.

### The First Cut (Cut-year 0)

In Cut-year 0, someone cut a piece of living wood from the Awakening Tree, carried it in earth to a town about a day's walk away, and planted it, and it grew. People had always grafted fruit trees. This was the first time a cutting from the Awakening Tree took root somewhere else.

Nobody knows who made the cut, and the town is not named either. People call that first graft the nameless green. A town Tree grows from wood cut from the Awakening Tree, so people count it as the same Tree. The devout, who speak of that Tree as the Mother's, call each town Tree a Hand of the Mother. The five stories of who cut are in "The First Cut."

### The years of argument (Cut-years 0 to 19)

A new graft cannot hold a Leaf-Fall, the Turning in which a town's own children stand under it and are Given a colour or hugged, until it has grown for about a generation, about twenty years. For nineteen years nobody knew whether the nameless green would ever Turn a child.

During those years other towns on the Near Mile cut their own pieces and planted them, and most of the cuttings died. The First Seat said the cutting was theft and forbade it. People cut anyway.

In Cut-year 19 the nameless green held its first Turning-Week, and a child of that town was Turned under it. The stories disagree about whether the child received a colour or a hug. Either way, a town that was not the Awakening Tree's town had Turned its own child. The First Seat stopped trying to forbid cutting and took charge of it instead. From then on, a cutting needed the Seat's permission, called Cutting-leave. That permission, and the waiting list of towns that want a graft, called the scion-queue, still exist. Rithnali, a Speaker of the First Seat, wrote the record of that summer's meeting. It says the Seat will write down how a cutting is done and will not write down who did the first one. The minute is under "A minute of the First Seat."

### The Closing (Cut-years 19 to 38)

The Closed Seat's power depended on being the only gate to the only Tree. A town that could Turn its own children did not need grove-leave. From Cut-year 19, the Closed Seat sent riders out with sacks to dig up the new grafts and bring them back, or burn them in their pots. They told every square the same sentence: the copy was a theft of the one Tree, so bring the wood home, or burn the pot it was growing in.

They failed. Some of the grafts died, and the towns that meant to keep a cutting hid it in a well and watered it at night. The lodging-towns around the grove, which made their living housing travellers, would not stop housing them to help the Closed Seat, and would not starve a season of beds to please a gate. The First Seat wanted to control who cut, not to destroy trees, and it would not help burn a graft that had already Turned a child. The Closed Seat could not guard every town within a day's walk in every direction, in a world where an order dies with the person sent to carry it. People call this war the Closing, because what they closed in the end was their walls. Folk who watched the riders also call it the Uprooting. The remnant that still keeps the book says the Retreat. No one agrees which pot was the first they failed to pull. Naming that town would pick a hearth, and a hearth would pick a hand.

In Cut-year 38, Hildal *(HIL-dal)*, the last person to speak for the Closed Seat whose name people still use, ordered the Retreat. Hildal was a clerk, not a warlord. The Closed families gave up the grove, moved a day's walk away into a walled city, and took their Book with them. The Book is their record of who owes what, and the walled city is Rothallo, which people still call the Inner Close. They believed they had been doing horticulture and mercy. One Tree, as they told it, cannot bear the whole world coming at once, so someone has to choose who takes the road. That is the same wish to ration the Tree that the First Seat later named Cutting-leave, and the same wish the Inner Close still files as rank. The rule that strikes a Given heir off the roll is older than the Retreat. The war did not invent it. The war moved it off the grove and put it behind a wall.

The Closed Heirs say they closed the walls so the Book would live, and that they sat the grove because someone had to. Folk, the lodging-towns, and the college say the riders went out to pull pots and could not, and that the Retreat was a gate that chose a wall. A Long-Lived person who walked to the grove as a child in Cut-year 10, and a Closed Heir who keeps Hildal's last page, will fight about the same summer, because the memory is perfect and what the summer meant is not. A Salt-Walk house will still skip the years 19 to 38 in its own count, call the gap an origin delay, and collect on the notes.

The Closed Seat's power split four ways, and nobody has put it back together.

- Nobody controls who may stand under the Awakening Tree anymore. A family needs nobody's leave to bring a child there in Turning-Week.
- The First Seat controls who may cut a graft, through Cutting-leave and the scion-queue.
- The lodging-towns joined together and became Orenbren, which houses travellers near the Tree. The hospitality is how the rent is paid. They were not a new crown.
- The Closed families kept the Book, and the Book only governs people who live inside Rothallo's walls. It classifies who owes. It is not a church.

Rothallo is Orenbren's capital, and it lies inside Orenbren, so the Closed families did not found a new country when they moved. They share Orenbren with the lodging-towns. The lodging-towns run the inns outside Rothallo's wall, and the Book runs the city inside it. Strandoren's houses had never been a province of the gate, and the far hearths on the Long Mile had never been held by it. The collapse of the gate did not make that road any shorter.

### The Closed Heirs after the Retreat (Cut-years 38 to 387)

The Closed families are now called the Closed Heirs. After the Retreat they kept the same habits inside Rothallo that the Closed Seat had kept at the grove, and they have kept them for three hundred and forty-nine years. The group that keeps the Book is called the Walled Book. This is what a visitor to Rothallo can see today.

- **Where a person sits.** At a meal in a Closed house, Kept guests sit nearest the host. A guest with a Condition is shown to the far end of the table. When the Book is read aloud, the Kept sit on the front benches and everyone else sits behind them.
- **Whose word a clerk writes down.** When two people disagree in front of a clerk of the Book, and one is Kept and the other has a Condition, the clerk writes the Kept person's account as what happened. The other account is written underneath as "also said." The person with a Condition can still win, but only if a second Kept person says the same thing.
- **Who holds a key.** Only a Kept person may hold a key to a gate in the wall, to the Book's house, to a cistern, or to a granary. A person with a Condition holds the key to their own door and their own stall.
- **What the school teaches.** At the school beside the Book's house, the teacher Volnai tells the children that a hug is rare, and that a child the Tree hugged was kept back for something better than a colour.
- **Who inherits.** A Closed Heir who receives a colour at the Leaf-Fall is struck off the heir-roll. A Closed Heir who is hugged stays on it.
- **Who works.** Everyone who lives inside the walls owes the city turns of work, the way a town expects each household to take a turn on the watch. Kept households mostly keep the watch on the wall and hold the keys. Households with a Condition get the heavier turns, and more of them.

A person with a Condition in Rothallo is not a slave. They work for wages, keep what they earn, marry whom they choose, and can own a stall with a licence from the Book. They can walk out of the gate on any day it is open and live somewhere else. The Book's list of turns has no claim on anyone who lives outside the walls, and the Book cannot send for a person who has left.

The school's lesson gets the numbers wrong. About two children in five are hugged at the Leaf-Fall, and about a quarter of all people stay Kept for their whole lives. Being hugged is common. What is rare is a family like the Closed houses, which has stayed without a Condition on purpose for generations. They do it by striking every Given heir off the roll and keeping their heirs away from the oaths that can Strike a person. The clerks of the Book can read the real count. The school teaches the same lesson every year anyway.

The gate at the grove has been gone since Cut-year 38. The belief that a hugged person ranks above a person with a colour is still alive in Cut-year 387, and in a few places it reaches past Rothallo's walls. Some inns just outside Rothallo's gate keep their best table for Kept guests on nights when a Closed house dines there, and they move a guest with a Condition to another table if the Closed house asks. Brenthael, a town with its own Tree on the Near Mile, has no copy of the Book. At its Turning-Week supper, Kept people still sit on the benches nearest the Tree and people with a Condition sit on the outer benches, and a child who has just received a colour gets up and moves outward before the food is served. Nobody in Brenthael can say when this started. Other places reject it in public. The gift-hall at Eolvaeth, the seat of Vaethorn in the Waiting Lands, teaches that a colour and a hug are equal, and says aloud that the Close is wrong. Given heirs who were struck off the roll eat there. Hithaen is one of them.

In Cut-year 387, a Closed child named Talnin is on the heir-roll and has not yet had a Leaf-Fall. If a colour falls for Talnin, Talnin will be struck off.

### How the grafts spread

From Cut-year 0, the grafts spread outward from the Awakening Tree, town by town, as each new Tree grew old enough to Turn children and to give cuttings of its own. The order is the same everywhere: Maiethorn first, then Strandoren by sea, then Heskoren, which is still receiving them, and Kumbaan never took one. A town that gets a graft still waits about twenty years before its Tree holds its first Leaf-Fall. Until then its children walk to a neighbouring town's Tree. The time since town Trees became the ordinary way to Turn is called the Years of Hands. The spread is told at length in "How the wave moved," and the present in "The Years of Hands."

On Maiethorn, from Cut-year 0 to about 80, the towns nearest the Awakening Tree got grafts first, because a cutting could survive a short trip. By about Cut-year 80, most of Maiethorn's core had its own Trees. The dry east beyond the Rain-Wall mountains, called the Rain-Shadow, was slower, because grafts die in thin, dry soil. Some families there still walk west to the Awakening Tree in Cut-year 387.

On Strandoren, from about Cut-year 40 to 160, ships that had carried children across the Old Crossing began carrying grafts in pots of living earth, kept on deck and watered at dawn. The rich port cities could pay for Cutting-leave and a ship in the same summer, so they got grafts first, while some Maiethorn villages were still waiting. Strandoren's inland towns got grafts later, by river and cart. By about Cut-year 160, the coasts had mature Trees.

Then came a wait of about forty years, from Cut-year 160 to 200, in which no graft reached Heskoren. Cuttings died on the long road. A cutting has to come from the Awakening Tree or a mature town Tree, and the line of mature Trees took time to grow. The First Seat's list served towns that could pay first. Heskoren did not wait because its people were unworthy, whatever some Vaethorn preachers say.

From Cut-year 200 to the present, grafts began arriving by sea from Strandoren to Heskoren's coast. The luckiest towns, such as Harrow's Green, got a graft in the 200s and have Turned their own children for a few generations. Many districts still have no Tree. Their ten-year-olds walk to a neighbouring town, or walk the Long Mile, as children did in the Walking Years. The Three Hamlets Past the Ford can see Harrow's Tree from their fields and are still on the list. This is happening now.

No graft has crossed the storm-wall around Kumbaan, the drowning weather that closes the isle. Sailors tell of pots that went into the sea. Dead wood is not a Tree, and Kumbaan's people count moons, not Cut-years.

A town Tree can also sicken and stop holding a Leaf-Fall. When that happens, the town's children go back to walking to a neighbour, even in Maiethorn. The Mill-hold is a town where that has happened.

### Other dated years

These events happened while the grafts were spreading. They sit beside the history of the Trees, and they are not another age of the world. "The Other Count" below sets them in a table.

In Cut-year 67, the old hymn-country of the Sacred Core split in two over whether to write people down. People call that split the Two Papers. Limrae opened a desk in a river-town and began recording every person, saying that a person you cannot find again is a person you cannot look after. Manril, in the country around, refused to be put on the list. Manril said a person belongs because people saw them Turn, and stays well because their neighbours supply what their Condition needs. A Tithe is that upkeep, the thing a Condition asks of the person who carries it. There was no battle. Households chose which desk to deal with. The side that kept the list became Threnmaieth, the Tallied Crown, and the side that refused became Maiethvael. Threnmaieth counts years from that summer, so Cut-year 387 is Crown-year 320 there. That list is a later paper. It is the same wish to reckon people, carried into a country of hymns, and it is not the Closing's child in a straight line.

In Cut-year 171, a fever started in the crowded holds of ships and spread through the ports on both sides of the Old Crossing, on the Hinge Shore and on the Ledger Coast. People call that summer the Grey Summer, and on the docks it is the Salt-Grey. It was an ordinary disease, not a Tithe. The fever killed people who had a town Tree, a guild ticket, and their prayers said, the same as anyone else. Ships stayed in port all summer. Some grafts went unwatered because the Taken-In, the people the wild has taken partway into itself and given back able to tend living wood, were dead, and a few coastal towns sent their children to a neighbour's Tree the next year. Long-Lived bankers did not die. Their borrowers did, and the debts passed to the families. Afterward, Trenledd began keeping a list of persons, and Dirrol added a column for who was still alive. In Cut-year 174 the small towns of Brenledd put their charters together into one league, so that nobody could set their prices one town at a time. Leddvael made the signing of a contract a public ceremony, because so many witnesses had died. For about ten years fewer ships sailed toward Heskoren. Deathless clerks still write "Grey + 1" on the back of a note that survived the fever.

In Cut-year 233, the snow on the Rain-Wall in Lirorn melted in one week instead of over a month. People call it the Thaw-Break, and on the ridges the one-week melt. Valleys flooded, people died, and one well-town lost its cistern to silt. The old high road over the Noon Pass was washed out. Narol of the Pass took the toll on the new, lower road, the Shelf-gate, and Narol's house still collects it. The west road in Saelthael was cut for two summers, so children who should have walked to the Awakening Tree in 233 and 234 went late, went to a neighbour, or missed their week. A graft on its way to Ornled died because the cart was late to the quay. Vathne wrote on a wall, "We will not wait with a hymn for a stick that drowned in someone else's thaw." Vathne meant that Ornled would stop praying and waiting for grafts that other countries' troubles could take away. Ornled still copies that sentence.

In Cut-year 248, at Harrow's Green, a warden announced copper-green for a child the Tree had actually hugged, because the town wanted a Taken-In. The child was sent to learn a Taken-In's work, and the green did not take, because there had been no Gift to tend. A visiting Speaker of the First Seat corrected that announcement two years later. It was a person's doing, not the Tree's. People call the year the Wrong Green. Speakers still name it when they teach wardens not to announce the colour a town wishes it had.

In Cut-year 280, Ledan, a Long-Lived clerk of the White Note House, wrote to the First Seat to ask which year number to put on a hundred-year loan, because the bank, the borrower's town, and the Seat each counted the year differently. Cut-year 280 was the bank's 200th year. The bank still converts its years the way Ledan's letter did, and Cut-year 387 is the White Note's 307th year. The letter is under "A clerk's query."

From Cut-year 299 to 304, the Hinge Shore and the Ledger Coast fought for five summers over who set the price of crossing the Old Crossing. They seized each other's ships and blocked each other's quays. People call those summers the Hinge War. Taerso spoke for the Hinge Shore's houses, and Sirtol spoke for a group of Ledger Coast charters. In Cut-year 304 they agreed a peace that names no winner. There is one charge for the crossing, called the Hush-rate, and it is still collected. The Hinge Shore gives a blessing to anyone who wants one, and the Ledger Coast writes the paperwork. The same peace covers the far crossing to Heskoren, and Netstrand keeps the ships that sail it. Saelvaeth may receive a graft without paying a blessing-fee to Vaethorn, while Vaethorn may bless a landing and may not tax it. Ornled charges a fee when a ship puts in on the beach. People call the peace the Hinge Hush.

### Three old places you can still walk into

Three stone places come from before anyone counted years. Nobody knows when they were built or by whom, and the people who look after them will not let anyone cut a year into them. A party can visit all three. They were found, not founded, and they do not give the Tree a year.

The Low Wall is a low stone wall in Orenbren's lodging-country, off the Near Mile, within the first few days' walk from the Awakening Tree. It is not part of Rothallo's wall, and the stone is a different colour. People who tell you the two walls are the same wall are making a claim. It has three gaps that people use as gates: one widened for carts, one only wide enough for a single person, and one with an old post-socket. Midway along is a stone panel whose letters were chiselled off long ago. The wall ends in the back garden of an inn, where Thuda rents beds in the loft. The Closed Heirs say the wall is theirs. The Watching say it is older than any count. In Cut-year 381, Nurreith and three lodging-guild workers dug under the panel. Delamem, who keeps the Book of Tithes, wanted a chip of stone that matched Rothallo's quarry. It did not match. They filled the hole in the same week.

The Seeing-Ring is a ring of standing stones on a hill in Maiethorn's old Watching country. It is not the Awakening Tree's grove. The stones are set so that a person walking around the ring turns to look outward. Four wider gaps face outward. One faces the Awakening Tree, one faces the Old Crossing, and two face nothing anyone can name. Later someone built a shrine on top of the ring, and later still someone took the roof off and left the steps. Tithsael, of the Watching, keeps a night there every week and lets visitors sit with them, unless the visitor has brought a colour-ribbon to tie on the stones or means to carve a year into them.

The Dry Stair is a flight of steps cut into a rise in Saelthael's dry country, above a small well-town that is not Ornsael. The steps climb to a door-shaped stone with no wall around it and a basin cut in the rock that has never held water in living memory. A tree-warden once planted a cutting in the basin, and it died. Local children dare each other to climb the last step. Behind the basin there is a narrow crawl to a second dry basin lower in the rock.

### The memory cup

The memory cup is a late practice among the Long-Lived. It began in the Years of Hands, long after the First Cut, and the circles that keep it do not write down the year it started. It is old enough that people have written about it at length. Long-Lived scholars who are not members have written commentaries on it, and those are kept in the long-houses where the Long-Lived meet. The Motherfaith, the faith that speaks of the Mother, has said what it thinks the cup is, and so have the Watching and the Fair Hand, the faith that dates a bargain from the day it was signed. It is recent enough that a Long-Lived person who walked to the Awakening Tree as a child, before their town had a Tree of its own, can say that nobody did this when they were young.

The group that keeps the cup is the Pourers, also called the Cup Circle. Every Long-Lived person is asked to join once, in person, by a member. A person who says no is not punished, keeps their standing, and is still given a seat in the long-houses. Most Long-Lived people are not members, and all of them know the circle exists.

A member gives one year of their memory into a cup at a time, of their own free will. Each year is its own night, with its own minutes, and the member may stop before the next. The circle asks five years of a member in all, and for each of those years the member adds one drop of their own blood, because the Long-Lived read memory from blood. Afterward the member no longer has the memories of that year. They still need to keep learning, as every Long-Lived person does, and what they lose is a year of remembering, including the deaths of the people they knew in that year.

Members say they give "the memories those years held, and the years themselves." The second part means two things. For each year given, the member also gives the long-house shelf all the papers they wrote or kept in that year, such as letters, account books, and diaries, and the long-houses that keep the circle stop counting that year in the member's age, so a member who has given all five years is seated as if five years younger. Among the Long-Lived, age decides who speaks first and who keeps a key, so each gift costs a member rank in those long-houses. Courts, banks, guilds, and Long-Lived who are not members keep counting the ordinary way.

Members can read the cup the way a Long-Lived reads a drop of blood, and what they read is true. One reading can hold several members' memories of the same year. Members also keep books in the Long-Lived library in Lestrand, on the Ledger Coast, and they make copies of books in the Old Tongue and in the common speech for it. People use those books. Molevad keeps the minutes of the best-known circle in the same library. A second circle meets in an upper room of a lodging-house in Orenbren and does not ask Molevad's circle for permission.

A member may leave, and the years already given stay in the cup. What comes back to the member is their papers, which members call a thinner copy. The other members stop reading blood with them, and the long-houses that keep the circle stop giving them a seat.

The circle claims that by giving to the cup they made a god, which they call the Poured God, and among themselves the Cup We Made. They say every gift is freely given. That claim is the circle's belief. The Pourers are a separate group from the Walled Book, and from the Protectors, the quiet house that says it keeps the one Phoenix in its care so the stories will stop arriving at ordinary doors. A Phoenix is the one person in the world who dies and rises again, each time carrying less of who they were.

### What nobody knows

Nobody knows who made the First Cut. Five different accounts are told, and this history does not pick one. In one, a parent stole the branch so that no more children would stay UnTurned for want of the trip, or die on the road. In another, a tree-warden cut it before the First Seat allowed cutting. The devout say the Tree let a branch come away. The Watching say people had always grafted trees and the count was added later. The Old Ways say the land received a guest.

Nobody knows how old the Awakening Tree is. It was standing before anyone counted years, and the Watching argue against any date anyone proposes.

Nobody has settled whether there is a Leaf-Mother. People who believe in her ask why her Trees could not Turn children far away until a graft was planted near them, and why Kumbaan has no Tree. Believers do not agree on the answer. Skeptics say distance and soil explain everything, and that the Tree is a plant. This history does not answer the question.

## How years are numbered

There is no year-zero that everyone shares. The calendar these counts sit on has the same twelve months, and it has three different days for the new year. The past is the same past. There are seven honest ways to count it, and the number a clerk writes on a debt is a theology, because the year you choose is the place you are willing to start the story from.

| Who | Counts from | Sounds like |
|---|---|---|

| Kumbaan | moons | no mainland count |

The present is Cut-year 387, the 387th summer since the First Cut. It is not a round number chosen to look finished.

What nobody can date is older than that number. The Awakening Tree is older than any reliable dating, and Before the Walk has no start. Who made the First Cut is contested, and the argument is kept without a winner. Believers ask why her Trees reached the far lands late, and skeptics say there is no Leaf-Mother. The Low Wall, the Seeing-Ring, and the Dry Stair are stone places from before the walk. People found them standing. They carry no builder's name.

Cut-year 0 is a graft. It is the first witnessed copy of living wood, and it is not the first Condition, not the first Giving, and not the Tree's birthday. The Giving, and the walk that carried children to it, have no start year. Becoming at a door is older than the walk. Being born a Kind is older than both.

### The Other Count

The years on this table are the dated ones inside Cut-year 0 to Cut-year 387 that are not the spread of grafts. Two clocks still organize that past. One counts how you were Turned, by a walk or under a town Tree. The other counts where the wood has reached. These three hundred and eighty-seven summers also held a war, a split over paper, a fever, a flood, and a hush that is still the peace on the Old Crossing. This count sits beside the clocks. It does not take their place. A clerk who writes only Eoloren-years can still be asked what the Grey Summer did to a note. A hearth in Vaethorn that dates itself from "the year our graft took" can still owe a hull-fee under the Hinge Hush.

In the Walking Years the grove at the origin was not open to everyone who arrived. A class of the untithed, people of whom no Tithe was asked, sat on Thaeloren and decided who might walk up to it. They were a gate that grew a country around the one Tree, and not a banner planted on all three continents. Strandoren paid for the Salt Walk and was never a province of that gate. Heskoren lay beyond a ride from that gate, and Kumbaan was not in the story. Who held the nameless knife, the person who made the first cut, is still unwritten. The First Seat stays in the wood. Rothallo, still called the Inner Close, is a walled city inside Orenbren, and it is Orenbren's capital. It is not a sixteenth power.

The present year is Cut-year 387. Who cut remains unpicked. A chronicle that writes a first king onto the Before is laying a later story on a time that was left uncounted. The Watching warn against that.

Maiethorn is what followed the grove-gate: lodging, gift, reckoning, hinge, thaw, and dry sowing. Those are six successors of one gate, not six ancient kingdoms. Strandoren is what the Salt Walk became once the gate was dead and the fever had made people write new paper. Heskoren came into the clocks late, and host-rights were already there. Waiting, sowing, and slate are three answers to the same cart that arrived late, not three eternal peoples. Borders and hull-clauses have causes. They do not have a first king.

What a traveller notices, besides the wood, is often a date that will not sit still. A debt can carry three dates, and a fourth if the note survived the Grey Summer: a Cut-year, a house-year, the year a graft took, and "the summer after the fever." A kitchen still will not send a child through the Inner Close's gate, and cannot say whether the fear is the Book or the older Closed Seat. In Lirorn, a stone on the pass has a water-line over the old ribbon-notch, above the mark where a ribbon used to be tied. A quay on the Hinge Shore charges the Hush-rate and will not call it a victory. The Watching keep a night on a ring of stones that is older than any Cut-year anyone will write down.

## What the two clocks did

These are the lasting effects of the two clocks on law, money, the clergy, language, the map, and faith.

**Law.** Citizenship because someone local saw you Turned is the ordinary way belonging works in the Years of Hands. In the Walking Years the town that saw you Turn was the crowd under Thaeloren, or it was nobody, if you never arrived. Children in the far lands who stayed UnTurned were not simply missing from a roll. They were unwitnessed as Given, because they did not make the trip. People invented ways to carry that original witness home across the distance: a Long-Lived person who remembers the fall at the origin, the word of someone Bound, who swore a contract in extremity and whose sworn word holds, and the feeling of someone Far-Voiced, whose voice carries a feeling exactly as far and as true as they mean it to. That vouching at a distance was made so the origin's witness could travel. The Struck who were never vouched for are older than the grafts, because a becoming on a far threshold was always unwitnessed. The Grafting did not invent that unease. It localized who is expected to have been seen, and it put that expectation in the town where the person lives.

**Money.** Many of the deathless houses, the houses of the Long-Lived, began as road-houses. They were inns, they held endowments, and they extended credit across a century to families who had to walk. When the walk stopped being required in the core, the lands nearest the Awakening Tree, those houses still had a Long-Lived memory of every debtor on the road, and they had paper that outlived the pilgrimage season. They became banks. The endow-not-inherit loop, in which a house is endowed rather than passed to an heir, is older than the Years of Hands. The trusted note, the paper a house will still honour years later, is an instrument from the Walking Years, and it outlived the road it was written for. The same houses are still here, with a different business, and the banking people use now is old.

**Clergy.** Cutting-leave is how the First Seat captured a practice that had begun without the Seat's permission. People argued the first successful cut as theft, as mercy, as the Tree offering a branch, and as old horticulture that the Motherfaith later gave a name. The Seat won the authorization, the right to say which cutting may be made. The scion-queue is the Grafting's child. It is the waiting list that appears, and the bottleneck with it, when someone has the power to decide which town is next.

**Language.** The pilgrimage kept the old speech from drifting. Once a year, people from far away walked to the same grove and heard the same liturgy, so the Old Tongue stayed nearer to itself along the road. When a town had its own Tree, its children grew up where they were born, and the speech grew there with them. That is why the drift in the Old Tongue follows how thin the Trees are, and why Lestrand moved so far from the old speech, and so quickly. Lestrand had trade, it had a canopy of its own, and nobody there needed to walk to the Motherwood, the grove of the Awakening Tree. The Old Tongue split into three daughter-drifts, later speeches descended from that root where the Trees grew thin: one that Threnmaieth still keeps close to the liturgy, a worn speech on the Vaethorn frontier, where the written name Vaethorn is said Wetorn, and an eroded speech on the Lestrand coast, which moved the farthest and the fastest. Those three belong to the Years of Hands, sitting on a root the Walking Years had held nearer to itself.

**Geography.** The graded reach of the Trees across the map is the Grafting as it stands in Cut-year 387. Maiethorn is old in the Years of Hands, and its towns have had their own Trees for a long time. Strandoren is mature in those same years, and the commerce that carried the grafts is still in the way the country works. Heskoren is the Grafting in this decade, the place the wood is arriving now. Kumbaan never entered either clock. To someone living in the world, this reads as distance and as history, and that reading is true as far as it goes.

**Faith.** The Watching remember Before the Walk as the Before, a seeing before anyone had called the Tree a mother, and they will not start history at a Cut. The Old Ways were already becoming, already taking a Condition at their own doors, before any pilgrim road existed, and for them the Years of Hands are when guests arrived late. The Fair Hand dates a contract from the day it was signed, not from the First Cut in the wood, and it treats the Grafting as a fact about the supply of wood. The Open Table, the faith kept on Kumbaan, which grew with no Tree, owes these ages nothing. It does not count its life from them.

### What a traveller notices

- A Long-Lived person says "I walked," and the room goes quiet. That is the personal clock, how you were Turned, spoken as a life.
- A mile-shrine stands on a road that now ends at a town Tree. The stone was set for a Turning that no longer has to happen on that road. The devout still leave a ribbon on it, and the town's children climb it.
- An old inn lets its upper room, once full of pilgrim-beds, to merchants, and the beds are empty of pilgrims. The deathless house that owns the inn still dates its notes from the founding-summer, the summer those beds were full.
- One debt carries three dates for the same summer: a Cut-year, a house-year, and the year the graft took. A fourth date appears if the note survived the Grey Summer.
- A hamlet on Heskoren still sends its ten-year-olds to walk to a neighbour, and that walk is this year's necessity, not a history the hamlet has decided to perform.
- Someone names a Phoenix as a way to mark an era, "the Phoenix of the last Walking summer," and nobody in the room agrees which summer that was.

## Before the Walk

Before the Walk is the unnamed preface. The Awakening Tree was already standing before anyone had a year to set it in. People already had Kinds, and some had already become something at doors the later faiths would struggle to place. This stretch of time has no honest start. The ages that follow it are the spine above.

Most of the world does not have a name for this time. The devout skip it, and skeptics call it "before anyone bothered to walk," which is a shrug, not a chronicle. The one name that is still lived, rather than shrugged off, is kept by a single faith.

The Watching keep that name: the Before. In their telling the world was Kind and Kind alone, what people already were and not yet anything they became, and then the Tree was already there, watching. The Giving, their name for the later story in which people began to be Given, came after the Watching. The Mother-name, the later story that calls the Tree a mother, came after that. Each of those is a later layer, and each layer is a story laid over a seeing.

The Old Ways do not grant that becoming waited on a road. Heskoren's people already crossed doors, a drowning, a grove, a stone, before any pilgrim mile. When the Walking Years arrived as a foreign traffic, they were not the beginning of history. They were the beginning of company.

Kumbaan's hill-history does not owe Thaeloren a preface. The Open Table grew there with no Tree and no Mother-name.

What can be said without inventing kings is only this. The origin Tree is the one Tree no one grafted from another. Nobody grew it from a cutting of some earlier tree, and it was not planted in a remembered summer. Far from it, people lived as they lived. Some were Struck at thresholds because thresholds exist: a door was already there, and a person became something at it, with no journey organized to bring that about. Some were never either Given or Struck, because there was no local fall to miss. Where nothing nearby offered a fall, they were not people who had failed to attend one. When people organized the tenth-year trip, and a child's tenth year became a journey households could plan, the Walking Years began. That organization is the start of the walk, not the start of the world.

None of it is dated. That is the point. A later chronicle that discovers a first king, a first war, or a first sermon in this era is laying a story that has a beginning over a seeing that was never given one. The Low Wall, the Seeing-Ring, and the Dry Stair can still be walked into. They do not start a year.

## The Walking Years

Once there was only the Awakening Tree, and a child's tenth-year Turning, standing under that Tree where they might be Given what they would become, meant a walk to it. Families who could not make the trip stayed UnTurned for want of it, never Given, and folk called them Kept, so the far reaches ran heavy on the UnTurned and the Struck as the ordinary count, not as an edge. Everyday speech calls the time the Walking Years. The liturgical name is Brenvaeth *(BREN-vaeth)*, "the hearth gone to the waiting." It grows out of Before the Walk with no dated start.

In these years the Leaf-Fall happened in one place: one grove, on Maiethorn, under Thaeloren. A tenth summer was a journey, or it was no Leaf-Fall at all, because you were not there.

The far-flung who stayed home were not people a town could prove a goddess had skipped. They were children who did not walk. Some were later Struck at a drowning, an oath, or a death, a becoming with no leaf and no crowd, because the only crowd that counted was a continent away. Some stayed UnTurned for life, wanted or not, because want of a trip is a distance, not a theology.

This is where today's thin reach comes from, stated as a fact of the map. Thin reach used to mean far from the one Tree. After the First Cut, thin reach came to mean places where the Trees are young, sick, or few, which is why a hamlet next door to a dying scion can be "far" in the old sense without being far on a map.

The pilgrimage did not vanish when grafts began. It changed jobs. In the Years of Hands the devout still walk to the origin, and the poor and the Tree-bereaved still walk to a neighbour. The Walking Years are the time when everyone who Turned as Given had walked, or had been carried.

The later romance of the old mile is a feeling from the Years of Hands, laid on graves from the Walking Years.

The era has no dated start. It grows out of Before the Walk when the trip becomes the way to Turn. It has a dated hinge, the First Cut, at Cut-year 0. After that the Walking Years cease to be the default in lands the wood has reached, and they remain the default in lands it has not. Heskoren is still, in places, in this era. That is this year's road, not a metaphor.

### The door is one week

Turning-Week is the week around the High Solstice in Eolthael, the month people also call Turning-month, because that month holds the sun's high turning. Every child who has turned ten since the last High Solstice Turns that week, under Thaeloren, or they do not Turn as Given at all.

If the child misses that week, the door has shut for good, and an eleven-year-old who reaches the origin the following summer is only a visitor, because the Tree's Given-door is that tenth-year group, in that week, and not a standing appointment you can make up later. Doors that Strike a person still open afterward, elsewhere, with no leaf and no crowd. That later mercy was always the far lands' other path.

This is why families from any distance left early, and why the richest or most frightened households from far away spent a whole year at the origin. A nine-year-old wintered in a road-house so the child could not miss. That held-over child is the expensive mercy. Everyone else gambled one summer.

Towns that later grew a Tree of their own did not grow a second door. A fever in your own square during Eolthael shuts the week the same way a late arrival on the Long Mile does. There is still no year in which an older child is Given because they tried.

### Three walks, not one road

People named the walks in the common tongue, because the walk was a problem of food and berths before it was a liturgy.

| Walk | Who used it | How long | What it cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **the Near Mile** | Maiethorn inland, anyone already on the Motherland | three to twelve days | a harvest-timing; almost everyone who meant to come, came |
| **the Salt Walk** | Strandoren: ship the Old Crossing, then the Near Mile | three to five days' sail plus the inland days; usually one to three weeks in all | a berth in fair weather; miss the last crossing and you miss the year |
| **the Long Mile** | Heskoren, and the Strandoren interior before the coasts were rich | six to twelve weeks | a year's surplus, or a road-house note, or you did not go |

On the road, people did not say Brenvaeth. They said "We are on the Long Mile," or "We took the salt." The later names still sort by who is speaking. Vaethorn, in the Waiting Lands, calls the whole past the waiting-road. Lestrand, on the Ledger Coast, files it as the road-years. Threnmaieth, the Tallied Crown, wants the miles on a roll.

The timing sat against the year, and the month names are the mainland's. Near-Mile families left in Haelren, which people also call Hale-month, when the land is counted sound, or in early Eolthael, and were home by Vaelorn, Gift-month, after the Turning, when the colours settle. Salt-Walk households watched the Old Crossing from Saelorn, Seed-month, the first green, and a late storm in Hale-month left an UnTurned child. Long-Mile columns left in Liren, the Thaw, when the water runs again, or in Saelorn, and hoped the High Solstice had not happened without them. Some arrived in Leddorn, which people call Ledger-month, the late-summer markets, with a ten-year-old who had already missed. The road-houses knew that silence. They did not charge extra for it. They also did not refund the note.

No walk crossed the storm-wall to Kumbaan.

### Who could afford it

The Near Mile was a civic season. Whole towns moved a short way together, and a poor child walked in a neighbour's dust. The cost was real, lost labour and a week of food, and it was rarely what decided whether a child went.

The Salt Walk sorted people by cash and weather. Coastal wealth on Strandoren bought berths. The interior of Strandoren was already "far" in these years, even though the coasts would later be rich in Trees. A family might send the Turning-child and one adult, and leave the rest. Guilds sometimes advanced a berth against a future colour, so that a Given Answered, a person an element has gone on answering, or a Far-Voiced was worth the loan, while a hug was a debt with no new trade in it. The Fair Hand would later call this a term. In Brenvaeth it was just the quay.

The Long Mile sorted people by whether you could lose a year. You needed food, shoes, a second adult if you had one, and the note you signed at the first road-house that would take it. Many could not afford that. Those who could often could not afford it for every child.

The split household is the era's ordinary cruelty. A family with three children near ten could sometimes walk one of them, so the one who walked might come home Given, and the ones who stayed were UnTurned, or were Struck later at a local door, and grew up in the same kitchen as a sibling the origin had seen. Distance did this. Vaethorn's later guilt, the sermon that says "we wait because we are unworthy," is a reading from the Years of Hands laid over that fact. The miles do not agree with the guilt.

Old Ways households on Heskoren often refused the walk on purpose. Their children were already the land's. Motherfaith neighbours, counting a child by whether the trip had been made, read those children as UnTurned for want of the trip, which is a misreading the walk itself cannot correct. Both families are telling the truth they have.

### Who was let through

Distance and coin were not the only things that sorted a child. A class of the untithed sat on Thaeloren and decided who might stand under it. Folk called them the Closed Seat, and also the Grove-Sitters. A family could have shoes and a Salt-Walk berth and still be turned away at the gate. Grove-leave was the paper you carried to that gate if you hoped to be let through.

Far lands ran heavy on the UnTurned because some children did not walk, and because some children walked and were turned away. Both of those fill a kitchen. The later romance of the old mile prefers the children who never started, and the folk songs remember the ones who arrived and were turned from the gate.

The Closed Seat was a gate at the origin, not an empire of the world. Strandoren paid the crossing and was never a province of that gate, while Heskoren lay beyond a gate's ride. When a Near-Mile town Turned under a copy, the gate was no longer the only seeing. The war that tried to make that untrue is the Closing, told in "A history in order." The road was already a life. The gate was a third cruelty laid on it.

### Who died

Children died on the road, and so did the adults who carried them. They died at river crossings in the thaw, of a fever in a packed Near-Mile inn, in a late squall on the Old Crossing, of hunger on a Long-Mile stretch between hearths, and in a birth that would not wait for the grove. This is not a slaughter-age and not a hymn. It is a problem of getting people down a long road, and long roads kill people.

The graves are mostly unmarked at first. Then someone sets a stone at a day's walk, because the next column needs to know the water, and because a name should sit somewhere. A ribbon goes on the stone the year after, and a later column uses the stone as a mile-mark. The romance and the graves are the same object seen at two hours of the day.

A child who dies on the way to be witnessed may Strike as Returned on the road. A Returned is a person who died with something unfinished and whose purpose keeps them walking, so this was a becoming with no crowd, on the way to the only crowd that counted. It is rare, and it is remembered.

The origin also filled with children who arrived and had no one to walk home with. Road-houses took some in, and a bed became a life. Origin-towns took some. A Given child with no one to carry the witness home is the other half of the grave: they lived, and belonging still had to be invented for them.

### Far reaches as the rule

In Brenvaeth, a land far from Thaeloren was heavy with the UnTurned and the Struck because most ten-year-olds never stood under the canopy. That was not a curiosity of the frontier. It was how the world worked anywhere the walk was too far or too dear.

The Given were the ones who had been there, a minority that shrank as the miles piled up. They came home as people who could do work the town could not otherwise grow, and as people the town had not watched become.

The UnTurned were the ordinary far childhood, because they never stood under the Tree. They were whole, untithed, and unwitnessed as Given, and folk called them Kept. The few who walked and were hugged were Kept in the strict sense, kept by the Tree with no colour. Some of the UnTurned were wanted that way. Some were the price of a summer the family could not raise. Some were Old Ways children the Motherfaith had misfiled.

The Struck were the far lands' other door, and they always had been. A drowning, an oath, or a death made them, with no leaf and no origin-crowd. The unease that follows the Struck is older than the grafts. The walk only made it louder, because the Given in the same town had been seen, a continent away.

When the wood later travelled, this pattern moved. A pocket with few Trees, next to a dying scion, reads the same way on a count of who was seen, and it is not far on a map. Some of today's Heskoren is still Brenvaeth, this year, for this group of children. Some of it is a neighbour's canopy and a queue. Both can be true on the same road.

### Institutions of the road

There was no travelling church with offices in these years. Tree-wardens who carry grafts belong to the Years of Hands. In Brenvaeth the origin-hearth was the only Speaking that counted, the only place where a speaker under Thaeloren could name what a child had become and have that naming travel. Far towns had no Tree to tend, so what the road had was inns, stones, and summer traffic.

#### Mile-shrines

A mile-shrine is a stone at about a day's walk, with water if the ground allows, a flat top where a pack can sit, and often a name and a ribbon.

They begin as graves and waymarks in the same act. You bury someone where the column had to stop, and the next year the stop is the place you aim for. The devout later leave ribbons for the Tree, children later climb the stones, and Hands-towns later argue about pulling them down, because the road now ends at a local canopy and the stone is in the way. The stone does not know which story it is in.

A ribbon is not a leaf-colour. People tie what they have, so a copper-green scrap on a Long-Mile stone does not mean a Taken-In died there, even when a traveller who has only heard the romance reads the green that way. Travellers who have walked once know the difference. Travellers who have only heard the romance do not.

#### Road-houses

Many deathless houses began on this road. In Brenvaeth they were road-houses: beds, porridge, a dry room, and credit for a walk you could not miss twice.

The everyday name is road-house. The cant is brenhael *(BREN-hael)*, from *bren* and *hael*, "the hearth kept whole." The Waiting Lands say waiting-hearth. The Ledger Coast says the bed-house, and still means the bank. The Tallied Crown would like them on the roll.

What a road-house actually sold was ordinary, and several later institutions are that ordinary thing with a different job.

They sold a bed in the two crowded months, and a quieter bed in the other ten. Held-over nine-year-olds were the winter trade. An empty pilgrim-bed in an upper room of the Years of Hands used to be this bed, in the season when the road was full.

They sold a note. You eat now, and you pay at the origin, or on the way home, or when the child is grown, or when a sibling walks next year. The Long-Lived innkeeper forgets none of it, which is why the paper will outlive the pilgrimage season and become the trusted note. In the act, it is an innkeeper who will still know your name in fifty years, which is a kindness and a leash.

They kept a little furnishing for a Tithe, improvised, and not a guild. A green-lot behind the house was there for a Taken-In parent, and a shed was there so a Far-Voiced could cry without taking the dormitory with them. The road itself is novelty enough that a Long-Lived innkeeper's own Tithe stays fed, which is why the deathless stayed in the trade, and why the same people later endow libraries: the hunger that kept them on the mile is the hunger that built the archive. Brenvaeth had a pot and a shed.

They kept a held bed. A family said they would come, and they did not, so the innkeeper keeps the bed a year, then two, then as a habit that looks like hospitality and functions as a shrine. The slate below is one of those beds, still being made.

The White Note's founding-summer is a house-year, not a Cut-year. In the Walking Years that house was a hearth with too many boots by the door. This is the same fortune, earlier.

#### Summer traffic

For two months the Near Mile is a moving town, columns and dust, with Far-Voiced cries carrying from ridge to ridge: the road is full, the ford is wrong, a child is down. The Two-Bodied, people who carry a second animal self and can use it at will, range the edges, because the ranging is the Tithe and the column is a herd to keep. Markets grow at the mile-shrines and vanish when Eolthael ends. Then the road is empty and local, and the road-house counts the notes.

There were no pilgrim-knights and no official convoy. People walked with whoever left the same week. A Bound's word might hold a column together for a fortnight, and a Bound cannot cheaply lie about what they swear. A Kept adult with no Tithe to manage was, then as now, the person who could carry the extra pack.

### Witness at the origin, or not at all

Belonging because your own town saw you Turn comes later, with the Years of Hands. In Brenvaeth the town that saw you Turn was the crowd under Thaeloren, or it was no one.

Going home did not bring that crowd with you. Your village had not watched. Belonging at home required someone who had been under the boughs to stand for you: a parent, a neighbour from the column, or a Long-Lived who keeps origin-falls the way they later keep town-falls. Vouching at a distance was invented to carry the origin's witness home. The three pillars of that vouch, Long-Lived memory, a Bound's word, and a Far-Voiced feeling, are older than the grafts. The job of carrying a far Turning is what belongs to the Walking Years.

A Given child with no surviving witness is thin in the law of the place they grew up. Origin-orphans, road-house children, and a Bound stranger who will swear the colour they did not see, and who cannot cheaply lie, are all versions of that thinness. The unvouched are older than the unease that later attached to the Struck, and they include people the Tree did Give.

Someone Struck on the road carries a double unease: an unwitnessed becoming, and that becoming happening on the way to the witnessing. A Returned child may walk the last days of the Near Mile beside the parent who has not yet forgiven the threshold.

Staying home meant you were never witnessed as Given. You were still of your town, because your town had watched you be a child. An UnTurned child was not a blank on a roll that had never been written. Folk called such children Kept, though no Tree had hugged them.

The origin-crowd was enormous in Eolthael and ordinary the rest of the year. Speakers of the origin-hearth named colours until their voices went. The colours were already known to folk. The warden's word made them count for people who would have to carry that word home.

### Long-Lived who still say "I walked"

A Long-Lived person who says "I walked" is saying when they were young, and the memory of that walk is perfect even when what they mean by the sentence is not. There is no single Long-Lived account.

At present, Cut-year 387, the sentence does at least four jobs, and the people who say it argue about which job is being done.

Some mean they walked as a child, under Thaeloren, before their town had wood. A Long-Lived person on Maiethorn who was Given in the late Walking, among the last cohorts in the core before Maiethorn filled during the first Cut-years, is speaking of a summer most of the room has only as a ribbon. A Long-Lived person on Heskoren may mean last decade. The words are the same. The wood reached their towns at different times.

Some mean they walked the mile as the inn, and a road-house Long-Lived may never have stood under the canopy as a pilgrim at all. They walked the same stretch every summer for two hundred years, so they remember every debtor, and they will not always tell you which children came back.

Some remember the graves and will not call them holy. These are the skeptics among the deathless, and they say the road was bodies and bad fords. They are sincere, and they still tear up at a ribbon, which they will not explain.

Some remember the graves and will not call them a problem of logistics. These are the devout among the deathless, and they say every stone was a waiting. They are sincere, and they still know which ford killed which name, which they will tell you whether you asked or not.

A Blood-Read, a Long-Lived reading of the memory in a drop of blood, will give you a true fragment of a walk. It will not tell you which of the four jobs the living speaker is doing.

They also disagree with Seat chronicles that start history at the First Cut. A Long-Lived person who walked as a child, and a clerk who counts Cut-years, will fight about the same summer, and both can be honest, though only one of them was there.

### What a character notices

- In Hale-month the Near Mile is dusty with people on the move, and in Ledger-month that same road has no dust on it, because the walking season is over.
- A mile-shrine carries a ribbon and the scuffs of someone climbing it, and a child of the Years of Hands looking at the stone does not know it was a grave.
- An inn's upper room has more beds than the present trade can fill, and the house still dates its notes from a founding-summer when those beds were full.
- A held bed has not been let in anyone's living mortal memory. The innkeeper can tell you the name on it. They will not tell you whether they think the child is dead, or Kept, or of the Old Ways, or was never born.
- A pair of siblings will not walk the same side of a road. One of them is Given, and the other never stood under a Tree, so that second child is UnTurned, though folk still call them Kept.
- A Far-Voiced cry goes out along a ridge in the old pattern, "the ford is wrong." No column answers. Birds do.
- Someone at a table says "I walked," and the room sorts itself by who goes quiet.

### A Turning-Week slate

What follows is a Turning-Week slate from the third hearth on the Near Mile, kept by Thilim, who is Long-Lived and has kept this house since before the Cut, as later clerks would say. The back of the sheet is knife-scored with tally-marks that do not match the front.

> *Eolthael, the summer the ford ran high.*
>
> *Beds: forty-and-two spoken for. Notes out: Tora of the long cape, two people, pay on the home-walk. Valein of the salt, one person, paid at the quay. The Held: Seine of the far cape — tenth summer, not come. Hold the bed. The mother said next year. Next year was last year.*
>
> *Porridge: enough. Shed: the blue-leaf girl from the second column cried the rafters quiet; no charge; we needed the quiet. Green-lot: used. The Taken-In man from the long cape left a cutting. I put it in the pot. It is not a Tree.*
>
> *I do not ask which summer was holy. I ask which people will be here at dark. The Held bed is made. If Seine comes I will not have to remember I made it for nothing.*

The cutting in the pot is not a First Cut. It is a Taken-In man who missed his green, and Thilim put the cutting in the pot and wrote that it was not a Tree.

## The First Cut

Someone cut living wood from Thaeloren, or from a branch that came away in a hand, carried it a day's earth, married it to new ground, and it took. A town on the Near Mile Turned a generation later under a canopy that was not the origin's, which is to say the town stood its own child under a local tree. Everyday speech calls it the First Cut. The liturgical name is Eoloren *(EOL-or-en)*, "the witnessed hinge." The clerical count of years starts here, so this taking is Cut-year 0 and the present year is Cut-year 387.

Everyone agrees that a first town-graft succeeded. It happened within a day's living earth of Thaeloren, close enough that a cut could still be green when it met the new ground. That much is horticulture, the keeping of living wood, and not a theology. No two houses agree whose story the cut is, and this account does not pick a winner for them.

The first green is not named. Folk who need an object to point at call it the nameless green. Naming the town would pick a hearth, and a hearth would pick a hand.

In the Walking Years the origin was not an open grove. The Closed Seat decided who might walk in to it. Distance was one way they sorted a child, and grove-leave was the other. A local Tree meant you did not need their gate. That is the monopoly the Cut broke, and it is not a sixth story about whose hand held the knife. The five stories still disagree about the hand, and they can agree about what the copy did. The Closed Seat tried to uproot the copies, and they could not. The war, the collapse, and the retreat are the Closing. Who cut stays unwritten there too. The riders fought a pot, and they never learned a face.

A freshly planted scion still takes a generation to hold a Leaf-Fall. So Cut-year 0 is a wound in wood, and the first local Turning is about twenty summers later. "A graft arrived" and "this town Turns under its own canopy" are never the same year. The wave is slow because each Hand has to grow up before it can be a source for the next cut.

### The five attributions

The five stories are not speeches someone stands up and recites. They are jobs a room is already doing, and which job the room is doing is a faster read than any creed. Who cut stays unpicked. All five stay in use.

#### Folk: the stolen branch

Folk tell the cut as a theft that was also a mercy. A parent who would not watch another child stay UnTurned for want of a trip the family could not make, or who would not bury another child on the Near Mile, took wood without leave.

The object people keep is the nameless knife, and it is not a saint. Some hearths hang a kitchen-knife on a ribbon, the way children in the Years of Hands climb the mile-shrines, and they cannot say whether they are honouring a theft or a gift. There is no name on the handle.

Thief-songs on the Near Mile are walking-songs, and the singers say "we" for the cutter. A singer from the Salt Walk turns it into a joke on the quay and calls it the first unpaid berth. Versions from the Long Mile are not funny. Vaethorn's later guilt wants this parent to have been unworthy. The songs do not agree.

Folk-right cutting did not stop when the Seat wrote a leave. A Taken-In with a sick neighbour and no year to spare will still take a slip, a small cutting. Most of those slips die. The ones that take are how the queue learns that it is not the only horticulture in the world. Road-hands call a stolen slip a problem. Towns that lived through a stolen year call it a year they Turned.

In this telling, the later Cutting-leave fee is a fine that forgot it was a fine. Lestrand will broker that fee without blushing, which is this same story with a clerk. What the telling leaves out is that a stolen Hand still left a town behind that did not get the next one. The songs leave that town and go on.

#### Clergy: the warden ahead of the college

In the clergy's telling, a tree-warden of a Near-Mile hearth cut with a leave that did not yet exist. The wood took. The hearth at the origin spent the generation of argument, from Cut-year 0 into the first local Leaf-Fall, trying to decide whether the taking was blight, theft, or rite. The First Seat as a desk that issues Cutting-leave is what that argument left sitting on the ground. The Road-hands are the scandal moved into an office. They are the people who walk what used to be done without sanction.

Speakers who will not say "first" are part of how the office lives. A Speaker will say "the witnessed hinge." They will date a note. They will not point at a grave. Some of them are skeptics who will grant the horticulture and refuse the saint. Some of them are devout, and they think that naming a cutter would steal the offering from the Tree. It is the same silence, doing two jobs.

After the first copy, the Motherwood tried to forbid any further cut. Wood died in pots anyway, and neighbours cut anyway. Forbidding a copy that had already taken is how a college learns that it does not own the Tree's living wood. It owns only the authorization, the right to say which next cut is allowed.

The Road-hands were not designed as a church that travels. They are the generation of argument, given a cart. What this telling leaves out is that "we will write how, not who" is how a heresy becomes a monopoly. The college writes the how.

#### Devout: the Tree offered

In the devout telling, a branch came away in a hand. There was no theft in it, and no genius, only a Hand giving a Hand. The Mother, or the Tree if you will not say Mother, meant the wood to travel. This is the version the Seat prefers when it preaches, because it makes the scion-queue sound like stewardship rather than like a ration.

At Turning-Week in the Motherwood, a Speaker says she sent her hands outward so the hearths could stay. On a good day the graves of the Walking Years are named in the same breath. On a bad day they are not.

The watering-words, the words said when the wood is married to new ground, are offered as proof. The devout ask why the wood would listen if that rite were only horticulture. A skeptic's answer is that you watered it. Both of those people can stand under the same canopy.

In this telling, Cutting-leave is a blessing. Vaethorn calls it the wood's blessing, and the devout need that name. Without it the queue is only a list of who waits. What the telling leaves out is that "she meant the wood to travel" narrates a wave that went to paying coasts before it went to the poor far. The thing saved, when anything was, was a walk, and it was not everyone's walk.

#### The Watching: horticulture, then a later name

The Watching say people had always been able to cut living wood. They grafted fruit, kept kin in orchards, and set a slip in a pot. The Motherfaith later named one of those cuts the First and started a count from it, a later name laid on a thing people were already doing. The Tree was already seeing. A date does not make a mother.

They refuse the Cut-count. A Watcher clerk will date a debt from a house-year, or from a drowning-year, or from a night they kept. They will convert the date into the Eoloren-count if a court makes them, and they will not call that count history.

Watcher hearths on Maiethorn still graft ordinary pears in the same week a Road-hand passes with carrying-earth, the living earth a cutting has to travel in. They do not think the pear is a joke. They think the pear is the whole argument, done with hands.

They withhold the word "first" in the same cadence as a blessing they withhold. What they will say is that what was cut was seen, and what took was seen, and that the Tree does not reach twice by chance. There is no Mother in that sentence, and no epoch. What the telling leaves out is that refusing the date does not refuse the wave, because children still died on the road until the copies took. The later name is only a name. The graves were already in the ground.

#### The Old Ways: the land received a guest

In the Old Ways telling, nobody "cut" in the sense the Seat means. A grove took kin in. The Hands, the years of local trees, are when guests began arriving from the east, and host-rights start there, not in a warrant from a college.

The first meal for a new graft is food left at the planting, the way you feed a guest before you ask their business. The Door-Keepers, who keep that meal, still do this. A Road-hand who eats is doing the courtesy of a host, whether or not the Seat wrote the meal down. A Road-hand who plants and leaves without sitting is making a claim.

A Guest-reading town and a First-reading district start the story in the same place and then split. A Guest-reading town will make the pilgrimage and keep the meal. A First-reading district will tend the same wood as a guest in their house, and will still refuse the Tender's blessing, the line a warden says over a child at the fall. Both of them start at a receiving, not at a knife. The Cut is a mainland word for what they call an arrival.

They do not start history at Cut-year 0. On Heskoren the guests arrive in the Cut-year 200s. Calling that arrival "late" is already the Motherfaith talking. Calling it "the First Cut, delayed" is a map drawn by the Seat. The land was already making children at the old doors.

What the telling leaves out is that a guest who takes a generation to speak, which is what the first local Leaf-Fall is, will still be called a Tree by the children who Turn under it. Host-rights do not stop a town from becoming a town of the Hands years.

People tell all five. Skeptics will grant the horticulture, because you can see it, and will deny that a mind chose the travelling. The Fair Hand is not a sixth story about the cut. They come into it when the wood boards a ship. The Open Table is not in this story.

### The generation of argument

Cut-year 0 is a cut. The next twenty summers are copies, dead wood, a college that cannot un-cut what has taken, and a first local Leaf-Fall that ends the argument without settling it.

What people actually did was smaller than the sermons, and more stubborn. Near-Mile towns cut because the first green was visible from the next square. They had living tissue, a day's earth, a pot, and a Taken-In who knew soil. That is why the towns at the origin filled first. It was the distance a sapling can survive, not a later sermon about worthiness.

Most of the early copies died. The wood was mortal, or the season was wrong, or a pot dried on the Near Mile in Hale-month, or a well-meant slip went into the ground with no carrying-earth under it. Those deaths are why the Seat could tell itself, for a while, that the first taking had been a fluke.

The hearth at the origin tried to forbid the next cut. Speakers named it theft, blight, a wound in the First Hand, and neighbours cut anyway. A prohibition that has no riders cannot police a day's walk in a world without a printing press, where the order dies with the person sent to carry it.

The proof arrived as a ceremony. When the nameless green held its first Turning-Week, a colour fell, or a hug fell. The tellings do not agree which, and the disagreement does not change the paper. A town that was not the origin had Turned its own child. Belonging because the town itself had seen the child came into that square the way weather does, a generation after the knife, without anyone passing a law to create it. The walk stopped being the only door. The Seat can argue doctrine until the leaves fall. It cannot argue the leaves back onto Thaeloren.

What the Seat captured was not the living wood, which the college could not own. It could own who may cut next, and that ownership is the copy-right. Cutting-leave is the paper. The scion-queue is what a copy-right becomes when the demand runs ahead of a generation of wood.

The fee is framed as the cost of carrying-earth and of a Road-hand's year, and it is the same coin heard four ways. Folk hear a fine in it, Lestrand hears a licence, Threnmaieth wants it written on a roll, and Vaethorn calls it an offering. Historically it is how an institution absorbed a heresy and called the heresy horticulture. Cutting that had no sanction became cutting that had a permit. The practice survived by changing owners, and the queue is old.

Road-hands are the people you send when you have claimed the knife and you still need the wood to walk, and they are not a second order of their own.

Once a town had its own tree, the new injustice was which town is next, and that injustice is paid in twenty more years of borrowed Turnings, twenty years in which children still stand under someone else's tree while theirs grows up.

### How the wave moved

This is how the wood moved, inside the dates already given.

A scion still takes a generation before it can Turn a child. A healthy town Tree may supply the next cut, acting for the origin. So the wave is a chain of Hands that have to grow up, not a crate of miracles you could open on any shore in the same summer. The sea can jump a link in that chain. A cart on the Long Mile cannot, not until carrying-earth will last the trip, and not until there are rest-hearths whose job is the wood rather than the pilgrims.

#### Maiethorn, Cut-years 0–80 (origin-towns first; Rain-Shadow slower)

The Near Mile stops being a requirement in the core, town by town, as the canopies come of age. The squares at the origin go first, a day's earth, then two, then a valley. The pilgrimage does not disappear. It remains as devotion at Thaeloren, and as necessity wherever a graft has not yet spoken.

The Rain-Shadow is slower, and the slowness is in the ground. It is the same continent, with thin soil, wells, and sand-country. A cut that takes in the Sacred Core, where the soil will hold it, dies in the dry hills unless a Taken-In and a lucky year agree. This is dirt, not unworthiness and not a different gospel. Hearths of the Fox of the Sands, the fox-born of that dry country, still walk west toward Thaeloren after the squares on the coast have already stopped. The wave runs at two speeds on one continent.

The Seat's narration of those years is that her hands filled the Motherland. Folk memory says they stopped burying children on the Near Mile where the wood took, and they did not stop where it did not. Both sentences can be said in the same market.

By Cut-year 80 the Motherland is old in the Years of Hands in the core, towns whose trees have been ordinary for a long time, and still waiting in pockets. That waiting is not Heskoren's story yet. It is why a hamlet on Maiethorn can still send this year's ten-year-olds to a neighbour, and not be play-acting a history that has closed.

#### Strandoren, Cut-years 40–160, by sea (wealthy coasts first, interior later)

The Old Crossing already knew how to move a child. Moving a Hand is the same berth with worse instructions on it: living earth, do not stow the pot below, water it at dawn, and a Taken-In or a Road-hand sleeps beside it.

The sea starts while Maiethorn is still filling, in Cut-year 40, because a ship is faster than filling every inland hamlet. A wealthy coast can pay a Cutting-leave and a Salt-Walk crew in the same summer that a Near-Mile village is still sitting on the Seat's list. The wave went where the ships and the fees went.

The coasts take first. Port-cities take, and the deep interior waits on river-days and a cart, which is a short version of the Long Mile. Strandoren's later combination of a wide reach and a worn faith is not a paradox. The wood arrived as cargo, and cargo does not require a mother. The Fair Hand dates the carrying as a fact of a supply line. They will attend a planting as witnesses to a signing. They will not start history at a wound in wood. "The first carrying" is their name for Eoloren, and what they mean by it is a bill of lading, the paper that says what the ship had aboard.

Once a coast Turns under its own canopy, the berth is no longer a necessity of a child's tenth year. It becomes a luxury, a hinge in a contract, a devout extra. Road-houses on the inland days lose a season of boots and keep the notes. What belongs here is the quay that noticed the pots had replaced the children.

By Cut-year 160 the shore-lands are mature in the Years of Hands on the water, and still patchy inland. Lestrand files the whole motion as "the carrying." They are not wrong about the ships.

#### The gap (why Heskoren waits until Cut-year 200)

The wait is not unworthiness, and three frictions are stacked under it.

Wood dies on the Long Mile. The trip is weeks to months. A day's earth is a Near-Mile problem, and a ship's hold is a Salt-Walk problem someone has already solved, if the pot is watered. A cart between hearths in thaw-mud is how scions become sticks. Until carrying-earth will last, and until there are rest-hearths whose job is the wood and not the pilgrims, Heskoren cannot be a circuit a living tree survives. Road-hands had to exist as a standing office before the far country could be a route, rather than a funeral of saplings.

The chain has to grow up. You cut from the origin, or you cut from a healthy town Tree. Maiethorn's first generation of sources comes of age in the Cut-year 20s to 40s. Strandoren's coastal Hands are not ready to be sources until later still. You cannot skip ahead to the far reach without a mature link, unless you spend origin-wood on a march that kills it. The Seat, mostly, did not. That is horticulture, and it is also a choice about who is next.

The queue prefers a next town that can pay. After the coasts, the carrying-earth is committed to interiors, to replacements, and to sick Trees, the quiet work that does not look like a wave. Heskoren is poor, far, and already accused, by a guilt that gets its theology later, of having waited because it deserved to wait. They waited because the wood was slow, the road was long, and the list was written in the Motherwood.

Vaethorn will preach that they wait because they are unworthy. The gap is the evidence that sermon uses. The chronicle records the slow wood, the long road, and the list.

#### Heskoren, Cut-years 200–387 (still receiving)

The frontier coast faces back across the sea toward Strandoren, and that facing is the lifeline new grafts come in on. The luckiest towns have had a local Turning for a few generations, a scion that arrived in the 200s and spoke in the 220s, or later than that. Whole districts still send their children down the road, to a neighbour's new canopy, or still along the Long Mile, if what the neighbour has is a stick in a pot.

This is the wave in the present tense. It is not a re-enactment of the years when walking was the only way. Some hamlets are in the Walking Years this year, for this group of children. Some are already in the Years of Hands, one valley over. Both can be true of the same road. The waiting Vaethorn lives in is the front of the wave, not a metaphor for a history that has finished.

Host-rights of the Old Ways start here as a collision people are living, not as a sermon preached on Maiethorn. A graft set in an old Taken-In green is a guest, a Cutting-leave is a paper from the mainland, and the meal comes first, or else the planting is a claim. The people on both sides of that argument love the wood.

The Seat's narration says her will is still travelling. Folk memory says the cart came late, the soil was called thin, and the three hamlets past the ford can see Harrow's green from here. On Heskoren that sentence is the decade they are in.

#### Kumbaan: never

No graft crossed the storm-wall, and Road-hands do not carry a Tree over that drowning sea. Sailors tell of pots that went into the water, and dead wood is not a Tree, and no old scion has ever been found on the Isle. The Open Table owes Eoloren nothing, and counts its time in moons, not in Cut-years.

### Seat narration vs folk memory

The Seat's account and the folk memory sit side by side, the way the Walking Years kept the romance and the graves in the same sentence.

| | Seat / devout sermon | Folk memory (and the other houses) |
|---|---|---|
| The knife | She offered a Hand. | Someone took one. |
| The paper | It is stewardship, and it is horticulture. | It is a fine that forgot what it was, or a licence, or a warrant. |
| The order of the wave | It is her will, travelling. | It is ships and fees, and then the far. |
| Heskoren's wait | It is soil, timing, and the Mother's pace. | It is the list, the dead wood, and the cart that came late. |
| What was saved | The world received her. | Children stopped dying on *some* roads. |

A Long-Lived person who can say "I walked," because they did, and a Speaker who keeps the Eoloren-count, will fight about the same summer. A Long-Lived person who planted, a Road-hand who held the work for a long tenure, or an innkeeper who watched the pots replace the children, will not always tell you which taking they think was holy, because the memory is perfect and what the sentence means is not.

As the trees spread, they moved the place where a child is expected to have been seen at ten. A town of the Years of Hands can still meet a feared Struck person, whose change did not come from being seen at ten, with a rope. A canopy of the town's own does not stop the rope.

### What a character notices

Along the Near Mile the cut is still sitting in ordinary things. A house hangs a kitchen-knife on a ribbon and will not name a saint for it, and in the same week the Watchers in the next yard are grafting pears while a Road-hand waters the carrying-earth. At a planting, the bowl from the first meal is already licked clean by the dogs, and that meal is one the Seat never wrote down. The clerk who copies the old ship's line, "one living hand, do not stow below, water at dawn," files it as cargo. Farther out, three hamlets can see a neighbour's canopy and are still waiting, having learned not to say "queue," while a child of the Rain-Shadow is still on the west road after the core has stopped walking. The Salt Walk's berths are empty in Eolthael, the season when they used to fill, except a hold that still smells of wet earth out of season. A Long-Lived person who planted will not say "I walked," because the job was the planting, and the room still goes quiet.

### A minute of the First Seat

What follows is a minute of the First Seat, attributed to the Speaker Rithnali, from the summer the nameless green held its first Turning-Week. Later clerks file it as Cut-year 19. The back of the leaf is a list of pots that died, and no one read that list aloud.

> *The wood that took within a day's earth of the First was cut without leave. We will not write who. We will write how, so the next cut does not kill what it carries.*
>
> *Carry in living earth. Marry with the watering-words. Do not cut a second without the Seat. If the scion sickens, send word; do not take another wound in the First to hide the first.*
>
> *The Tree does not reach twice by chance, and neither do we. From this summer a cut is a leave, or it is a theft we have decided not to chase. The towns that wait will wait on horticulture. The soil is thin in more places than it is not.*

Folk who keep the nameless knife think this paper is a confession. The Seat thinks it is competence. Both of them can hold the same copy.

### Consequences

The wave starts here. The Grafting, as a wave, is wood carried outward, a generation to mature, Maiethorn first, Strandoren by sea, Heskoren last and still live, and Kumbaan never.

Where the wood has taken, a town Tree becomes the ordinary way. Children Turn under the town canopy. The pilgrimage remains, with a new job: devotion, or prestige, or necessity when a graft fails.

The Seat gains a bottleneck. Cutting-leave and the scion-queue are how an institution took a heresy in and called the result horticulture.

The clerical count of years begins. Other people do not have to use it, and the count a person does use shows where they are willing to start.

Language and money were already bent by the road, and the bend eases where the trees stand. Local trees let children grow up in place, so the road's pull toward the older speech loosens. Road-houses in the core lose their pilgrim-seasons and keep the notes. What both of those changes left behind is in the Years of Hands: the two fates of the road-house, and the three jobs the walk still has.

## The Years of Hands

Where the wood has taken, a town Turning Tree is the ordinary way to Turn, a child standing under a Tree in their own town rather than walking to the one grove. People call these years the Years of Hands, and also Since the Hands. The liturgical name is Ornthael *(ORN-thael)*, "Tree-land." The Grafting is not a previous age you leave. It is a wave of living wood that is still moving.

This is the era the rest of the world lives in without stopping to name it. Where a graft has taken, the Leaf-Fall can happen in the square. Belonging can rest on this town having watched you become, and the pilgrimage to Thaeloren is an extra rather than the only door.

The clerical count opens the era at the First Cut, Cut-year 0, and the present, where those grafts have taken, is Cut-year 387. That count is not a finished map. Read how far the living wood has reached as a date, not as a chart of a completed age. The two clocks overlap, and neither one has closed.

Maiethorn is old in these years, and the grafts there are dense and mature, so the Walking is biography for the Long-Lived who still remember those roads, and a ribbon on a mile-shrine for everyone else. The exceptions are the Rain-Shadow, where the waiting went on after the core stopped, and any place where a Tree has sickened. A town that has had its own Tree for a long time can lose the use of it. That is weather, not the age ending. The Years of Hands go on around that town, and the town's children go back onto a road.

Strandoren is mature in these years, and the faith there is worn. The road-houses finished becoming banks on this coast. What is left of the walk is a luxury, a hinge in a contract, and a hold that still smells of earth when the passenger is a pot of living wood.

Heskoren is the Grafting this decade. The luckiest towns have had a local canopy for a few generations, and whole districts still send their children down the road. Vaethorn's waiting is not a metaphor for history. It is the wave's front.

Kumbaan never came onto either clock. No graft of this era reaches it, and no extra mile does either.

The pilgrimage did not vanish. It changed jobs. The First Seat still authorizes cuts, and what used to be a decision is now a queue. Deathless houses still remember the road, and what used to be a bed is now credit. Mile-shrines still stand, and what used to be a grave is now folklore, an argument, and a thing children climb. The romance of the old mile is a feeling of these years. The graves are facts of the Walking Years. They stand on the same ground.

These years are not "the modern age after the Grafting." The wave is still moving. A child on the Long Mile this summer is making the week, not commemorating it. Those same 387 summers also held a war, a split of paper, a fever, a flood, and a hush, and those years sit beside the two clocks. They do not replace them.

A Phoenix's rebirths remain the one marker of an era that even a town with its own Tree cannot file, and the people who use a rebirth as a year cannot agree which summer it marks.

### The walk's three jobs

Brenvaeth is the name for the years when a child's tenth summer meant the road. In those years the walk was how you Turned. In Ornthael it is three jobs that share roads, inns, and the one-week door, and do not share a reason.

| Job | Who still walks | What they are buying | What they are not |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Devotion — the extra mile** | People who already have a local Tree and still want Thaeloren | an origin-fall; a ribbon at the First Hand; the Motherwood in Eolthael | necessity. They could have Turned at home. |
| **Prestige — the First-Hand year** | Lestrand money, a Bound signing-watch, a guild-ticket hinge, a wedding extra | being seen at the origin when you did not need to be; a held-over winter as a product | piety, unless it is also that. The Fair Hand will attend as witnesses to a signing. |
| **Necessity — the neighbour's week** (still the old mile when the neighbour is a continent) | Tree-poor, Tree-sick, Tree-young; a missed fall this year; Heskoren this cohort; the Rain-Shadow after the core stopped | a Given-door that is not at home | folklore. They are not re-enacting the old road. They are making the week. |

People did not say Ornthael on the road. They said they were going to the Thallow, the worn everyday name for Thaeloren, or that they Turn at Harrow's, or that the berth is for the child. Vaethorn calls almost any walk the waiting-road. Lestrand files the extra as the First-Hand year and the need as the borrowed week. Threnmaieth wants the miles on a roll.

They share the Near Mile in Hale-month, the month when the road still fills. A devotion-column, a prestige household with too much luggage, a necessity-family who sold a year to make the trip, and a Road-hand going the other way with a pot of wood can sleep in the same upper room. The innkeeper can tell who is which. They will not always tell you. The Far-Voiced ridge-cry still works. Someone calls that the ford is wrong, and some of the people who answer are pilgrims, while some are carts of wood.

The inversion is lived on the road, not only in the names. Thaelvaeth is wood carried out, the Tree going to the hearths. Brenvaeth was hearths walking in, people going to the Tree. In the Years of Hands both directions are traffic. A Road-hand and a tenth-year pass on a bank and do not owe each other a sermon. That passing is the two clocks in one afternoon.

A town Tree that sickens, or that fails a fall, puts a Hands-town back on the neighbour's week. A sick Tree is not the age ending, even in the core. The Seat will call it thin soil, and the town will call it this year.

The one-week door did not loosen. Miss Eolthael and the Given-door has shut, whether you missed it at home or at the origin. Struck remains the later mercy, and there is still no second-chance year. The held-over nine-year-old is still the expensive mercy, and on the prestige-walk it is also a winter you can buy.

No extra mile crosses the storm-wall to Kumbaan.

### What the Walk left behind

What the Walking built, the Hands still sleep in, and still use. The reuse is a thing finding a new job, not a field of ruins. A Hands-town that treats a mile-shrine as only a ruin is telling itself the road is over.

#### Mile-shrines

A mile-shrine is still a stone at about a day's walk, with water, a flat top, and often a name and a ribbon. In a Hands-town the last one is frequently in the square, because the town grew around a graft planted beside the road, and the road's destination moved closer until the stone was in the middle of the life that replaced the walk.

The town does not agree which thing the stone is allowed to be. It is a grave, and a Long-Lived person who says "I walked" can still tell you which ford the name belongs to. It is a waymark, because necessity-walkers still drink there and Road-hands still aim for the water. It is folklore, because devotion leaves a ribbon and the town's children climb it. And it is an argument: a warden wants it moved because it is in the way of the Leaf-Fall crowd, a house that still dates its notes from a founding-summer wants it left, and a neighbour who still walks wants it left for the water and the mile, not for the memory.

The stone does not know which story it is in. A ribbon is still not a leaf-colour. Travellers who have walked once know this. Travellers who have only heard the romance do not.

Hands-towns that pull a stone down are doing theology with a hammer. They are not wrong that the road no longer has to happen there. They are wrong if they think the graves went with the requirement. The next necessity-column will miss the water.

#### The upper room

An old inn's upper room has too many beds for the present trade. In Brenvaeth those beds were the two crowded months. In Ornthael the same room is put to three uses, sometimes in the same fortnight.

Merchants take the beds, which is the White Note's conversion, occupancy treated as proof the house is competent. Eolthael still opens some of them, because devotion and prestige swell a Near-Mile house for a fortnight. And some stay held. A family said they would come, and they did not, so the innkeeper keeps the bed a year, then two, then as a habit that looks like hospitality and functions as a shrine and as proof the house remembers. Thilim still makes Seine's. Houses that became banks mostly stopped making those beds, except the one they will not let, which is how a clerk writes "house custom" on a slate and means a grave.

The romance of the empty bed belongs to these years. The name written for the held bed belongs to the Walking Years.

#### Roads that end at a Tree

The pilgrim-road was often the main road. A graft took beside it, the town grew around the canopy, and the road now ends at the Tree. The last holy mile is a square. Beyond the boughs the old way is a cart-track to timber, a neighbour, or a ford. Children are told the road goes to the Motherwood. It goes to the mill. Both can be true if you keep walking, which most children in a Tree-town do not.

For that town, the road ends at the Tree, and the children Turn at home. The family from the next valley is still walking those stones, because the wood has not reached them. The stones are the same stones.

#### Other things the Walk left that a character notices

What a character can still see, without anyone announcing an era, is the walk's leftovers still doing a job or refusing to stop.

In Eolthael the Salt Walk's berths sit empty except the expensive ones and the holds that smell of wet earth, because a pot of wood is the passenger now and the earth comes with it. The old ridge-cry still goes up, "the ford is wrong," and no column answers, though a Road-hand does, and so do the birds. A pair of siblings will not walk the same side of a road, one Given and one UnTurned, folk still calling the second Kept, because they are a split household's grandchildren keeping a courtesy they cannot date. Markets still swell at an old mile-mark in Hale-month and vanish again even when no column comes, the habit having outlasted the road. In a Near-Mile house the nameless knife still hangs on a ribbon, not yet a museum piece, because the house will not name a saint for it.

### Deathless houses' road-past as present credit

Many deathless houses began as road-houses. That change of fortune is already in the past. The inns became banks, the same Long-Lived are still in the house, and the fortune is a different one.

The founding-summer is the credit. "We kept you on the mile" is an origin-story that still collects. A century-loan written from a house-year, counted from the summer the house was founded rather than from the First Cut, is not only a quirk of dating. It is a claim that the porridge was real, the note was real, and the house is still the same house. No mortal court can audit a Long-Lived person's memory of a walk two hundred summers back. That is why the trusted note holds. It is also why a house can call a debt whose grandchildren do not remember the ford.

Two fates of that same origin are still standing, and they do not agree about what success was.

The White Note House is the reformation. The beds are let to merchants and the paper is on the quay, so Ledan's query from the house's 200th summer, written in Cut-year 280, is already a clerk's problem in a house now in its 307th summer. The held names are in the founding-book, most of those beds are let, and one is not, which is how a bank proves it used to be an inn.

The Third Hearth is the house that stayed a hearth. Thilim still keeps it, still makes Seine's bed, and still knows which guests will be here at dark. The notes Thilim keeps are smaller than a bank's, and the shrine of the bed is larger. Lestrand calls this a bed-house that failed to become a bank. Thilim does not call it a failure.

The long-house of books is still the deathless paying their own Tithe. The road-past is why anyone trusted them to endow a library in the first place. A house that remembered every debtor on the mile is a house you let steer the shelves.

The prestige-walk is now a product some houses sell. A held-over winter at the origin, once the frightened mercy of a far family who could not miss the week, is a First-Hand year you can book. It is one commodity, and the desks do not all sell the same piece of it. Netstrand supplies the berths, hulls and crews and space on the West Water, the wider sea between Strandoren and Heskoren, chartered in to the Old Crossing. What the walk left Netstrand is that far crossing, not this winter at the grove. The White Note writes the terms, which is credit for a year you do not need. They noticed the upper room was empty in Nethael, the Deep, when the deathless houses close their books, and full of the wrong people in Eolthael, and they made a term for it, without having invented the hold. Orentel holds the departure: Sorim, the factor on the first quay, files who occupies that water, empty Eolthael berths included, in the city that stages people toward Maiethorn. Orenbren houses the year at the origin, beds within a week's walk of the grove, and it is not a fourth seller of the berth.

Necessity-families still sign the old kind of note: eat now, pay on the home-walk, or when the child is grown, or when a sibling walks next year. It is the same paper doing two jobs. A clerk who files both the necessity-note and the prestige-note as occupancy is counting beds, not jobs.

Vaethorn will preach that the houses abandoned the waiting-road, and the history is shorter than that sermon. The walk stopped being required in the core, the memory did not stop, and the paper outlived the season. That is a change of business, not a betrayal of the road, and it still collects.

### Heskoren as the live front

This is the wave in the present tense. Some hamlets are in the Walking Years this year, for this group of children, still sending them the long way because no nearer Tree can take them. Some are in the Years of Hands one valley over, with a canopy of their own. Both can be true on the same road.

Harrow's Green is the luckiest kind of Heskoren town. A scion arrived in the Cut-year 200s, spoke a generation later, and has now Turned local children for a few generations. It is a neighbour's canopy you can see from the ford. New towns say, "We Turned at Harrow's Tree before we had our own." That is how they talk everywhere. On Heskoren it is the decade, not a proverb about the past.

The Three Hamlets Past the Ford are the waiting kind. The Seat's Cutting-leave already named them as thin soil. They send this year's ten-year-olds to Harrow's, or further if Harrow's year is too full, or still the Long Mile if the neighbour is only a stick in a pot. They have learned not to say "queue." They can see the canopy from where they stand. Cutting by folk-right, with a kitchen-knife and without that paper, is a temptation. Most slips die. The ones that take are how the queue learns it is not the only horticulture in the world.

Old Ways host-rights start here as a collision, not a sermon. A graft set into an old Taken-In green is a guest in that green. A Cutting-leave is a mainland paper. The first meal comes first, or the planting is a claiming. The Door-Keepers and the Road-hands can both love the wood and still fight about the bowl.

Borrowed Turnings are the front's ordinary year. Belonging to the town that saw you Turn is the practice of these years, and the town that saw you may be Harrow's rather than the hamlet that raised you. Vouching at a distance was invented to carry an origin-fall home, someone who had been under Thaeloren standing for a child in a town that had not seen it. It now carries a neighbour-fall home instead, the same three pillars, over a shorter road. The Struck who were never vouched for are still older than the grafts. A local canopy did not dissolve them.

The Seat's narration is that her will is still travelling. Folk memory is that the cart came late, the soil was called thin, and Harrow's green is visible from here. Vaethorn will preach that they wait because they are unworthy. They wait because the wood is slow, the road is long, and the list of who is next was written in the Motherwood.

The Rain-Shadow on Maiethorn is the same wait on an older continent: waiting that went on after the core stopped. Ornsael is a town where this is happening. Its Tree speaks most years, even while the well is dropping and the west road is water. A child on that west road, after the Sacred Core has stopped walking, is the same waiting on the Motherland. That is how you can tell these years are not a finished age.

### What is strained

This is not a chronicle of collapse. What a traveller can already see is pressure, and it is not an ending.

The First Seat is old in the queue. It has authorized cuts since Cut-year 19, about three hundred and sixty-eight summers, and towns that can see a neighbour's canopy have learned the horticulture-sentence by heart. Folk-right slips still happen, and Threnmaieth still wants the college on the roll, so the Seat is heavy without being near a fall.

The Road-hands are thin on the Heskoren front. They carry living earth and mortal wood on a skeleton circuit, and their authority expires a week out from the Motherwood. A live Tree is a quiet success no one writes down, while a dead one becomes a name on a complaint. They plant what they were given leave to plant, and they sleep in the town that was not chosen.

Waiting towns hang on a single scion, or on a neighbour's week, and so does the Mill-hold, where a Tree has sickened. That is a civic crisis wearing a child's summer.

Deathless houses are richer in paper than in beds. The road-past is the credit, and the upper room is how you can see it. The prestige-walk is a product they already sell.

The towns where this present is already something you can walk into are Harrow's Green, the neighbour's canopy a waiting hamlet can see; the Three Hamlets Past the Ford, the queue made visible from the ford; the Third Hearth, a road-house that stayed a hearth; Ornsael, the same waiting on the Motherland; and the White Note House, a road-house that became a desk.

## Folklore of the road, the Cut, and what the Walk left behind

Three things people still tell or play. One is a counting song of the road, and the numbers will not say which line is a mile and which line is a person. One is the teaching-story of the Cut, and its last line will not choose a hand. One is a square-game that adults later mistake for a fable, and it has no official ending.

### The Child Who Counted Stones

People sing this. They do not recite it, and no two miles of the road keep the same gaps. The learned title is *The Child Who Counted Stones*. The count carries distance and graves in the same breath, and it does not stop to tell you which line is a mile and which line is a person.

The leader sings a number, and the line of walkers answers with whatever that number bought.

> **One** for water.
> **Two** for shade.
> **Three** for the woman who knew the ford.
> **Four** for bread.
> **Six** for the boy whose ribbon stayed.

The first time they hear it, children object that five is missing, and the line answers, "Keep your breath."

The song goes on. A number may buy a dry bed, or a ferryman's lie, or a shoe that had to be stitched twice, or a name that nobody at the next stone knows. Some of the verses are useful for getting along the road, some of them are the names of people, and most of the verses never tell you which of those they are.

When the line reaches a mile-shrine, the leader skips the number that is cut into the stone. At an unmarked grave, the leader sings one number twice. If somebody challenges that, the leader says, "Keep your breath," and keeps walking.

The origin has no number in the song, and neither does the Leaf-Fall. The line arrives, stands, and begins the song again on the road home.

> **One** for water.
> **Two** for shade.
> **Four** for bread.
> **Five** for the ribbon we left on the way.

Children object that five was the number missing before, and this time the line answers, "Then carry it."

A practical leader sings the verses about water and shelter in order to pace the day, so the song is also a way of knowing how far the line has come. A bereaved household puts a name into the song once, and if that verse is still being sung after three walks, the name has become part of the geography. Crews on a quay count the tides instead of the stones, and they leave long silences in the places where an inland line would be waiting for a number. Children in a town that already Turns its own use the song for skipping games. People who are still walking dislike hearing it played that way, until they notice which numbers the children have left out.

### The Branch That Came Away

This is the teaching-story of the Cut, the first time living wood was taken from a Tree so another town could plant a Tree of its own. People still tell it in a town that has a Tree when the neighbour does not. In the telling, a Hand is that planted wood, and sharing a Hand requires wounding a Hand, because the branch has to be cut from a tree that already stands. The first copy invented both the local mercy and the wait: wood that has taken where people can stand under it, and the question of who is allowed the next branch. The bones stay the same from town to town. The last line will not choose whose hand.

People tell it like this.

A branch came away from the Tree.

A person carried it. They watered it. They put it in new ground.

It died.

They carried another. Or they carried the same one, and the tellings do not agree, and it does not change the ground.

It took.

The origin said, "That wood is ours."

The new ground said, "That wood is here."

Both were right, which is why there had to be a paper.

The paper said who may take the next branch. The Tree was not asked. The Tree kept choosing children under both canopies.

A child asked which Tree was the real one.

The mother said, "Count the Hands."

The child counted two, then twenty, then the towns that were not in the count yet.

The child asked who was next.

The mother said, "The soil is thin there."

The child could see the neighbour's canopy from the ford, and could not say, later, whether they had been counting Hands or counting waits.

A Long-Lived person who planted the wood will tell you the child's name, and will not tell you which summer, and still will not write who cut.

The bones above are common. What a teller adds last is the stance they are willing to be heard taking.

- A Near-Mile singer says, "The mother was the one who cut. The paper came after, like a fine."
- A Speaker says, "The warden cut with a leave the college had not invented yet. The paper is how we kept the next one alive."
- A devout teller says, "The branch came away. No one stole a Hand. She gave one."
- A Watcher says, "We have always cut living wood. They named one cut the First. The count is optional."
- An Old Ways teller says, "The ground received a guest. The paper is a mainland word for a meal they did not stay for."
- A Fair Hand clerk says, "The second carrying was a berth. The first was an unlicensed one. That is the whole hymn."
- A child in a Tree-town says, "We Turn here. The neighbour walks. The story is why."
- A child who is still walking says, "We can see the canopy. The story does not plant anything."

Skeptics of every house tell a short ending. One of them says, "There was not a Hand that learned to walk. There was wood, and a pot, and a college that got to the knife late."

On Maiethorn the story is told at Turning-Week, both in the Motherwood and in kitchens that keep a knife on a ribbon. The Motherwood version starts at "came away," and the kitchen version starts at "took." On Strandoren the quay-version never finds a Tree until the hold is opened. The joke is the bill, and the bill is not a joke inland. On Heskoren some tellers omit the taking, and the child is still asking who is next when the story stops. Tellers who read the story as a guest-meal start at the meal. Tellers who read it as a first claiming start at the claiming. A Seat telling ends on the paper rather than at the ford, and the Speakers who give that telling think it is the real version. Kumbaan does not keep this story. A mainland guest who tells it is offered more supper, which is an answer.

### The Child Who Climbed the Stone

The learned title is *The Child Who Climbed the Stone*. Children call the game Here and Far. It has no fixed narrator, no doctrinal endings, and no Long-Lived witness who can swear they saw the first afternoon. A town can play it only if an old mile-stone still stands where a local Tree later made a square.

One child stands on the mile-stone and is Here, and everyone else waits at the edge of the square and is Far. Far calls, "How many days?" and Here must answer, "None. The Tree is here."

Far then takes one paving-stone forward and names something that is still being carried from the road. The things they are allowed to name are the old cargo: a bed kept empty, a cup set before dawn, a ribbon gone white, a child sent to the neighbour. Here may answer only, "Already here." If Here laughs, or repeats a thing that has already been named, or steps down off the stone, the nearest child takes the stone.

The game is meant to end when Far reaches the marker. It rarely gets that far, because the list of what people are still carrying grows faster than the square is wide. Children borrow grievances from the adults, invent cargo that nobody could have brought, and argue over whether a grave and a name are one thing or two. Shopkeepers walk through the line, carts break it up, and the child on the stone keeps shouting "Already here" at people who are plainly on their way somewhere else.

There is one rule the adults never teach, and every square already knows it. When a real walking household, people who are still carrying their lives on the road, crosses the game, play stops. Here gets down, the children move the marker aside so the packs can pass, and afterward they put the marker back, sometimes into the same socket it came out of.

A Road-hand, passing along the road, calls it courtesy when the children move the marker, and a paving clerk, who keeps the stones of the square, calls it vandalism when the children put the marker back. In a town with no waiting neighbours, no one still owed a journey to a Tree, the children run out of true things and start naming the weather. Past the ford, where the game has no stone at its centre, Here stands wherever the canopy can be seen, and nobody agrees whether that placement is mockery. Adults only tell the game as a story once they have forgotten how to lose it.

## Pages a party can find

Six short pages sit on shelves a party can visit. Each was written by someone inside the world, in that person's own words, and each is a page or two long. A page can be wrong about the world. What follows says where each page is kept, who works there, what that person says if a visitor asks, and what a reader is meant to understand from it. The pages themselves stay in the writers' voices. This chapter says what each page is, and it does not take the writer's voice for its own.

Three of the pages are in Rothallo: at the school beside the Book's house, in the Book's house itself, and at the gate clerk's desk. The Book's house is where the Closed houses keep their records, and the school beside it is where their children learn to read. The other three pages are on Molevad's long-house shelf in Lestrand, where the Pourers keep their library. People name that building by the house, and it has no other name.

### The school primer

The page is *First Lessons at the Close School*. It is kept at the school beside the Book's house in Rothallo, where the children of the Closed houses learn their letters, and where other families inside the walls can send a child if they pay. Volnai teaches there. If a visitor asks about the page, Volnai says, "This is what the children learn in their first year. A hug is rare. Take a copy if you like; I write out more every summer."

A reader is meant to understand that this is the lesson the Close teaches its children, set as questions and answers they say back. The Close is the name this lesson uses for the Closed houses' way of teaching, and Volnai believes every line of it. The primer teaches that most children receive a colour, that a hug is rare, and that the Tree keeps back a hugged child for something better than a colour. It also teaches the seating at a Closed table, who holds the keys, what happens to a Closed child who receives a colour, and why, on the primer's own account, households with a Condition get the heavier turns of work.

The primer is wrong about the hug. About two children in five are hugged at the Leaf-Fall, and about a quarter of all people stay Kept for their whole lives. Being hugged is common. What is rare is a family like the Closed houses, which has stayed Kept on purpose for generations. The primer gives no numbers of its own.

### A history of the Closing

The page is *The Keeping of the Grove*. It is kept in the Book's house in Rothallo, on the same shelf as the Book of Tithes, and Delamem keeps the Book of Tithes. If a visitor asks, Delamem says, "It is our history of the Seat. You may read it here. The Book's house does not lend."

A reader is meant to understand that this is the Closed Heirs' own account of the Closing, and that the account speaks well of the Closed Seat. It says the gate at the grove kept order at the one Tree, that children turned away at the gate went home unharmed, and that the riders who dug up and burned the new grafts were stewards bringing back stolen wood. It says Hildal, a clerk, chose to keep the Book rather than fight for the grove, and that the Closed families moved a day's walk away into Rothallo, in the country that became Orenbren.

The facts on the page match the history above. The war ran from Cut-year 19 to Cut-year 38, and the writer counts those years from the cutting and calls them the nineteenth and the thirty-eighth year. The page does not name who made the First Cut, and it does not put a year on the Tree.

What the page leaves out is the cost. It does not say that children walked the whole Long Mile and went home UnTurned because the gate refused them, or that people died on the roads. The page calls a child sent home "Kept" and "whole," as if that were no loss.

### Minutes from the long-house

The page is *Minutes of a Gift*. It is one meeting copied out of the minute-book, and it is kept on Molevad's long-house shelf in Lestrand. Molevad keeps the minute-book and the key to the shelf. If a visitor asks, Molevad says, "This is one meeting. K. said it could be read. The rest of the book stays shut."

A reader is meant to understand that these are the circle's own minutes of one willing gift. The circle asks five years of a member in all, one year on each night, and this meeting is the fifth that a Long-Lived member called K. gives, so K. is on the last of them. The Long-Lived are the people who can make this gift, because a year of their memory is a year they have actually kept. K. asks to give that year, names it, and says why: K. is tired of carrying a husband's death. The circle asks three times whether the gift is K.'s own wish. K. gives the year into the cup with one drop of blood. Afterward K. still knows the husband's name and face from before the marriage, and remembers nothing from that year. K.'s papers from that year go onto the shelf, and the minutes end with the long-house seating K. one year younger, five in all.

The minutes also record how the circle will describe its god this decade, that one reading held two members' memories of the same summer, and that nobody asked to stop. They end on the circle's claim that every gift is freely given and that the circle made the god it gives to. That claim is the circle's belief. These minutes do not settle whether it is true.

### A scene from a play

The page is *The Heir's Seat*. It is kept on Molevad's long-house shelf in Lestrand as a copy in the common speech. Members of the circle copy books into the common speech, and this play is one of those copies. Molevad holds the key. If a visitor asks, Molevad says, "People borrow it to put on in a yard at Turning-Week. Bring it back when you're done." The week Molevad means is when a town Turns its children.

A reader is meant to understand that this is a scene players can perform straight from the page, with a table, a tall chair, and a lantern, and that no theatre is needed. Two cousins of a Closed house were Turned in the same week. One was hugged, and the other received a storm-grey leaf, so that cousin is the one the Tree gave a colour, which is a Condition. A clerk of the Book strikes the Given cousin from the heir-roll and writes the name again on the page of turns of work. The Given cousin is sent to the far end of the table, and the hugged cousin is given the heir's chair and a gate key.

The scene does not say which cousin got the better life. The hugged cousin keeps the seat, and has to stay inside the walls and hold the keys. The Given cousin loses the seat, owes heavier work, and can walk out of the gate any morning. The play ends on a question nobody in it answers.

### A poem

The page is *A Room I Do Not Go Into*. It is kept on Molevad's long-house shelf in Lestrand. The copy has no name on it, and a Long-Lived writer wrote it. Molevad keeps the shelf. If a visitor asks, Molevad says, "One of us wrote it. Read it."

A reader is meant to understand that the poem is about a person who has five summers they no longer remember, and a room they walk past and do not open. People who read it often ask whether it is about the cup, where the Pourers give years of memory away, one year at a time. If anyone asks Molevad, or any other Long-Lived person at the shelf, whether the poem is about the cup, that person will not say whether it is.

### A road song

The page is *The Day-Road Count*. It is kept at the gate clerk's desk at Rothallo's gate, written on the back of an old sleep-line sheet, the list of who slept in the beds outside the wall. Breolnir, the gate clerk, keeps that desk and writes the list. If a visitor asks, Breolnir says, "The school children sing it on the road to the wood. Pilgrims keep asking for the words, so I keep a copy."

A reader is meant to understand that this is a counting song the school beside the Book's house teaches children to sing as they walk the day's road from Rothallo to the Awakening Tree. It counts the Kept the way the Close teaches. At every town Tree on the road, and even under the Awakening Tree, the song has every child receive a colour, and nobody is hugged. Only at the end, back at Rothallo's wall, does the song find a hug.

The song is wrong, because a tree-warden sees children hugged at every Leaf-Fall, in every town, and under the Awakening Tree itself, and a warden who hears children singing it on the road can tell. The song gives no numbers of its own.
