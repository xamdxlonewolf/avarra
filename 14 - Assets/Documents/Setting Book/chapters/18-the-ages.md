# The Ages

People keep two counts of the past. One is how you Turned: the walk to the origin, or standing under a town Tree. The other is where the wood has reached: the Grafting, still unfinished on Heskoren, never on Kumbaan. Which one a person glances at tells you where they stand.

**How you Turned** is personal and generational. Did you walk to Thaeloren, or to a neighbour who had already walked, or did you stand under a town **graft**? That is a cutting of living wood joined to new ground, and it grows as the same plant. A family says, "We are a Hands-house. Grandmother still talks of the road." **Hands** are the planted town Trees. That is also the era when those Trees are the ordinary way to Turn. A Long-Lived who says "I walked" is saying when they were young.

**Where the wood has reached** is geographic, and unfinished. The **Grafting** is a wave of living wood that started at the origin, filled Maiethorn, crossed to Strandoren by sea, and is still arriving on Heskoren. Kumbaan is off the wave entirely. Present-day thin reach is the front of a process that has not finished.

Both clocks sit on an unnamed preface. That is **Before the Walk**. The Tree was already there. No honest count starts it.

The Walking Years were a logistics problem that killed children on the road. The Grafting was horticulture that people later sanctified. The Seat now narrates both as her will travelling. Folk memory is less tidy. The romance of the old mile and the graves along it belong to the same stretch of road.

The liturgical pair inverts the Road-hands' own name. **Thaelvaeth** is the Tree gone to the waiting. That is wood carried *out*. **Brenvaeth** is the hearth gone to the waiting. That is people walking *in*. The Grafting is the moment the world stopped sending hearths to the Tree and started sending the Tree to hearths.

## A history in order

This section tells the history in order, from before anyone counted years to the present year. It gives years the way the First Seat and devout clerks count them, from the First Cut. That was the first time a piece cut from the Awakening Tree was planted in another town and grew. That year is Cut-year 0. The present year is Cut-year 387. Many people count another way; "How years are numbered" below says who they are. The later sections of this chapter tell each part at length. This section gives the order of events and what each one left behind.

### Before anyone counted years

The Awakening Tree already stood on Maiethorn, in the country now called the Sacred Core. It was the only Turning Tree in the world. Nobody planted it in any summer that anyone remembers.

People were already born into their Kinds. Some were already Struck into a Condition at a threshold, such as a drowning or an oath, far from the Tree, with nobody to witness it. Under the Tree, children in their tenth year were already Given a colour or hugged at the solstice. Nobody knows how long that had been going on.

The Watching call this time the Before. They teach that the Tree was already watching people before anyone called it a mother. The Old Ways on Heskoren say their people were already being Struck at the places they call doors, such as a drowning, a grove, or a stone, before any road to the Tree existed. Kumbaan is not part of this history. Its people have their own past and do not count it by the Tree.

Nobody wrote down a king, a war, or a founding from this time. Three stone places from it survive; they are described below. More is in "Before the Walk."

### The years of the walk

The time before the First Cut is called the Walking Years. It started when families began to organize the trip to the Tree, and nobody wrote down that year.

There was one Tree to Turn under, and one week. A child who had turned ten since the last High Solstice had to stand under the Awakening Tree during Turning-Week, the week around the High Solstice in Eolthael. A child who arrived after that week was not Given a Condition, that year or any later year. They could still be Struck later in life. Far families left months early. The richest families, and the most frightened, sent a nine-year-old to winter in a road-house near the Tree so the child could not miss the week.

People in Maiethorn walked the Near Mile, three to twelve days. People in Strandoren took the Salt Walk: three to five days on a ship across the Old Crossing, then the Near Mile, usually one to three weeks in all. People in Heskoren, and in the far inland parts of Strandoren, walked the Long Mile, six to twelve weeks. A family on the Long Mile gave up a year's spare food, or signed a debt at a road-house, or did not go.

Many families could not send every child. A family with three children near ten might send one. That child might come home with a Condition, while the brothers and sisters who stayed home grew up UnTurned, or were Struck later. So the far lands were full of UnTurned people, whom folk called Kept, and of people Struck later in life.

Inns along the roads gave travellers a bed and let them eat on credit, paid back on the way home or years later. Many innkeepers were Long-Lived and forgot no debt. After the walk stopped being necessary, some of those inns kept their records and became banks. The White Note House in Orentel is one. The Third Hearth is an inn that stayed an inn.

Children died on the road, and so did the adults who carried them: at river crossings in the thaw, of fever in crowded inns, in late storms on the Old Crossing, of hunger on the Long Mile. Travellers buried them where the group stopped and set a stone there. Those stones are the mile-shrines that still stand along the old roads, often with a ribbon tied on.

Reaching the Tree was not enough. A group of families with no Condition lived around the Awakening Tree and decided who was allowed to stand under it. People now call them the Closed Seat, and in ordinary talk the Grove-Sitters. A family needed a paper called grove-leave to pass their gate. Some children walked the whole Long Mile and went home UnTurned because the gate refused them. The Closed Seat ruled the country around the Tree. It never ruled the world. Strandoren's families paid for the crossing, but the Closed Seat never ruled Strandoren. Heskoren was too far away to rule. Kumbaan never came.

Some Long-Lived alive today walked one of these roads as children. They remember every day of it. They do not agree about what it meant. The road, the inns, the graves, and the gate are told at length in "The Walking Years."

### The First Cut (Cut-year 0)

In Cut-year 0, someone cut a piece of living wood from the Awakening Tree, carried it in earth to a town about a day's walk away, and planted it, and it grew. People had always grafted fruit trees. This was the first time a cutting from the Awakening Tree took root somewhere else. Nobody knows who made the cut. The town is not named either; people call that first graft the nameless green. A town Tree grows from wood cut from the Awakening Tree, so people count it as the same Tree. The devout call each town Tree a Hand of the Mother. The five stories of who cut are in "The First Cut."

### The years of argument (Cut-years 0 to 19)

A new graft cannot hold a Leaf-Fall until it has grown for about a generation. For nineteen years nobody knew whether the nameless green would ever Turn a child. Other towns on the Near Mile cut their own pieces, and most of the cuttings died. The college of tree-wardens at the Awakening Tree, the First Seat, called cutting theft and forbade it. People cut anyway.

In Cut-year 19 the nameless green held its first Turning-Week, and a child of that town was Turned under it. The stories disagree about whether the child received a colour or a hug. The First Seat stopped trying to forbid cutting and took charge of it instead. From then on a cutting needed the Seat's permission, called Cutting-leave. That permission, and the waiting list of towns that want a graft, called the scion-queue, still exist. Rithnali, a Speaker of the First Seat, wrote the record of that summer's meeting; it says the Seat will write down how a cutting is done and will not write down who did the first one.

### The Closing (Cut-years 19 to 38)

The Closed Seat's power depended on being the only gate to the only Tree. A town that could Turn its own children did not need grove-leave. From Cut-year 19 the Closed Seat sent riders out with sacks to dig up the new grafts and bring them back, or burn them in their pots.

They failed. Some grafts died. Towns hid cuttings in wells and watered them at night. The lodging-towns around the grove, which made their living housing travellers, would not stop housing them to help the Closed Seat. The First Seat wanted to control who cut, not to destroy trees, and would not help burn a graft that had already Turned a child. The Closed Seat could not guard every town within a day's walk in every direction. People call this war the Closing. Folk also call it the Uprooting.

In Cut-year 38, Hildal ordered the Retreat. Hildal was the last person to speak for the Closed Seat whose name people still use. Hildal was a clerk, not the Closed Seat's leader. The Closed families gave up the grove, moved a day's walk away into a walled city, and took their Book with them. The Book is their record of who owes what. That walled city is Rothallo, which people still call the Inner Close.

The Closed Seat's power split four ways, and nobody has put it back together:

- Nobody controls who may stand under the Awakening Tree anymore. A family needs nobody's leave to bring a child there in Turning-Week.
- The First Seat controls who may cut a graft, through Cutting-leave.
- The lodging-towns joined together and became Orenbren, which houses travellers near the Tree.
- The Closed families kept the Book, and the Book only governs people who live inside Rothallo's walls.

Rothallo is Orenbren's capital, and it lies inside Orenbren. The Closed families did not found a new country. The lodging-towns run the inns outside Rothallo's wall, and the Book runs the city inside it.

### The Closed Heirs after the Retreat (Cut-years 38 to 387)

The Closed families are now called the Closed Heirs. After the Retreat they kept the same habits inside Rothallo that the Closed Seat had kept at the grove, and they have kept them for three hundred and forty-nine years. The group that keeps the Book is called the Walled Book. This is what a visitor to Rothallo can see today.

- **Where a person sits.** At a meal in a Closed house, Kept guests sit nearest the host. A guest with a Condition is shown to the far end of the table. When the Book is read aloud, the Kept sit on the front benches and everyone else sits behind them.
- **Whose word a clerk writes down.** When a Kept person and a person with a Condition disagree in front of a clerk of the Book, the clerk writes the Kept person's account as what happened, and the other account underneath as "also said." The person with a Condition can still win, but only if a second Kept person says the same thing.
- **Who holds a key.** Only a Kept person may hold a key to a gate in the wall, to the Book's house, to a cistern, or to a granary. A person with a Condition holds the key to their own door and their own stall.
- **What the school teaches.** At the school beside the Book's house, the teacher Volnai tells the children that a hug is rare, and that a child the Tree hugged was kept back for something better than a colour.
- **Who inherits.** A Closed Heir who receives a colour at the Leaf-Fall is struck off the heir-roll. A Closed Heir who is hugged stays on it.
- **Who works.** Everyone who lives inside the walls owes the city turns of work, the way a town expects each household to take a turn on the watch. Kept households mostly keep the watch on the wall and hold the keys. Households with a Condition get the heavier turns, and more of them.

A person with a Condition in Rothallo is not a slave. They work for wages, keep what they earn, marry whom they choose, and can own a stall with a licence from the Book. They can walk out of the gate on any day it is open and live somewhere else. The Book's list of turns has no claim on anyone who lives outside the walls, and the Book cannot send for a person who has left.

The school's lesson is wrong about the count. Being hugged is common; the shares are given in "Kind and Condition." What is rare is a family like the Closed houses, which has stayed without a Condition on purpose for generations. They do it by striking every Given heir off the roll and keeping their heirs away from the oaths that can Strike a person. The clerks of the Book can read the real count. The school teaches the same lesson every year anyway.

The gate at the grove has been gone since Cut-year 38. The belief that a hugged person ranks above a person with a colour is still alive, and in a few places it reaches past Rothallo's walls. Some inns just outside Rothallo's gate keep their best table for Kept guests on nights when a Closed house dines there, and move a guest with a Condition if the Closed house asks. Brenthael, a town with its own Tree on the Near Mile, has no copy of the Book. At its Turning-Week supper, Kept people still sit on the benches nearest the Tree and people with a Condition sit on the outer benches, and a child who has just received a colour gets up and moves outward before the food is served. Nobody in Brenthael can say when this started. Other places reject it in public. The gift-hall at Eolvaeth in Vaethorn teaches that a colour and a hug are equal, and says aloud that the Close is wrong. Given heirs who were struck off the roll eat there; Hithaen is one of them.

In Cut-year 387, a Closed child named Talnin is on the heir-roll and has not yet had a Leaf-Fall. If a colour falls for Talnin, Talnin will be struck off.

### How the grafts spread

From Cut-year 0 the grafts spread outward from the Awakening Tree, town by town, as each new Tree grew old enough to Turn children and to give cuttings of its own. A town that gets a graft still waits about twenty years before its Tree holds its first Leaf-Fall. Until then its children walk to a neighbouring town's Tree. The time since town Trees became the ordinary way to Turn is called the Years of Hands.

- **Maiethorn, Cut-years 0 to about 80.** Towns nearest the Awakening Tree got grafts first. By about Cut-year 80 most of the core had its own Trees. The dry east beyond the Rain-Wall mountains, called the Rain-Shadow, was slower, because grafts die in thin, dry soil. Some families there still walk west to the Awakening Tree.
- **Strandoren, about Cut-years 40 to 160, by sea.** Ships carried grafts in pots of living earth, on deck, watered at dawn. The rich port cities got grafts first, while some Maiethorn villages were still waiting. Inland towns got them later, by river and cart.
- **The wait, about Cut-years 160 to 200.** For about forty years no graft reached Heskoren. Cuttings died on the long road, the line of mature Trees that could give cuttings took time to grow, and the First Seat's list served towns that could pay first. Heskoren did not wait because its people were unworthy, whatever some Vaethorn preachers say.
- **Heskoren, Cut-year 200 to the present.** Grafts began arriving by sea from Strandoren. The luckiest towns, such as Harrow's Green, got a graft in the 200s and have Turned their own children for a few generations. Many districts still have no Tree, and their ten-year-olds walk to a neighbouring town or walk the Long Mile. The Three Hamlets Past the Ford can see Harrow's Tree from their fields and are still on the list. Heskoren is still receiving grafts.
- **Kumbaan, never.** No graft has crossed the storm-wall. Kumbaan has no Tree, and its people count moons.

A town Tree can also sicken and stop holding a Leaf-Fall. Then the town's children go back to walking to a neighbour, even in Maiethorn. The Mill-hold is such a town. The spread is told at length in "How the wave moved," and the present in "The Years of Hands."

### Other dated years

These events happened while the grafts were spreading. They sit beside the history of the Trees. They are not another age of the world. "The Other Count" below gives them as a table.

- **Cut-year 67, the Two Papers.** The old hymn-country of the Sacred Core split in two over whether to write people down. Limrae opened a desk in a river-town and began recording every person, saying that a person you cannot find again is a person you cannot look after. Manril refused to be put on the list, and said a person belongs because people saw them Turn. There was no battle. Households chose which desk to deal with. The side that kept the list became Threnmaieth, and the side that refused became Maiethvael. Threnmaieth counts years from that summer, so Cut-year 387 is Crown-year 320 there.
- **Cut-year 171, the Grey Summer.** A fever started in the crowded holds of ships and spread through the ports on both sides of the Old Crossing. It was an ordinary disease, not a Tithe. Ships stayed in port all summer. Some grafts went unwatered because the Taken-In who tended them were dead, and a few coastal towns sent their children to a neighbour's Tree the next year. Long-Lived bankers did not die; their borrowers did, and the debts passed to the families. Afterward Trenledd began keeping a list of persons, and Dirrol added a column for who was still alive. In Cut-year 174 the small towns of Brenledd put their charters together into one league. Leddvael made the signing of a contract a public ceremony, because so many witnesses had died. For about ten years fewer ships sailed toward Heskoren.
- **Cut-year 233, the Thaw-Break.** The snow on the Rain-Wall in Lirorn melted in one week instead of over a month. Valleys flooded and people died. The old high road over the Noon Pass was washed out, and Narol of the Pass took the toll on the new, lower road, the Shelf-gate; Narol's household still collects it. The west road in Saelthael was cut for two summers, so some children went late to the Awakening Tree, went to a neighbour, or missed their week. A graft on its way to Ornled died because the cart was late to the quay. Vathne wrote on a wall that Ornled would not wait with a hymn for a stick that drowned in someone else's thaw, meaning Ornled would stop praying and waiting for grafts that other countries' troubles could take away. Ornled still copies that sentence.
- **Cut-year 248, the Wrong Green.** At Harrow's Green a warden announced copper-green for a child the Tree had hugged, because the town wanted a Taken-In. A visiting Speaker corrected it two years later. It was a person's doing, not the Tree's. Speakers still name that year when they teach wardens not to announce the colour a town wishes it had.
- **Cut-year 280, Ledan's question.** Ledan, a Long-Lived clerk of the White Note House, wrote to the First Seat to ask which year number to put on a hundred-year loan, because the bank, the borrower's town, and the Seat each counted the year differently. Cut-year 280 was the bank's 200th year. The bank still converts its years the way Ledan's letter did; Cut-year 387 is the White Note's 307th year. The letter is under "A clerk's query" below.
- **Cut-years 299 to 304, the Hinge War and the Hinge Hush.** The Hinge Shore and the Ledger Coast fought for five summers over who set the price of crossing the Old Crossing, seizing each other's ships and blocking each other's quays. Taerso spoke for the Hinge Shore's houses, and Sirtol for a group of Ledger Coast charters. In Cut-year 304 they agreed a peace that names no winner. There is one charge for the crossing, the Hush-rate, still collected. The Hinge Shore gives a blessing to anyone who wants one, and the Ledger Coast writes the paperwork. The agreement also covers the far crossing to Heskoren: Netstrand keeps the ships that sail it, Saelvaeth may receive a graft without paying a blessing-fee to Vaethorn, Vaethorn may bless a landing but may not tax it, and Ornled charges a fee for a beach landing. People call the peace the Hinge Hush.

### Three old places you can still walk into

Three stone places come from before anyone counted years. Nobody knows when they were built or by whom, and the people who look after them will not let anyone cut a year into them. A party can visit all three.

- **The Low Wall.** A low stone wall in Orenbren's lodging-country, off the Near Mile, within the first few days' walk from the Awakening Tree. It is not part of Rothallo's wall, and the stone is a different colour. It has three gaps that people use as gates: one widened for carts, one only wide enough for a single person, and one with an old post-socket. Midway along is a stone panel whose letters were chiselled off long ago. The wall ends in the back garden of an inn, where Thuda rents beds in the loft. The Closed Heirs say the wall is theirs. The Watching say it is older than any count. In Cut-year 381, Nurreith and three lodging-guild workers dug under the panel. Delamem, who keeps the Book of Tithes, wanted a chip of stone that matched Rothallo's quarry. It did not match. They filled the hole the same week.
- **The Seeing-Ring.** A ring of standing stones on a hill in Maiethorn's old Watching country. It is not the Awakening Tree's grove. The stones are set so that a person walking around the ring turns to look outward. Four wider gaps face outward: one toward the Awakening Tree, one toward the Old Crossing, and two toward nothing anyone can name. Later someone built a shrine on top of the ring, and later still someone took the roof off and left the steps. Tithsael, of the Watching, keeps a night there every week and lets visitors sit with them, unless the visitor has brought a colour-ribbon to tie on the stones or means to carve a year into them.
- **The Dry Stair.** A flight of steps cut into a rise in Saelthael's dry country, above a small well-town that is not Ornsael. The steps climb to a door-shaped stone with no wall around it and a basin cut in the rock that has never held water in living memory. A tree-warden once planted a cutting in the basin, and it died. Local children dare each other to climb the last step. Behind the basin a narrow crawl leads to a second dry basin lower in the rock.

### The memory cup

The memory cup is a late practice among the Long-Lived. It began in the Years of Hands, long after the First Cut. The circles that keep it do not write down the year it started. It is old enough that Long-Lived scholars who are not members have written commentaries on it, kept in the long-houses where the Long-Lived meet, and the Motherfaith, the Watching, and the Fair Hand have each said what they think it is. It is recent enough that a Long-Lived who walked to the Awakening Tree as a child, before their town had a Tree of its own, can say that nobody did this when they were young.

The group that keeps the cup is the Pourers, also called the Cup Circle. Every Long-Lived person is asked to join once, in person, by a member. A person who says no is not punished. They keep their standing, and the long-houses still give them a seat. Most Long-Lived people are not members. All of them know the circle exists.

A member gives one year of their memory into a cup at a time, of their own free will. Each year is its own night, with its own minutes, and the member may stop before the next. The circle asks five years of a member in all. For each year the member adds one drop of their own blood, because the Long-Lived read memory from blood. Afterward the member no longer has the memories of that year, including the deaths of the people they knew in it. They still need to keep learning, as every Long-Lived does.

Members say they give "the memories those years held, and the years themselves." The second part means two things. For each year given, the member also gives the long-house shelf all the papers they wrote or kept in that year, such as letters, account books, and diaries. And the long-houses that keep the circle stop counting that year in the member's age, so a member who has given all five years is seated as if five years younger. Among the Long-Lived, age decides who speaks first and who keeps a key, so each gift costs a member rank in those long-houses. Courts, banks, guilds, and Long-Lived who are not members keep counting the ordinary way.

Members can read the cup the way a Long-Lived reads a drop of blood. What they read is true. One reading can hold several members' memories of the same year. Members also keep books in the Long-Lived library in Lestrand, on the Ledger Coast, and copy books in the Old Tongue and in the common speech for it. People use those books. Molevad keeps the minutes of the best-known circle in the same library. A second circle meets in an upper room of a lodging-house in Orenbren and does not ask Molevad's circle for permission.

A member may leave. The years already given stay in the cup. What comes back to the member is their papers, which members call a thinner copy. The other members stop reading blood with them, and the long-houses that keep the circle stop giving them a seat.

The circle claims that by giving to the cup they made a god. They call it the Poured God, and among themselves the Cup We Made. They say every gift is freely given. The Pourers are a separate group from the Walled Book and from the Protectors.

### What nobody knows

Nobody knows who made the First Cut. Five different accounts are told. In one, a parent stole the branch so that no more children would stay UnTurned for want of the trip, or die on the road. In another, a tree-warden cut it before the First Seat allowed cutting. The devout say the Tree let a branch come away. The Watching say people had always grafted trees and the count was added later. The Old Ways say the land received a guest. This history does not pick one.

Nobody knows how old the Awakening Tree is. It was standing before anyone counted years. The Watching argue against any date anyone proposes.

Nobody has settled whether there is a Leaf-Mother, or, for those who believe in her, what limits her. Believers ask why her Trees could not Turn children far away until a graft was planted near them, and why Kumbaan has no Tree. They do not agree on the answer. Skeptics say distance and soil explain everything, and that the Tree is a plant. This history does not answer the question.

## How years are numbered

There is **no universal year-zero.** Same twelve months; three new-year's days. Same past; **seven honest ways to count it.** The number you write on a debt is a theology.

| Who | What they count from | What it sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| First Seat / devout clerks | **the First Cut**, in **Cut-years**. The liturgical name is the *Eoloren-count*. A Cut-year is numbered from that hinge. | "the 387th Cut-year" |
| Threnmaieth | Cut-years *and* a crown-count from the Two Papers (Cut-year 67; present **Crown-year 320**) | both numbers, set down for a roll |
| Lestrand / deathless houses | a **house-founding**, a note-issue, a contract-term | "the 307th summer of the White Note" (present); the old conversion is the 200th, from Ledan's query in Cut-year 280 |
| Vaethorn | **the year our graft took**, or the last *actual* Turning-Week | a child "turns the year" when the local Tree last managed a fall |
| the Watching | they **refuse the Cut-count** | the Tree was already seeing, before any Cut-count |
| the Old Ways | a closed door, a drowning-year, a guest that took | the Hands are when guests arrived; the old doors are older than that arrival |
| Kumbaan | **moons** | the mainland ages are a sailor's chart they do not owe |

**Present:** **Cut-year 387**. That is the 387th summer since the First Cut. It is not a round number.

**What no one can date.** The Awakening Tree is **old beyond reliable dating.** Before the Walk has no start. Who made the First Cut stays contested. Believers ask why her Trees reached the far lands late, and skeptics say there is no Leaf-Mother. The Low Wall, the Seeing-Ring, and the Dry Stair are stone places from before the Walk. People found them standing. They carry no builder's name.

**Cut-year 0 is a graft.** It is the first witnessed copy of living wood. It is not the first Condition, not the first Giving, and not the Tree's birthday. The Giving and the walk have no start year. Becoming at a door is older than the walk. Being born a Kind is older than both.

### The Other Count

Two clocks still organize how you Turned and where the wood has reached. Three hundred and eighty-seven summers also held a war, a split of paper, a fever, a flood, and a hush that is still the Old Crossing's peace. This count sits *beside* the clocks.

In the Walking Years a class of the untithed **sat on Thaeloren** and decided who might walk to it. People call them **the Closed Seat**. Folk say **the Grove-Sitters**. They were an *origin-gate* that grew a country around the one Tree. Strandoren paid the Salt Walk; it was never a province. Heskoren was beyond a gate's ride. Kumbaan lay beyond any road that gate could send.

They thought they were doing horticulture and mercy: the one Tree cannot stand a world at once, so someone must choose the road. That is the same hunger the First Seat later named Cutting-leave, and the same hunger Rothallo still files as rank. People still say the Inner Close. It is Orenbren's walled capital, one day's walk from the wood, inside Orenbren. The First Seat stays in the wood.

The present year is **Cut-year 387**. Who cut remains unpicked. A chronicle that writes a first king into the Before is adding a later story to a time nobody dated. The Watching warn against that.

**What a traveller notices, besides wood.** Three dates on a debt, and a *fourth* if the note survived the Grey Summer. A kitchen that still will not send a child through the Inner Close's gate. A Lirorn pass-stone with a water-line over the old ribbon-notch. A Hinge Shore quay that charges the Hush-rate and will not call it a victory. A Watching night on a ring of stones that is older than any Cut-year anyone will write.

Maiethorn is post-imperial lodging, gift, reckoning, hinge, thaw, and dry sowing — six successors of a grove-gate, not six ancient kingdoms. Strandoren is what the Salt Walk became when the gate died and the fever wrote new paper. Heskoren entered the clocks late. Host-rights were already there.

### Spread of the wave

| Cut-years | Where the wood went |
|---|---|
| **Cut-year 0** | the First Cut, near Thaeloren, Maiethorn |
| **Cut-years 0–80** | Maiethorn filling. Origin-towns first; the Rain-Shadow, the dry east beyond the Rain-Wall, slower. |
| **Cut-years 40–160** | Strandoren by sea. Wealthy coasts first, deep interior later. |

A freshly planted **scion** still takes **a generation** to hold a Leaf-Fall. That is the cutting itself, and it is not a seed. So "a graft arrived" and "this town Turns under its own canopy" are twenty years apart. Heskoren's waiting is the queue, plus the childhood of the wood.

## What the two clocks did

**Law.** Citizenship-by-local-witness is a Hands-era default. In the Walking Years the town that *saw you Turn* was the crowd under Thaeloren, or no one, if you never arrived. Far children who stayed UnTurned were unwitnessed as Given because they did not make the trip. No roll had a line for them. Vouching-at-a-distance was invented to carry the origin's witness home. A Long-Lived who remembers the origin-fall can carry it, or a Bound word, or a Far-Voiced feeling. The unvouched Struck are older than the grafts: a becoming on a far threshold was always unwitnessed. The Grafting localized who is expected to have been seen. The unease was already in the far towns.

**Money.** Many deathless houses began as **road-houses**. Those were inns on the pilgrimage roads that also issued credit, beds and century-notes for families who had to walk. When the walk stopped being required in the core, the houses still had Long-Lived memory of every debtor on the road, and paper that outlived the pilgrimage-season. They became banks. The endow-not-inherit loop is older than the Hands. The *trusted note* is a Walking-era instrument. It outlived its road.

**Clergy.** **Cutting-leave** is the First Seat's paper authorizing a cut. That is how the college captured a practice that began without it. The first successful cut was argued as theft, as mercy, as the Tree offering a branch, as old horticulture the Motherfaith later named. The Seat won the *authorization*. The **scion-queue** is the Grafting's child. That is the bottleneck that appears when her reach can travel but someone still decides *which town next.* Spreading Trees made a queue.

**Language.** The pilgrimage was a conserving pull. Once a year the far-flung walked to the same grove and heard the same liturgy, so the Old Tongue stayed nearer itself along the road. Local Trees let daughters grow in place. That is why drift tracks Tree-thinness, and why Lestrand eroded so far so fast: trade, a local canopy, and no need to walk to the Motherwood. The three daughter-drifts are Years-of-Hands change on a Walking-era root.

**Geography.** The reach-gradient *is* the wave, frozen at Cut-year 387. Maiethorn is old Hands. Strandoren is mature Hands with a commercial aftertaste. Heskoren is the Grafting this decade. Kumbaan never entered the clocks. A traveller meets that spread as distance, and as history.

**Faith.** The Watching remember Before the Walk as *the Before*, seeing without a mother's name, and will not start history at a Cut. The Old Ways were already becoming before any pilgrim road; the Hands are when *guests* arrived late. The Fair Hand dates contracts from a signing, not from a wound in wood; the Grafting is a supply-chain fact. The Open Table owes the ages nothing.

### What a traveller notices

- A Long-Lived who says "I walked," and a room that goes quiet. That is how that person Turned, spoken as a life.
- A **mile-shrine** stands on a road that now ends at a town Tree. That is a stone at about a day's walk, set for a Turning that no longer has to happen there. The devout still leave a ribbon. The town children climb it.
- **Empty pilgrim-beds** in an old inn's upper room, let now to merchants. The deathless house that owns the inn still dates its notes from the founding-summer the beds were full.
- Three dates for one summer, written on one debt: a Cut-year, a house-year, a "year the graft took."
- A Heskoren hamlet whose ten-year-olds still walk to a neighbour. That walk is this year.
- Someone names a Phoenix "the Phoenix of the last Walking summer," and no one in the room agrees which summer that was.

### A clerk's query (Cut-year 280)

*Ledan of the White Note, to the Speaker who keeps the Eoloren-count, in the house's 200th summer. It is filed in the founding-book, and the desk still reaches for its conversion. The verso has been folded around a second number.*

> *The house writes a century-loan from our founding-summer, which we call the 200th of the White Note. The debtor in Wetorn writes the same summer as the year their graft took. Your college will write it as the 280th Cut-year if I have the conversion right, and as the 279th if I have it wrong.*

## Before the Walk

The unnamed preface. The Awakening Tree already stood. People already had Kinds. Some already *became* things at doors the later faith would struggle to place. People tell little of it. There is no date. The Tree is old beyond reliable dating. No creation myth, no first king. The Watching keep **the Before**.

Most of the world does not have a name for this. The devout skip it. Skeptics say, "Before anyone bothered to walk," and they keep no years for it.

The Watching keep the one lived name: **the Before.** In their telling the world was Kind and Kind alone, then the Tree was already there, *watching*. The Giving came after the Watching. The Mother-name came after that. Each layer is a story laid over a seeing.

The Old Ways do not grant that becoming waited on a road. Heskoren's people already crossed doors (drowning, grove, stone) before any pilgrim mile. The Walking Years, when they arrived as a foreign traffic, were the beginning of *company*.

Kumbaan's hill-history does not owe Thaeloren a preface.

What can be said without inventing kings:

- The origin Tree is the one Tree no one grafted from another. It was not planted in a remembered summer.
- Far from it, people lived as they lived. Some were Struck at thresholds because thresholds exist. Some were never either Given or Struck, because there was no local fall to miss.
- When people organized the tenth-year trip, the Walking Years began. That organization is the start of the walk. The world was already there.

None of this is dated. A later chronicle that writes a first king, a first war, or a first sermon into this era is adding a later story to a time nobody dated. The Watching warn against that.

## The Walking Years

Once there was only the Awakening Tree, and a child's tenth-year Turning meant a *walk* to it. Families who could not make the trip **stayed UnTurned for want of it** (folk called them Kept), so the far reaches ran heavy on the UnTurned and the Struck as the ordinary count. People call them **the Walking Years**. The liturgical name is **Brenvaeth** *(BREN-vayth)*. That is the hearth gone to the waiting.

In these years the Leaf-Fall happened in one grove, on Maiethorn, under Thaeloren. A tenth summer was a journey, or it was no Leaf-Fall at all, because you were not there.

No town could show that a goddess had skipped the far-flung who stayed home. They were **children who did not walk.** Some were later Struck at a drowning, an oath, a death, unwitnessed, because the only crowd that counted was a continent away. Some stayed UnTurned for life, wanted or not, because want-of-a-trip is a distance.

Thin reach used to mean far from the one Tree. After the First Cut, thin reach came to mean *Tree-poor places*. The grafts there are young, sick, or few. A hamlet next-door to a dying scion is near on a map. The wood there is thin, which is what "far" came to mean.

The pilgrimage changed jobs when grafts began, and people still walk it. In the Years of Hands the devout still walk to the origin; the poor and the Tree-bereaved still walk to a neighbour. The Walking Years are the time when *everyone who Turned as Given had walked, or had been carried.*

The later romance of the old mile is a Hands-era feeling laid on Walking-era graves.

The era has no dated start. It grows out of Before the Walk when the trip becomes *the* way to Turn. It has a dated hinge: the First Cut (Cut-year 0). After that the Walking Years cease to be the *default* in lands the wood has reached, and remain the default in lands it has not. Heskoren is still, in places, *in* this era.

### The door is one week

Turning-Week is the week bracketing the High Solstice in **Eolthael**. Every child who has turned ten since the last High Solstice Turns that week, under Thaeloren, or they do not Turn as Given at all.

Miss the week and the door has shut. An eleven-year-old at the origin the following summer is a visitor. The Tree's Given-door is that tenth-year cohort, in that week. Struck doors still open later, elsewhere, with no leaf and no crowd. That is a different mercy, and it was always the far lands' other path.

This is why families from any distance **left early**, and why the richest or most frightened far households spent a *year* at the origin: a nine-year-old wintered in a road-house so they could not miss. The held-over child is the expensive mercy. Everyone else gambled one summer.

### Three walks, not one road

Named in the common tongue, because the walk was logistics before it was liturgy.

| Walk | Who used it | How long | What it cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **the Near Mile** | Maiethorn inland, anyone already on the Motherland | three to twelve days | a harvest-timing; almost everyone who meant to come, came |
| **the Salt Walk** | Strandoren: ship the Old Crossing, then the Near Mile | three to five days' sail plus the inland days; usually one to three weeks in all | a berth in fair weather; miss the last crossing and you miss the year |
| **the Long Mile** | Heskoren, and the Strandoren interior before the coasts were rich | six to twelve weeks | a year's surplus, or a road-house note, or you did not go |

On the road, people said, "We are on the Long Mile," or "We took the salt." Clerks keep the name Brenvaeth. Vaethorn still calls the whole past "the waiting-road." Lestrand files it as "the road-years." A Threnmaieth clerk wants the miles "on a roll."

**Timing against the year.** Near-Mile families left in **Haelren** or early **Eolthael** and were home by **Vaelorn**. Salt-Walk households watched the Old Crossing from **Saelorn**; a late storm in Hale-month left an UnTurned child. Long-Mile columns left in **Liren** or **Saelorn**, the thaw and the first green, and hoped the High Solstice had not happened without them. Some arrived in **Leddorn** with a ten-year-old who had already missed. The road-houses knew that silence. They did not charge extra for it. They also did not refund the note.

No walk crossed the storm-wall to Kumbaan.

### Who could afford it

The Near Mile was a civic season: whole towns moved a short way together, and a poor child walked in a neighbour's dust. Cost was real (lost labour, a week of food) and rarely decisive.

The Salt Walk sorted by **cash and weather.** Coastal wealth bought berths. Interior Strandoren was already "far" in these years, even though the coasts would later be Tree-rich. A family might send the Turning-child and one adult, and leave the rest. Guilds sometimes advanced a berth against a future colour. A Given Answered or Far-Voiced was worth the loan; a hug was a debt with no new trade in it. The Fair Hand would later call this a term. In Brenvaeth it was just the quay.

The Long Mile sorted by **whether you could lose a year.** Food, shoes, a second adult if you had one, and the note you signed at the first road-house that would take it. Many could not. Those who could often could not for *every* child.

**The split household** is the era's ordinary cruelty. A family with three children near ten could sometimes walk one. The one who walked might come home Given. The ones who stayed were UnTurned, or were Struck later at a local door, and grew up in the same kitchen as a sibling the origin had seen. Distance did this. Vaethorn's later guilt is a Hands-era reading of that fact. A preacher there says, "We wait because we are unworthy." The miles record the distance.

Old Ways households on Heskoren often **refused the walk** on purpose. Their children were already the land's. Motherfaith neighbours read those children as UnTurned for want of the trip, which is a misreading the walk itself cannot correct. Both families are telling the truth they have.

### Who was let through

Distance and coin were not the only sorters. A class of the untithed, the Closed Seat, which folk call the Grove-Sitters, sat on Thaeloren and decided who might stand under it. A family could have shoes and a Salt-Walk berth and still be turned away at the gate. **Grove-leave** was paper you carried to the gate.

Far lands ran heavy on the UnTurned because some children did not walk, and because some children walked and were turned away. Both fill a kitchen. The later romance of the old mile prefers the first cause. Folk songs remember the second.

The Closed Seat was an origin-gate, not a world-empire. Strandoren paid the crossing; it was never a province. Heskoren was beyond a gate's ride. When a Near-Mile town Turned under a copy, the gate was no longer the only way to Turn. The war that tried to make that untrue is the Closing, told in "A history in order" above.

### Who died

Children died on the road. So did the adults who carried them. River-crossings in the thaw, a fever in a packed Near-Mile inn, a late squall on the Old Crossing, hunger on a Long-Mile stretch between hearths, a birth that would not wait for the grove. Long roads kill people. That is what they do.

The graves are mostly unmarked at first. Then someone sets a stone at a day's walk, because the next column needs to know the water, and because a name should sit somewhere. A ribbon goes on the stone the year after. A later column uses the stone as a mile-mark. The romance and the graves are the same object seen at two hours of the day.

A child who dies on the way to be witnessed may **Strike as Returned** on the road. That is a becoming with no crowd, on the way to the only crowd that counted. Rare. Remembered. The dangerous edge of these years lives here, not in the Leaf-Fall waiting at the end of the walk.

The origin also filled with **children who arrived and had no one to walk home with.** Road-houses took some in (a bed that became a life). Origin-towns took some. A Given child with no one to carry the witness home is the other half of the grave: they lived, and belonging still had to be invented for them.

### Far reaches as the rule

In Brenvaeth, a land far from Thaeloren was **UnTurned-heavy and Struck-heavy because most ten-year-olds never stood under the canopy.** That was how the world worked anywhere the walk was too far or too dear.

- **Given** were the ones who had been *there*, a minority that shrank with distance. They came home as specialists the town could not otherwise grow, and as people the town had not seen become.
- **UnTurned** were the default far childhood, because they never stood under the Tree: whole, untithed, unwitnessed-as-Given. Folk called them Kept. The few who walked and were hugged were Kept in the strict sense. Some were wanted that way. Some were the price of a summer the family could not raise. Some were Old Ways children the Motherfaith had misfiled.
- **Struck** were the far lands' other door, and always had been. Drownings, oaths, deaths. No leaf. No origin-crowd. The unease that follows the Struck is older than the grafts. The walk made it louder, because the Given in the same town *had* been seen, a continent away.

When the wood later travelled, this pattern *relocated*. A Tree-poor pocket next to a dying scion has the same count of UnTurned and Struck. The place is near. The wood is thin. What decides it is where the wood has reached, not the distance. Today's Heskoren is this year. Some cohorts still walk. A valley over, children stand under a town Tree. Both can be true on the same road.

### Institutions of the road

In Brenvaeth the **origin-hearth** was the only Speaking that counted. Far towns had no Tree to tend. The road had inns, stones, and summer traffic. Tree-Wardens who carry grafts belong to the Years of Hands.

#### Mile-shrines

Water if the ground allows. A flat top for a pack. Often a name. Often a ribbon.

They begin as graves and waymarks in the same act. You bury someone where the column had to stop, and the next year the stop is the place you aim for. The devout later leave ribbons for the Tree. Children later climb them. Hands-towns later argue about pulling them down because the road now ends at a local canopy and the stone is in the way. The stone does not know which story it is in.

People tie what they have. A copper-green scrap on a Long-Mile stone is cloth. Travellers who have walked once treat it as cloth. Travellers who have only heard the romance look for a Taken-In's death in the colour.

#### Road-houses

Many deathless houses began here. In Brenvaeth they sold beds, porridge, a dry room, and **credit for a walk you could not miss twice.**

The cant is **brenhael** *(bren-HAYL)*. It is *bren* and *hael*. That is the hearth kept whole. Vaethorn says "waiting-hearth." Lestrand says "the bed-house" and still means the bank. A Threnmaieth clerk would like them "on the roll."

What a road-house actually sold:

- **A bed in the two crowded months**, and a quieter one in the other ten. Held-over nine-year-olds were the winter trade. Empty pilgrim-beds in a Hands-era upper room used to be this.
- **A note.** You eat now; you pay at the origin, or on the way home, or when the child is grown, or when a sibling walks next year. The Long-Lived innkeeper **forgets none of it.** That memory is why the paper will outlive the pilgrimage-season and become the trusted note. In the act, it is an innkeeper who will still know your name in fifty years, which is a kindness and a leash.
- **A little Tithe-furnishing**, improvised, not a guild. A green-lot behind the house for a Taken-In parent. A shed where a Far-Voiced can cry without taking the dormitory with them. The road itself is novelty enough that a Long-Lived innkeeper's own Tithe stays fed, which is why the deathless *stayed* in the trade, and why the same people later endow libraries: the hunger that kept them on the mile is the hunger that built the archive. Brenvaeth had a pot and a shed.
- **A held bed.** A family said they would come. They did not. The innkeeper keeps the bed a year, then two, then as a habit that looks like hospitality and functions as a shrine.

The White Note's founding-summer is a house-year, not a Cut-year. In the Walking Years that house was a hearth with too many boots by the door. This is the same fortune, earlier.

#### Summer traffic

For two months the Near Mile is a moving town of columns and dust. A Far-Voiced calls from ridge to ridge, "The road is full," "The ford is wrong," "A child is down." Two-Bodied range the edges because the ranging *is* the Tithe and the column is a herd. Markets grow at the mile-shrines and vanish when Eolthael ends. Then the road is empty and local, and the road-house counts notes.

People walked with whoever left the same week. A Bound's word might hold a column together for a fortnight. A Kept adult with no Tithe to manage was, then as now, the person who could carry the extra pack.

### Witness at the origin, or not at all

In Brenvaeth the town that *saw you Turn* was the crowd under Thaeloren, or no one. Citizenship-by-local-witness comes later, with the Years of Hands.

- **Going home did not bring the crowd with you.** Your village had not watched. Belonging at home required someone who *had* been under the boughs to stand for you: a parent, a neighbour from the column, a Long-Lived who keeps origin-falls the way they later keep town-falls. **Vouching-at-a-distance was invented to carry the origin's witness home.** The three pillars are older than the grafts. Those are Long-Lived memory, a Bound word, and a Far-Voiced feeling. The *job* of carrying a far Turning is Walking-era.
- **A Given child with no surviving witness** is legally thin in the place they grew up. Origin-orphans, road-house children, a Bound stranger who will swear the colour they did not see (and cannot cheaply lie). The unvouched are older than the Struck-stigma, and include people the Tree *did* Give.
- **Struck on the road** are a double unease: unwitnessed becoming, *on the way* to the witnessing. A Returned child walking the last Near-Mile days beside the parent who has not yet forgiven the threshold.
- **Staying home** meant you were never Given-witnessed. You were still of your town, because your town had watched you be a child. An UnTurned child was not unmarked on a roll. Folk called such children Kept, though no Tree had hugged them.

The origin-crowd was enormous in Eolthael and ordinary the rest of the year. Speakers of the origin-hearth named colours until their voices went. Folk-known colours still were; the warden's word made them *count* for people who would have to carry that word home.

### Long-Lived who still say "I walked"

A Long-Lived who says "I walked" is saying when they were young. Memory is perfect. What they mean by it is argued. There is no single Long-Lived account.

At present (Cut-year 387) the sentence does at least four jobs, and they argue:

- **"I walked as a child, under Thaeloren, before my town had wood."** A Maiethorn deathless Given in the late Walking is speaking of a summer most of the room has only as a ribbon. Those were the last core cohorts, before Maiethorn filled in the first Cut-years. A Heskoren deathless may mean "last decade." Same words. The wood reached their towns at different times.
- **"I walked the mile as the inn."** A road-house Long-Lived may never have stood under the canopy as a pilgrim. They walked the same stretch every summer for two hundred years. They remember every debtor. They will not always tell you which children came back.
- **"I remember the graves and I will not call them holy."** Skeptics among the deathless. The road was biology plus bad fords. They are sincere. They still tear up at a ribbon, which they will not explain.
- **"I remember the graves and I will not call them logistics."** Devout deathless. Every stone was a waiting. They are sincere. They still know which ford killed which name, which they will tell you whether you asked or not.

Blood-Read will give you a true fragment of a walk. It will not tell you which of the four jobs the living speaker is doing.

They also disagree with Seat chronicles that start at the First Cut. A Long-Lived who walked as a child and a clerk who counts Cut-years will fight about the same summer. Both can be honest. Only one of them was there.

### What a character notices

- Dust on a Near-Mile road in Hale-month, and none in Ledger-month.
- A mile-shrine with a ribbon and a climbing-scuff, and a Hands-child who does not know it was a grave.
- An inn's upper room with too many beds for the present trade. The house still dates its notes from a founding-summer the beds were full.
- A Held bed that has not been let in anyone's living mortal memory. The innkeeper can tell you the name. They will not tell you they think the child is dead, or Kept, or Old Ways, or never born.
- A sibling-pair who will not walk the same side of a road: one Given, and one who never stood under a Tree. That second child is UnTurned. Folk still call them Kept.
- A Far-Voiced calls from a ridge, in an old pattern, "The ford is wrong." No column answers. Birds do.
- Someone at a table says, "I walked," and the room sorts itself by who goes quiet.

### A Turning-Week slate

*From the third hearth on the Near Mile, kept by Thilim, who is Long-Lived and has kept this house since before the Cut, as later clerks would say. The verso is knife-scored with tally-marks that do not match the recto.*

> *Eolthael, the summer the ford ran high.*
>
> *Beds: forty-and-two spoken for. Notes out: Tora of the long cape, two people, pay on the home-walk. Valein of the salt, one person, paid at the quay. The Held: Seine of the far cape, tenth summer, not come. Hold the bed. The mother said next year. Next year was last year.*
>
> *Porridge: enough. Shed: the blue-leaf girl from the second column cried the rafters quiet; no charge; we needed the quiet. Green-lot: used. The Taken-In man from the long cape left a cutting. I put it in the pot. It is not a Tree.*
>
> *I do not ask which summer was holy. I ask which people will be here at dark. The Held bed is made. If Seine comes I will not have to remember I made it for nothing.*

The cutting in the pot is not a First Cut. It is a man who missed his green.

## The First Cut

Someone cut living wood from Thaeloren (or from a branch that came away in a hand), carried it a day's earth, married it to new ground, and it *took*. A town on the Near Mile Turned, a generation later, under a canopy that was not the origin. People call it **the First Cut**. The liturgical name is **Eoloren** *(EOL-or-en)*. That is the witnessed hinge. The clerical count of years starts here.

Everyone agrees *a first successful town-graft occurred.* It happened within a day's living-earth of Thaeloren, close enough that a cut could still be green when it met new ground. That is horticulture. No two houses agree whose story the cut is.

The first green is not named. Folk who need an object call it **the nameless green**. Naming the town would pick a hearth, and a hearth would pick a hand.

A freshly planted scion still takes **a generation** to hold a Leaf-Fall. So Cut-year 0 is a wound in wood. The first *local Turning* is about twenty summers later. "A graft arrived" and "this town Turns under its own canopy" are never the same year. The wave is slow because each Hand must grow up before it can be a source.

### The five attributions

The five stories are *jobs*. Which one a room is doing tells you more than a creed would. Who cut stays unpicked. All five stay in use.

#### Folk: the stolen branch

A parent who would not watch another child stay UnTurned for want of a trip, or would not bury another child on the Near Mile, took wood without leave. Mercy as theft.

- **The nameless knife** is the object, not a saint. Some hearths keep a kitchen-knife on a ribbon the way Hands-children climb mile-shrines: they cannot say whether they are honouring a theft or a gift. There is no name on the handle.
- **Thief-songs** on the Near Mile are walking-songs. The singers say "we" for the cutter. A Salt-Walk singer makes it a quay-joke and says, "The first unpaid berth." Long-Mile versions are not funny. Vaethorn's later guilt calls this parent "unworthy." The songs do not agree.
- **Folk-right cutting** continued after the Seat wrote a leave. A Taken-In with a sick neighbour and no year to wait will still take a slip. Most of those slips die. The ones that take are how the queue learns it is not the only horticulture in the world. Road-hands call this a problem. Towns that lived through a stolen year call it a year they Turned.

The later Cutting-leave fee is, in this telling, **a fine that forgot it was a fine.** Lestrand will broker it without blushing. That is this story with a clerk.

A stolen Hand still left a town behind that did not get the next one. The songs leave that town and go on.

#### Clergy: the warden ahead of the college

A tree-warden of a Near-Mile hearth cut with a leave that did not yet exist. The wood took. The origin-hearth spent **the generation of argument** trying to decide whether this was blight, theft, or rite. That argument ran from Cut-year 0 into the first local Leaf-Fall. The First Seat as a *Cutting-leave desk* is what that argument left on the ground. The Road-hands are this scandal in an office. They are the people who walk what used to be unsanctioned.

- **Speakers who will not say "first."** They say, "The witnessed hinge." They will date a note. They will not point at a grave. Some of them are skeptics who grant the horticulture and refuse the saint. Some of them are devout who think naming a cutter would steal the Tree's offering. Both rooms keep that silence.
- **The quiet years at the origin.** After the first copy, the Motherwood tried to forbid further cuts. Wood died in pots anyway. Neighbours cut anyway. Forbidding a copy that had already taken is how a college learns it does not own the Tree's tissue, only the *authorization.*
- **Road-hands as regularized heresy.** They were not designed as a travelling church. They are the generation of argument, given a cart.

A Speaker says, "We will write how, not who." That sentence is how a heresy becomes a monopoly. The college writes the how.

#### Devout: the Tree offered

A branch came away. No theft, no genius. A Hand giving a Hand. The Mother (or the Tree, if you will not say Mother) *meant* the wood to travel. This is the version the Seat prefers in sermons, because it makes the scion-queue sound like stewardship rather than rationing.

- **Eoloren sermons** at Turning-Week in the Motherwood. A Speaker says, "She sent her hands outward so the hearths could stay." The graves of the Walking Years are named in the same breath, on a good day. On a bad day they are not.
- **The watering-words** as proof. A devout neighbour says, "If the marrying-rite were only horticulture, why would the wood listen?" A skeptic says, "Because you watered it." Both can stand under the same canopy.
- **Cutting-leave as blessing.** Vaethorn calls it "the wood's blessing." The devout use that name. Without it the queue is a list of who waits.

Devout sermons say, "She meant the wood to travel." The wave went to paying coasts before it went to the poor far. The thing saved, when anything was, was a walk. Not everyone's.

#### The Watching: horticulture, then a later name

People had always been able to cut living wood. Fruit-grafts, orchard-kin, a slip in a pot. The Motherfaith later named one cut the First, and started a count. That is a story laid over a seeing. The Tree was already seeing. A Watcher says, "A date does not make a mother."

- **They refuse the Cut-count.** A Watcher clerk will date a debt from a house-year, a drowning-year, a night they kept. They will convert to Eoloren-count if a court makes them, and they will not call it history.
- **Pear-grafts as the tell.** Watcher hearths on Maiethorn still graft ordinary fruit in the same week a Road-hand passes with carrying-earth. They do not think this is a joke. They think it is the whole argument, done with hands.
- **The withheld *first*.** Same cadence as the withheld blessing. A Watcher says, "What was cut, was seen. What took, was seen. The Tree does not reach twice by chance." The sentence names no Mother and no epoch.

Children still died on the road until the copies took. The Watching's refusal is a name for the count. The graves are in the ground.

#### The Old Ways: the land received a guest

In the Old Ways telling, a grove took in kin. The Hands are when guests began arriving from the east. Host-rights start at that receiving. A college's warrant is a different paper.

- **The first meal** for a new graft is food left at the planting. You feed a guest that way before you ask their business. The Door-Keepers still do this. A Road-hand who eats is doing host-courtesy whether the Seat wrote it down or not. A Road-hand who plants and leaves is a claiming.
- **Guest-reading vs First-reading.** A Guest-reading town will pilgrimage *and* keep the meal. A First-reading district will tend the same wood as a guest in their house and still refuse the Tender's blessing. Both start the story at a *receiving*. The Cut is a mainland word for an arrival.
- **Their count does not open at Cut-year 0.** On Heskoren the guests *arrive* in the Cut-year 200s. Motherfaith calls that arrival "late." A Seat map calls it "the First Cut, delayed." The land was already making children at the old doors.

A guest who takes a generation to speak will still be called a Tree by the children who Turn under it. That speaking is the first local Leaf-Fall. Host-rights do not stop a town from becoming Hands-era.

People tell all five. Skeptics grant the horticulture. They can see it. They deny that a mind chose the travelling. The Fair Hand enters when the wood boards a ship. The Open Table keeps moons, and stays off this argument.

### The generation of argument

Cut-year 0 is a cut. The next twenty summers are copies, dead wood, a college that cannot un-cut, and a first local Leaf-Fall that ends the argument without settling it.

**What people actually did.**

- Near-Mile towns cut because the first green was visible from the next town. Living tissue, a day's earth, a pot, a Taken-In who knew soil. This is why the origin-towns filled first. A sapling can survive that distance. Worthiness is a later sermon.
- Most early copies **died.** Mortal wood, wrong season, a pot that dried on the Near Mile in Hale-month, a well-meant slip with no carrying-earth. The deaths are why the Seat could tell itself the first taking was a fluke, for a while.
- The origin-hearth tried **forbidding.** Speakers named it theft, blight, a wound in the First Hand. Neighbours cut anyway. A prohibition with no riders cannot police a day's walk in a pre-print world.
- **Proof arrived as a ceremony.** When the nameless green held its first Turning-Week, a colour fell (or a hug; the tellings do not agree, and it does not change the paper). A town that was not the origin had Turned its own child. Citizenship-by-local-witness begins in that town, a generation after the knife. The Walk is no longer the only door. The Seat can argue doctrine until the leaves fall. It cannot argue the leaves back onto Thaeloren.

**What the Seat captured.**

The college could not own the tissue. It could own **who may cut next.** That is the copy-right. **Cutting-leave** is the paper. The **scion-queue** is what a copy-right becomes when demand outruns a generation of wood.

The fee is framed as carrying-earth and a Road-hand's year. Folk hear a fine. Lestrand hears a licence. Threnmaieth wants it on a roll. Vaethorn calls it an offering. Same coin, four stances. Historically it is how an institution absorbed a heresy and called it horticulture. Unsanctioned cutting became authorized cutting. The practice survived by changing owner.

Road-hands are not a second order. They are the people you send when you have claimed the knife and still need the wood to walk.

Spreading Trees made a queue. Struck stigma, Tithe-leverage, the pitied Kept: none of that dissolved when a second canopy opened. The new injustice is *which town next*, paid in twenty more years of borrowed Turnings. She did not write the list. People did.

### How the wave moved

A scion still takes a generation to Turn. A healthy town Tree may supply the next cut, acting on the origin's behalf. So the wave is a **chain of maturing Hands**, not a miracle crate. Sea can jump a chain. A Long-Mile cart cannot, until carrying-earth lasts and there are rest-hearths that are not just pilgrim-inns.

#### Maiethorn, Cut-years 0–80 (origin-towns first; Rain-Shadow slower)

The Near Mile stops being a requirement in the core, town by town, as canopies come of age. Origin-towns first: a day's earth, then two, then a valley. The pilgrimage remains: devotion at Thaeloren, and necessity wherever a graft has not yet spoken.

**The Rain-Shadow is slower.** That is Maiethorn's dry east, beyond the Rain-Wall. Same continent, thin soil, wells, sand-country. A cut that takes in the Sacred Core dies in the dry hills unless a Taken-In and a lucky year agree. This is dirt. Fox of the Sands hearths still walk west toward Thaeloren after coastal towns have already stopped. The wave runs at two speeds on one continent.

A Seat sermon says, "Her hands filled the Motherland." Folk memory says, "We stopped burying children on the Near Mile where the wood took, and we did not stop where it didn't." Both sentences can be said in the same market.

By Cut-year 80 the Motherland is **old Hands** in the core and **still waiting** in pockets. That waiting is why a Maiethorn hamlet can still send this year's ten-year-olds to a neighbour. The walk is this year.

#### Strandoren, Cut-years 40–160, by sea (wealthy coasts first, interior later)

The Old Crossing already knew how to move a child. Moving a Hand is the same berth with worse instructions. The written instructions say, "Living earth. Do not stow below. Water at dawn." A Taken-In or a Road-hand sleeps beside the pot.

**Why the sea starts while Maiethorn is still filling (Cut-year 40).** A ship is faster than filling every inland hamlet. A wealthy coast can pay a Cutting-leave and a Salt-Walk crew in the same summer a Near-Mile village is still on the Seat's list. The wave went where ships and fees went.

**Coasts first.** Port-cities take. Deep interior waits on river-days and a cart, which is a small Long Mile. Strandoren's later high reach and worn faith sit together. The wood arrived as cargo. The Fair Hand treats that cargo as needing no mother, and dates the carrying as a supply-chain fact. They will attend a planting as witnesses to a signing. They date a signing, and they leave the wound in wood to other houses. Their name for Eoloren is "the first carrying," and they mean a bill of lading.

**What the Salt Walk becomes.** When a coast Turns under its own canopy, the berth stops being a tenth-year necessity and becomes a luxury, a contract-hinge, a devout extra. Road-houses on the inland days lose a season of boots and keep the notes.

By Cut-year 160 the Shore-lands are **mature Hands** on the water and still patchy inland. Lestrand files the whole motion as "the carrying." The ships are in that filing.

#### The gap (why Heskoren waits until Cut-year 200)

Three stacked frictions, none of them unworthiness:

1. **Wood dies on the Long Mile.** Weeks to months. A day's earth is a Near-Mile problem. A ship's hold is a solved Salt-Walk problem if you water it. A cart between hearths in thaw-mud is how scions become sticks. Until carrying-earth lasts, and until there are **rest-hearths whose job is wood, not pilgrims**, Heskoren cannot be a circuit. Road-hands as a standing office had to exist before the far could be a route rather than a funeral of saplings.
2. **The chain has to grow up.** You cut from the origin, or from a healthy town Tree. Maiethorn's first generation of sources come of age in the Cut-year 20s–40s. Strandoren's coastal Hands are not ready to be sources until later still. You cannot skip to the sundered reach without a mature link, unless you spend origin-wood on a death-march. The Seat did not, mostly. That is horticulture and it is also a choice about who is next.
3. **The queue prefers a paying next.** After the coasts, carrying-earth is committed to interiors, replacements, sick Trees: the quiet work that does not look like a wave. Heskoren is poor, far, and already accused (by a later guilt-theology) of waiting because it deserved to. They waited because the wood was slow, the road was long, and the list was written in the Motherwood.

Vaethorn will preach, "We wait because we are unworthy." The gap is the evidence they use. The chronicle records the slow wood, the long road, and the list.

#### Heskoren, Cut-years 200–387 (still receiving)

The frontier coast faces the sea back toward Strandoren; that is the lifeline of new grafts. Luckiest towns have had a local Turning for a few generations (a scion that arrived in the 200s and spoke in the 220s, or later). Whole districts still send children down the road, to a neighbour's new canopy, or, still, the Long Mile, if the neighbour is a stick in a pot.

Today's Heskoren is this year. Some cohorts still walk. A valley over, children stand under a town Tree. That is the wave in the present tense. Both can be true on the same road. Vaethorn's waiting is the wave's front.

Old Ways host-rights start *here* as a lived collision, not as a Maiethorn sermon. A graft in an old Taken-In green is a guest. A Cutting-leave is a mainland paper. The meal comes first, or the planting is a claiming.

A Seat sermon says, "Her will is still travelling." Folk memory says, "The cart came late, the soil was called thin, the three hamlets past the ford can see Harrow's green from here." New towns already say which canopy they can see. On Heskoren that is the decade.

#### Kumbaan: never

No graft crossed the storm-wall, and Kumbaan has no Tree. Road-hands do not carry a Tree over drowning sea. Sailors tell of pots that went into the water. Dead wood is not a Tree. No old scion has ever been found on the Isle. The Open Table owes Eoloren nothing. Moons, not Cut-years.

| | Seat / devout sermon | Folk memory (and the other houses) |

| The knife | She offered a Hand | Someone took one |
| The paper | Stewardship; horticulture | A fine that forgot; a licence; a warrant |

A Long-Lived who says "I walked" and a Speaker who keeps the Eoloren-count will fight about the same summer. A Long-Lived who planted (a Road-hand's long tenure, an innkeeper who watched pots replace children) will not always tell you which taking they think was holy. Memory is perfect. What the sentence means is argued.

The unvouched Struck, the pitied Kept, guild conscription, and Tithe-leverage are still in the towns. Spreading Trees *relocated* who is expected to have been seen at ten. A Hands-town can still meet a feared Struck with a rope. A local canopy leaves that rope where it was.

### What a character notices

- A kitchen-knife on a ribbon in a Near-Mile house that will not name a saint.
- Watchers grafting pears in the same week a Road-hand waters carrying-earth.
- A first-meal bowl at a planting, licked clean by dogs, which the Seat did not write down.
- A clerk still copies a ship's instruction: "One living hand. Do not stow below. Water at dawn." The clerk files it as cargo.
- Three hamlets that can see a neighbour's canopy and are still on the queue. The college's word for the wait stays in the college.
- A Rain-Shadow child on the west-road after the core has stopped walking.
- Empty Salt-Walk berths in Eolthael, and a hold that still smells of wet earth out of season.
- A Long-Lived who planted says something other than "I walked." The job is the planting. The room still goes quiet.

### A minute of the First Seat

*Attributed to the Speaker Rithnali, from the summer the nameless green held its first Turning-Week. Later clerks file it as Cut-year 19. The verso is a list of pots that died, which no one read aloud.*

> *The wood that took within a day's earth of the First was cut without leave. We will not write who. We will write how, so the next cut does not kill what it carries.*
>
> *Carry in living earth. Marry with the watering-words. Do not cut a second without the Seat. If the scion sickens, send word; do not take another wound in the First to hide the first.*
>
> *The Tree does not reach twice by chance, and neither do we. From this summer a cut is a leave, or it is a theft we have decided not to chase. The towns that wait will wait on horticulture. The soil is thin in more places than it is not.*

Folk who keep the nameless knife think this paper is a confession. The Seat thinks it is competence. Both can hold the same copy.

### Consequences

- **The wave starts.** The Grafting is a *wave*. That is wood carried outward, a generation to mature, Maiethorn first, Strandoren by sea, Heskoren last and live, and Kumbaan never.
- **Where the wood has taken, a town Tree becomes the default.** Turning under a town canopy is the ordinary way. The pilgrimage remains. Its new job is devotion, prestige, or necessity when a graft fails.
- **The Seat gains a bottleneck.** Cutting-leave and the scion-queue are how an institution absorbed a heresy and called it horticulture. She did not write the queue.
- **The clerical epoch.** Cut-years begin. Other people do not have to care.
- **Language and money.** Local Trees let daughters grow in place; the road's conserving pull eases. Road-houses in the core lose pilgrim-seasons and keep the notes.

## The Years of Hands

A town Turning Tree is the ordinary way to Turn *where the wood has taken.* People call them **the Years of Hands**, and also *Since the Hands*. The liturgical name is **Ornthael** *(ORN-thale)*. That is Tree-land. The Grafting is still moving through the present.

This is the era the rest of the world lives in without naming it. Town Trees. Local Leaf-Fall. Citizenship because *this* town watched you. The pilgrimage to Thaeloren as an extra, not the only door.

It is also incomplete. Read the reach-gradient as a *date*, not as a finished map:

- **Maiethorn:** old Hands. Dense mature grafts. The Walking is biography for the deathless and a ribbon on a mile-shrine for everyone else, except in the Rain-Shadow, and except wherever a Tree has sickened. Old Hands can un-Hands. A sickened Tree sends that town's children back onto a road. The Years of Hands go on around them.
- **Strandoren:** mature Hands, worn faith. The road-houses finished becoming banks here. The walk is a luxury, a contract-hinge, and a hold that still smells of earth when a pot is the passenger.
- **Heskoren:** the Grafting *this decade.* Luckiest towns have had a local canopy for a few generations. Whole districts still send children down the road. Vaethorn's waiting is the wave's front.
- **Kumbaan:** never took a graft, and does not walk its children to Thaeloren. It sits off both clocks.

The pilgrimage changed jobs, and people still walk it. The First Seat still authorizes cuts, now as a queue. Deathless houses still remember the road, now as credit. Mile-shrines still stand, as folklore, as argument, and as a thing children climb. The romance of the old mile belongs to the Years of Hands. The graves belong to the Walking Years. They stand on the same ground.

A child on the Long Mile this summer is making the week.

### The walk's three jobs

In Brenvaeth the walk was *how you Turned.* In Ornthael it is three jobs that share roads, inns, and the one-week door, and do not share a reason.

| Job | Who still walks | What they are buying | On the road |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Devotion: the extra mile** | People who already have a local Tree and still want Thaeloren | an origin-fall; a ribbon at the First Hand; the Motherwood in Eolthael | They could have Turned at home. They walk anyway. |
| **Prestige: the First-Hand year** | Lestrand money, a Bound signing-watch, a guild-ticket hinge, a wedding extra | being seen at the origin in a year they could have stayed home; a held-over winter as a product | Piety may be in the party. The Fair Hand attends as witnesses to a signing. |
| **Necessity: the neighbour's week**. It is still *the old mile* when the neighbour is a continent. | Tree-poor, Tree-sick, Tree-young; a missed fall this year; Heskoren this cohort; the Rain-Shadow after the core stopped | a Given-door in another town | They are making the week. |

On the road, people said, "We are going to the Thallow," or "We Turn at Harrow's," or "The berth is for the child." Clerks keep the name Ornthael. Vaethorn still calls almost any walk "the waiting-road." Lestrand files the extra as "the First-Hand year" and the need as "the borrowed week." A Threnmaieth clerk wants the miles "on a roll."

**They share the Near Mile in Hale-month.** A devotion-column, a prestige household with too much luggage, a necessity-family who sold a year, and a Road-hand going the other way with a pot, can sleep in the same upper room. The innkeeper can tell who is which. They will not always tell you. The Far-Voiced ridge-cry still works. Someone calls, "The ford is wrong." Some who answer are pilgrims. Some are carts of wood.

**The inversion, lived.** Thaelvaeth is wood carried *out*. Brenvaeth was hearths walking *in*. In the Years of Hands both directions are traffic. A Road-hand and a tenth-year pass on a bank and do not owe each other a sermon. That passing *is* the two clocks in one afternoon.

**Hands can un-Hands.** A town Tree that sickens, or that fails a fall, puts a Hands-town back on the neighbour's week. A sick Tree is not the age ending. The wood has thinned. The Seat calls that thin soil. The town calls it this year.

The one-week door did not loosen. Miss Eolthael and the Given-door has shut, at home or at the origin. Struck remains the later mercy. There is still no second-chance year. The held-over nine-year-old is still the expensive mercy, and on the prestige-walk it is also a winter you can buy.

No extra mile crosses the storm-wall to Kumbaan.

### What the Walk left behind

What the Walking built, the Hands still sleep in, and still use. A Hands-town that treats a mile-shrine as only a ruin is telling itself the road is over.

#### Mile-shrines

Still water, a flat top, often a name, often a ribbon. In a Hands-town the last one is frequently **in the square**, because the town grew around a graft planted beside the road, and the road's destination moved closer.

They are, at once:

- **Graves.** Walking facts. A Long-Lived who says "I walked" can still tell you which ford the name belongs to.
- **Waymarks.** Necessity-walkers still drink there. Road-hands still aim for the water.
- **Folklore.** Devotion leaves a ribbon. Hands-children climb.
- **Argument.** A warden wants it moved because it is in the way of the Leaf-Fall crowd. A house that still dates notes from a founding-summer wants it left. A neighbour who still walks wants it left for a different reason.

The stone does not know which story it is in. A ribbon is still not a leaf-colour.

Hands-towns that **pull a stone down** are doing theology with a hammer. The road no longer has to happen there. The graves stay in the ground. The next necessity-column will miss the water.

#### The upper room

An old inn's upper room has too many beds for the present trade. In Brenvaeth they were the two crowded months. In Ornthael they are:

- **Let to merchants.** The White Note's conversion; occupancy as competence.
- **Opened for Eolthael.** Devotion and prestige still swell a Near-Mile house for a fortnight.
- **Held.** A family said they would come. They did not. The innkeeper keeps the bed a year, then two, then as a habit that looks like hospitality and functions as a shrine *and* as proof the house remembers. Thilim still makes Seine's. Houses that became banks mostly stopped making them, except the one they will not let. A clerk writes "house custom" on a slate and means a grave.

The romance of the empty bed belongs to the Years of Hands. The name written for the held bed belongs to the Walking Years.

#### Roads that end at a Tree

The pilgrim-road was often the main road. A graft took beside it. The town grew around the canopy. **The road now ends at the Tree.** The last holy mile is a square. Beyond the boughs the old way is a cart-track to timber, a neighbour, a ford. Children are told the road goes to the Motherwood. It goes to the mill. Both can be true if you keep walking, which most Hands-children do not.

For that town, the road ends at the Tree, and the children Turn at home. The family from the next valley is still walking those stones, because the wood has not reached them. Same stones.

#### Other things the Walk left that a character notices

- Empty Salt-Walk berths in Eolthael, except the expensive ones and the holds that smell of wet earth out of season.
- A Far-Voiced calls from a ridge, in an old pattern, "The ford is wrong." No column answers. A Road-hand does. Birds do.
- A sibling-pair who will not walk the same side of a road: one Given, one UnTurned. Folk still call the second one Kept. They are a split household's grandchildren, keeping a courtesy they cannot date.
- Markets that still swell at an old mile-mark in Hale-month, then vanish, even when no column comes. The habit outlasted the road.
- A kitchen-knife on a ribbon in a Near-Mile house that will not name a saint. That is the nameless knife, and it is still not a museum piece.

### Deathless houses' road-past as present credit

Many deathless houses began as road-houses. That fate-shift is already in the past: inns that became banks, the same Long-Lived, a different fortune.

**The founding-summer is the credit.** "We kept you on the mile" is an origin-story that still collects. A century-loan written from a house-year is not only a dating-quirk. It is a claim that the porridge was real, the note was real, and the house is still the same house. No mortal court can audit a Long-Lived's memory of a walk two hundred summers back. That is why the trusted note holds. It is also why a house can call a debt whose grandchildren do not remember the ford.

**Two fates of the same origin, still standing.**

- **The White Note House.** The reformation. Beds let to merchants. Paper on the quay. Ledan's query from the house's 200th summer (Cut-year 280) is already a clerk's problem; the house is now in its 307th. The Held names are in the founding-book. Most of those beds are let. One is not, which is how a bank proves it used to be an inn.
- **The Third Hearth.** The house that stayed a hearth. Thilim still keeps it, still makes Seine's bed, still knows which guests will be here at dark. The notes are smaller. The shrine is larger. Lestrand says, "A bed-house that failed to become a bank." Thilim keeps the hearth.

The long-house of books is still the deathless paying their own Tithe. The road-past is why anyone trusted them to endow in the first place: a house that remembered every debtor on the mile is a house you let steer a library.

**The prestige-walk is now a product some houses sell.** A held-over winter at the origin, once the frightened far mercy, is a First-Hand year you can book. The White Note did not invent that. They noticed the upper room was empty in Nethael and full of the wrong people in Eolthael, and they made a term for it. The year is assembled on three desks: **Netstrand charters the hull, the White Note writes the term, Orentel holds the berth.** Orenbren houses the sleep at the origin. Necessity-families still sign the old kind of note: eat now, pay on the home-walk, or when the child is grown, or when a sibling walks next year. Same paper, two jobs. The clerk who files both as occupancy is counting beds, not jobs.

Vaethorn will preach, "The houses abandoned the waiting-road." The walk stopped being required in the core. The memory did not. The paper outlived the season. That is a reformation, and it still collects. Spreading Trees did not make the notes kind.

### Heskoren as the live front

Today's Heskoren is this year. Some cohorts still walk. A valley over, children stand under a town Tree. That is the wave in the present tense. Both can be true on the same road.

**Harrow's Green** is the luckiest kind of Heskoren town: a scion that arrived in the Cut-year 200s, spoke a generation later, and has now Turned local children for a few generations. It is a neighbour's canopy. From the ford, that canopy is in sight, and the towns past the ford are still waiting. New towns say, "We Turned at Harrow's Tree before we had our own." That is how they talk everywhere. On Heskoren it is the decade.

**The Three Hamlets Past the Ford** are the waiting kind. The Seat's Cutting-leave already named them as thin soil. They send this year's ten-year-olds to Harrow's, or further if Harrow's year is too full, or still the Long Mile if the neighbour is a stick in a pot. The college's word for the wait stays in the college. They can see the canopy. Folk-right cutting is a temptation with a kitchen-knife. Most slips die. The ones that take are how the queue learns it is not the only horticulture in the world.

**Old Ways host-rights start here as a collision, not a sermon.** A graft in an old Taken-In green is a guest. A Cutting-leave is a mainland paper. The first meal comes first, or the planting is a claiming. The Door-Keepers and the Road-hands can both love the wood and still fight about the bowl.

**Borrowed Turnings are the front's ordinary year.** Citizenship-by-local-witness is a Years-of-Hands practice. The town that *saw you* may be Harrow's, while the hamlet that raised you is a different place. Vouching-at-a-distance, invented to carry an *origin*-fall home, now carries a *neighbour*-fall home. Same three pillars. Shorter road. The unvouched Struck are still older than the grafts. A local canopy leaves them in the town.

A Seat sermon says, "Her will is still travelling." Folk memory says, "The cart came late, the soil was called thin, we can see Harrow's green from here." Vaethorn will preach, "We wait because we are unworthy." They wait because the wood is slow, the road is long, and the list was written in the Motherwood.

The Rain-Shadow on Maiethorn is the same wait on an older continent: waiting that went on after the core stopped. Ornsael is a town where this happens; its Tree Speaks most years, and its well is dropping. A west-road child after the Sacred Core has stopped walking is the same waiting, on the continent where the Hands first matured. Ornthael is still underway there.

### What is strained

What a traveller can already see:

- The **First Seat** is old in the queue. It has authorized cuts since Cut-year 19, about three hundred and sixty-eight summers. Towns that can see a neighbour's canopy have learned the horticulture-sentence by heart. Folk-right slips still happen. Threnmaieth still wants the college on the roll. The Seat is *heavy.*
- The **Road-hands** are thin on the Heskoren front. Living earth, mortal wood, a skeleton circuit, authority that expires a week out from the Motherwood. A live Tree is a quiet success no one writes down. A dead one is a name on a complaint. They plant what they were given leave to plant. They sleep in the town that was not chosen.
- **Waiting towns** hang on a single scion, or on a neighbour's week. That is a civic crisis wearing a child's summer.
- **Deathless houses** are richer in paper than in beds. The road-past is the credit. The upper room is the tell.

The pressure is already visible.

### A letting-slate from the White Note's inland house

*Kept by Mataero, who is junior to Ledan and has never made a Held bed. Cut-year 387; the house-year is not written on the recto. The verso is a copy of founding-names in an older hand.*

> *Nethael closing. Upper room: twelve beds. Let: eight, to the wool out of Lestrand, term through Hale-month, pay on the quay-note. Open for Eolthael: four, First-Hand year, held-over child already spoken for, the mother paid the winter in advance. Occupancy will cover the loft.*
>
> *Do not let the north bed. House custom. The founding-book has a name I am not to copy onto a term. Ledan says the house is the same house. I say the house is the note. The bed is empty. Empty is not a shrine. Empty is a bed we are not charging for.*
>
> *Thilim's third hearth still holds a far-cape name. We do not. We are not an inn that failed. We are the house that remembered long enough to be trusted.*
>
> *I do not ask which summer was holy. I ask which debtors will pay in Ledger-month. If the north bed is a grave, it is a grave that does not belong on a letting-slate.*

## Folklore of the road, the Cut, and what the Walk left behind

Three teaching-stories. The bones stay the same. Each teller adds a last line.

### The Child Who Counted Stones

The teaching-story of the road is a child counting stones. It is told on the Walking Years' three walks, and still told in Hands-towns that have never sent a child as far as the next valley. The count holds the romance and the graves together.

A child asked how far it was to the Tree.

The mother said, "As many days as there are stones."

The child walked, and counted. At each stone there was a ribbon, or there was not. The child asked if the ribbons were for people who had arrived.

The mother said, "Some."

They drank at the stones. They slept in their lee. They used them to know the water. The child kept count, and the count was the days, and the count was also the names the mother would not read aloud.

They arrived. A leaf fell, or the leaves hugged. The tellings do not agree, and it does not change the walk home.

On the way back the child asked to leave a ribbon.

The mother said, "We are not a stone yet."

The child left one anyway, on a stone that already had too many, and could not say, later, whether they had been adding a day or a grave.

A Long-Lived who keeps a hearth on that mile will tell you the child's name. They will not tell you which summer. They still make a bed they will not let.

**The last lines.** The bones above are common. The teller adds one of these:

- **Near Mile (devout).** A devout teller on the Near Mile says, "The Mother had counted them first. The stones were how she kept the days."
- **Near Mile (tired).** A tired teller on the Near Mile says, "It was a road. The stones were so you did not miss the water."
- **Salt Walk.** A Salt-Walk teller says, "The sea has no stones. That is why we charge for the berth."
- **Long Mile.** A Long-Mile teller says, "The mother was wrong. There are more stones than days. That is how you know you are on the Long Mile."
- **the Old Ways.** An Old Ways teller says, "The stones were there before the walk. The land was already counting."
- **the Watching.** A Watcher says, "The Tree saw every ribbon. The count was optional."
- **Hands-era children.** A Hands-era child says, "We climb them. There is a good one past the mill. The ribbon is just rag."

Skeptics of every walk tell a short ending. One of them says, "There were not two counts. There were people, and weather, and a grove that would not come to you."

**Variants.**

- On Maiethorn it is a walking-song; the chorus is only numbers.
- On Strandoren the quay-version starts at the berth and never finds a stone until the inland days, which is the joke and the bill.
- On Heskoren some tellers omit the arrival. The child is still counting when the story stops.
- A road-house telling ends on the Held bed, not the grove. Innkeepers think this is the real version.
- Kumbaan does not keep this story. A mainland guest who tells it is offered more supper, which is an answer.

### The Branch That Came Away

The teaching-story of the Cut. Told about the First Cut, and still told in towns that have a Tree and a neighbour that does not. Sharing a Hand requires wounding a Hand, and the first copy invented both the local mercy and the wait.

A branch came away from the Tree.

A person carried it. They watered it. They put it in new ground.

It died.

They carried another. Or they carried the same one, and the tellings do not agree, and it does not change the ground.

It took.

The origin said, "That wood is ours."

The new ground said, "That wood is here."

Both were right, which is why there had to be a paper.

The paper said who may take the next branch. The Tree was not asked. The Tree kept choosing children under both canopies.

A child asked which Tree was the real one.

The mother said, "Count the Hands."

The child counted two, then twenty, then the towns that were not in the count yet.

The child asked who was next.

The mother said, "The soil is thin there."

The child could see the neighbour's canopy from the ford, and could not say, later, whether they had been counting Hands or counting waits.

A Long-Lived who planted will tell you the child's name. They will not tell you which summer. They still will not write who cut.

**The last lines.**

- **Folk (Near Mile).** A Near-Mile singer says, "The mother was the one who cut. The paper came after, like a fine."
- **Clergy.** A Speaker says, "The warden cut with a leave the college had not invented yet. The paper is how we kept the next one alive."
- **Devout.** A devout teller says, "The branch came away. No one stole a Hand. She gave one."
- **the Watching.** A Watcher says, "We have always cut living wood. They named one cut the First. The count is optional."
- **the Old Ways.** An Old Ways teller says, "The ground received a guest. The paper is a mainland word for a meal they did not stay for."
- **the Fair Hand.** A Fair Hand clerk says, "The second carrying was a berth. The first was an unlicensed one. That is the whole hymn."
- **Hands-era children (a Tree-town).** A child in a Tree-town says, "We Turn here. The neighbour walks. The story is why."
- **Hands-era children (still walking).** A child who is still walking says, "We can see the canopy. The story does not plant anything."

Skeptics of every house tell a short ending. One of them says, "There was not a Hand that learned to walk. There was wood, and a pot, and a college that got to the knife late."

**Variants.**

- On Maiethorn it is told at Turning-Week in the Motherwood and in kitchens that keep a knife on a ribbon. The Motherwood version starts at "came away." The kitchen version starts at "took."
- On Strandoren the quay-version never finds a Tree until the hold is opened. The joke is the bill. The bill is not a joke inland.
- On Heskoren some tellers omit the taking. The child is still asking who is next when the story stops. Guest-reading tellers start at the meal. First-reading tellers start at the claiming.
- A Seat telling ends on the paper, not the ford. Speakers think this is the real version.
- Kumbaan does not keep this story. A mainland guest who tells it is offered more supper, which is an answer.

### The Child Who Climbed the Stone

The teaching-story of what the Walk left behind. Told in the Years of Hands about mile-shrines that outlived the requirement, and still told in towns that have a Tree in the square and a neighbour that walks. Neighbours still use the stone as a mile. The canopy in the square is already Turning children.

A town grew a Tree beside the road.

The last stone of the old mile stood in what became the square. Children climbed it. A ribbon stayed on it, or did not. The tellings do not agree, and it does not change the scuff.

A warden said, "It is in the way of the crowd."

A Long-Lived who keeps beds said, "It is a name."

A child of the Tree-town asked how far it was to the Tree.

The mother said, "We are already here."

A child from the hamlets past the ford came for a borrowed week, saw children on the stone, and asked how far it was to the Tree.

The Tree-town mother said, "We are already here."

The waiting child said, "We can see your canopy from the ford."

They climbed together. The waiting child left a ribbon. The Tree-town child could not say, later, whether they had been playing or counting a wait.

A Long-Lived who still makes a bed they will not let will tell you both children's names. They will not tell you which summer. They still will not move the stone, and they still will not say the road is over.

**The last lines.**

- **Tree-town (devout).** A devout teller in a Tree-town says, "The Mother stopped the road when the Hand arrived. The stone is how we remember the waiting."
- **Tree-town (tired).** A tired teller in a Tree-town says, "The Tree is here. The stone is in the way. We climb it because it is the right height."
- **Waiting child / Heskoren.** A waiting child on Heskoren says, "We can see the canopy. The story does not plant anything."
- **Lestrand.** A Lestrand clerk says, "The beds upstairs are let. The stone is a mile-mark the clerks have not billed yet."
- **Innkeeper.** An innkeeper says, "The stone is the upper room. We did not let it. That is the whole hymn."
- **the Watching.** A Watcher says, "The stone was already a stone. The Tree saw the climb. The count is optional."
- **the Old Ways.** An Old Ways teller says, "The land was already counting. The square is a guest's furniture."
- **Road-hand.** A Road-hand says, "I water at it. I sleep in the town that was not chosen. I do not say queue."
- **Hands-era children (who have never walked).** A Hands-era child who has never walked says, "There is a good one in the square. The ribbon is just rag."

Skeptics of every house tell a short ending. One of them says, "There were not two roads. There was a stone, and a Tree, and a child who still had to walk."

**Variants.**

- On Maiethorn it is told as a square-game; the chorus is only "already here." The Rain-Shadow telling (Ornsael) omits the Tree-town mother, or makes her wrong.
- On Strandoren the quay-version starts in the loft and never finds a stone until inland, which is the joke and the bill.
- On Heskoren some tellers omit the climb. The waiting child is still asking how far when the story stops. Guest-reading tellers start at the meal under the stone. First-reading tellers start at the claiming of the square.
- A Seat telling ends on the crowd, not the ford. Speakers think this is the real version: the stone should move.
- A White-Note telling ends on the north bed. Juniors think this is the real version: empty is not a shrine.
- Kumbaan does not keep this story. A mainland guest who tells it is offered more supper, which is an answer.

## Pages a party can find

Six short pages sit on shelves a party can visit. Each was written by someone inside the world, in that person's own words, and each is a page or two long. A page can be wrong about the world. Three are in Rothallo: at the school beside the Book's house, in the Book's house itself, and at the gate clerk's desk. Three are on Molevad's long-house shelf in Lestrand, where the Pourers keep their library. People name that building by the house; it has no other name.

### The school primer

*First Lessons at the Close School* is kept at the school beside the Book's house in Rothallo. The children of the Closed houses learn their letters there, and other families inside the walls can send a child if they pay. Volnai, the teacher, works there. Asked about the primer, Volnai says, "This is what the children learn in their first year. A hug is rare. Take a copy if you like; I write out more every summer."

It is the lesson the Close teaches its children, in questions and answers they say back, and Volnai believes every line of it. It teaches that most children receive a colour, that a hug is rare, and that the Tree keeps back a hugged child for something better than a colour. It also teaches the seating at a Closed table, who holds the keys, what happens to a Closed child who receives a colour, and why households with a Condition get the heavier turns of work.

The primer is wrong about the hug. Being hugged is common; the shares are in "Kind and Condition." What is rare is a family like the Closed houses, which has stayed Kept on purpose for generations. The primer gives no numbers of its own.

### A history of the Closing

*The Keeping of the Grove* is kept in the Book's house in Rothallo, on the same shelf as the Book of Tithes. Delamem, who keeps the Book of Tithes, works there. Asked about it, Delamem says, "It is our history of the Seat. You may read it here. The Book's house does not lend."

It is the Closed Heirs' own account of the Closing, and it speaks well of the Closed Seat. It says the gate at the grove kept order at the one Tree, that children turned away at the gate went home unharmed, and that the riders who dug up and burned the new grafts were stewards bringing back stolen wood. It says Hildal, a clerk, chose to keep the Book rather than fight for the grove, and that the Closed families moved a day's walk away into Rothallo, in the country that became Orenbren.

The facts on the page match the history above. The war ran from Cut-year 19 to Cut-year 38; the writer counts those years from the cutting and calls them the nineteenth and the thirty-eighth year. The page does not name who made the First Cut, and it does not put a year on the Tree. What it leaves out is the cost. It does not say that children walked the whole Long Mile and went home UnTurned because the gate refused them, or that people died on the roads. It calls a child sent home "Kept" and "whole," as if that were no loss.

### Minutes from the long-house

*Minutes of a Gift* is kept on Molevad's long-house shelf in Lestrand. It is one meeting copied out of the minute-book. Molevad keeps the minute-book and the key to the shelf. Asked about it, Molevad says, "This is one meeting. K. said it could be read. The rest of the book stays shut."

These are the circle's own minutes of one willing gift: one year, the fifth that a Long-Lived member called K. gives. The circle asks five years of a member in all, one year on each night, and K. is on the last. K. asks to give that year, names it, and says why: K. is tired of carrying a husband's death. The circle asks three times whether the gift is K.'s own wish. K. gives the year into the cup with one drop of blood. Afterward K. still knows the husband's name and face from before the marriage and remembers nothing from that year. K.'s papers from that year go onto the shelf, and the minutes end with the long-house seating K. one year younger, five in all. The minutes record how the circle will describe its god this decade, that one reading held two members' memories of the same summer, and that nobody asked to stop. They end with the circle's claim that every gift is freely given and that the circle made the god it gives to. That claim is the circle's belief. The minutes do not settle whether it is true.

### A scene from a play

*The Heir's Seat* is kept on Molevad's long-house shelf as a copy in the common speech, made by members of Molevad's circle. Molevad holds the key. Asked about it, Molevad says, "People borrow it to put on in a yard at Turning-Week. Bring it back when you're done."

It is a scene players can perform straight from the page, with a table, a tall chair, and a lantern. No theatre is needed. Two cousins of a Closed house were Turned in the same week. One was hugged. The other received a storm-grey leaf. A clerk of the Book strikes the Given cousin from the heir-roll and writes their name again on the page of turns of work. The Given cousin is sent to the far end of the table. The hugged cousin is given the heir's chair and a gate key.

The scene does not say which cousin got the better life. The hugged cousin keeps the seat and has to stay inside the walls and hold the keys. The Given cousin loses the seat, owes heavier work, and can walk out of the gate any morning. The play ends on a question nobody in it answers.

### A poem

*A Room I Do Not Go Into* is kept on Molevad's long-house shelf. The copy has no name on it; it was written by a Long-Lived writer. Asked about it, Molevad says, "One of us wrote it. Read it."

The poem is about a person who has five summers they no longer remember, and a room they walk past and do not open. People who read it often ask whether it is about the cup, where the Pourers give years of memory away one year at a time. If anyone asks Molevad, or any other Long-Lived person at the shelf, whether the poem is about the cup, that person will not say whether it is.

### A road song

*The Day-Road Count* is kept at the gate clerk's desk at Rothallo's gate, written on the back of an old sleep-line sheet. Breolnir, the gate clerk, who writes down who slept in the beds outside the wall, works there. Asked about it, Breolnir says, "The school children sing it on the road to the wood. Pilgrims keep asking for the words, so I keep a copy."

It is a counting song the school beside the Book's house teaches children to sing as they walk the day's road from Rothallo to the Awakening Tree. It counts the Kept the way the Close teaches: at every town Tree on the road, and even under the Awakening Tree, every child gets a colour and nobody is hugged. Only at the end, back at Rothallo's wall, does the song find a hug.

The song is wrong. A tree-warden sees children hugged at every Leaf-Fall, in every town, and under the Awakening Tree itself, and a warden who hears children singing it on the road can tell it is wrong. The song gives no numbers.
