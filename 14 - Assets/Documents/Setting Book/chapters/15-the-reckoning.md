# The Reckoning of the Year

The Turning keeps time by the sun. The Leaf-Fall, the week when the Trees turn their colour and the year's ten-year-olds stand under them, falls at a solstice, so the count is also how people know when children Turn, when the great festival falls, and how a world with mechanical clocks and no printing press numbers its days. The sky here is enough to run the rite and to date a debt.

### The sky

There is one sun, and on the mainland one moon, on a familiar monthly round that is enough for nights and for tides. Kumbaan keeps twelve named moons and does not owe that mainland round a translation. They are the same sky, counted differently. There are four true seasons, and the winters are real. People stand on one ground. The storm-wall is weather, current, and reef, and it is not a door. The hills at Ndenjoo are hills people live in. A Tree is a tree on this ground, and a person who has become is still standing on this ground. The Low Wall, the Seeing-Ring, and the Dry Stair are older stone on this same ground, not a ruined heaven you can walk into. People reach a place by walking or by sail. What a faith calls a door, people keep as a practice, a meal, a return, or a hymn.

The fact that matters for the rite is the **solstice**, because that is when the Trees turn.

At the High Solstice, the longest day, a Tree's shadow at noon sits almost on its own roots. Supper happens in light that should have been gone, and Turning-Week is that whole week of light. A storm can soak the crowd. The weather does not extend the week or move it off the sun's day. On the Rain-Wall the sun clears a shelf it leaves in shadow in the Deep, and the snow-line looks further off than it is. On the Old Crossing the day is long, and a squall in Hale-month, when the land is supposed to be sound and the roads are full, can still take the sail. On Heskoren the same long light falls on a canopy, a bog, or neither, depending on the fold. People argue about what the light did. They are not arguing about a map of stars. Believers say the Mother timed her choosing to the year's hinge, and skeptics say a tree answers light, but both stand in the same noon and see the same short shadow.

At the Deep Solstice the sun is a guest. It rises late, stays low, and is soon gone, so the work moves to the hearth and the roads shorten to daylight. The mainland moon is the light you actually use, when cloud allows, and the deathless houses finish closing their accounts in this dark. A clear Deep night shows the moon and a great many unnamed lights. People do not farm by those lights. They farm by the months.

At Ndenjoo, on a bright night, the moon is the daylight, and you can carry a bowl across the hill-yard without a lamp. The storm-wall is a darker ring around that brightness, reef-thunder under the cloud at the edge and the hill itself lit. The bright moon washes the small lights out, so a person here does not steer by a field of named stars. They say which moon it is. Njunda, who keeps the hall's table, will say the moon's name. There is no Leaf-Fall in that sky, and no Tree to throw a noon shadow. The long mainland day is a sailor's story here. The bright night is the ordinary one.

The Leaf-Fall happens at the solstice because that is when the Trees turn their colour and choose. The sun reaches its turning, and so do the children. The devout and the skeptics share that calendar.

### The Turning Year

The year hinges on **two solstices**, and those two dates are the fixed anchors everyone can see.

The **Deep Solstice**, at midwinter, is the longest night, the low of the year and its turning point. The season around it belongs to the hearth, to endowment-giving, and to the deathless houses' closing of their accounts.

The **High Solstice**, at midsummer, is the longest day, and it is **the Turning**. This is the great one: the solstice when the Trees turn and the year's ten-year-olds meet the Leaf-Fall.

The High Solstice is warm, safe to travel and to gather, and full of light. A child's Turning is a joyous festival, so midsummer suits it, and that same warmth makes the pilgrimage to Thaeloren a summer road.

### Turning-Week

The Leaf-Fall is not a single day. It runs through **Turning-Week**, the week that brackets the High Solstice, when the Trees hold their colour and the choosing is done. Every child who has turned ten since the last High Solstice Turns during this week, or misses it. The town gathers, the tree-wardens officiate, and the week is at once the holiest rite and the biggest fair of the year, part christening, part graduation, and part festival. A city nests that witness among the people who already know the child. A village stands as one circle. The year of a person's Turning becomes a lifelong marker of age and of cohort, which is what people mean when they say, "We Turned the same summer."

### Months and weeks

The calendar is plainly countable. Twelve months follow the moon's cycle, arranged as four seasons of three months each, hinged on the two solstices, with the two equinoxes between them kept as the lesser markers. The week is the working unit of the guild towns: guild-weeks, market-days, and the mechanical clock-towers of the trade cities striking the hours. There is no printing press, so clergy and clerks keep the calendars by hand, which is one reason the tree-wardens and the Tallied Crown's clerks hold a quiet power. They are the ones who keep the dates.

#### The twelve Maiethren months

The liturgical names are Maiethren, the old speech of Maiethorn. Beside them sits the worn common-tongue form, the way Lestrand says the same month. The name a person uses shows the stance they are taking. The table is counted **from the Deep Solstice**, as an astronomical order. The argument about which day opens the year comes after it.

### When the year starts: three new-year days by stance

The devout and the clergy begin the year at **Eolthael**, because for them the Turning is the hinge that matters. Lestrand and the deathless houses begin it at **Nethael**, because midwinter is when the books close. In Vaethorn the pulpit follows the clergy, and the fields follow the last summer a local Tree actually managed a fall, so a child "turns the year" from that summer. The twelve names do not change. Any of the three days can open the year, and any of the three can open a dispute about what year a debt belongs to. A debt can be late on one count and early on another, and people will argue which day began it.

### Kumbaan's moons

The Yumboes of Kumbaan do not use the mainland table. They keep **twelve named moons**, a lunar count that owes the solstice nothing:

**Yaaba · Ndibi · Sambiyo · Waandi · Lunji · Saalo · Soonke · Nkoosa · Ndenjoo · Yendo · Njili · Mbela**

*Saalo* is a moon, and Seed-month is a mainland month. A mainlander who points up in Saalo and calls it Seed-month has put the moon in the wrong quarter of the year. Njunda will say the moon's name. The hall will not correct the chart. It corrects the visitor by still being on its own moon when the mainlander's month has moved on. Soonke, in that list, is one of the twelve moons. The sand below Ndenjoo is also called Soonke, because Soonke is the person who sits that stretch.

### How years are numbered

Months name a season inside the year, and **years have no universal number**. The count written on a debt is a theology, the same argument as which day opens the year, turned now toward history. The Ages chapter keeps the full count.

The First Seat and the devout clerks count **Cut-years** from the First Cut, the first time living wood was successfully cut and carried. In the liturgy that count is the *Eoloren-count*. The present year is **Cut-year 387**.

Lestrand and the deathless houses count from a house-founding or from the issue of a note. A clerk of the White Note writes the present year as "the 307th summer of the White Note." The famous problem of turning one count into the other is the archival **200th**. Cut-year 280 was that house's 200th summer, and Ledan, the Long-Lived clerk who still writes its quay-desk, asked the First Seat which number a hundred-year loan should carry, because the bank, the borrower's town, and the Seat were not counting the same summer. The house still converts its years the way that query did. The Ages chapter keeps the letter.

Threnmaieth writes the Cut-years and also a crown-count taken from the Two Papers. The papers fall in Cut-year 67, and the present is **Crown-year 320**.

Vaethorn counts from the year the local graft took. People there say, "the year our graft took," which is the same local-fall logic as the way they start the year.

The Watching refuse the Cut-count entirely. Kumbaan keeps its moons and owes the ages nothing.

The Awakening Tree is old beyond reliable dating. There is no year the world began, and no year she appeared.

### How it is lived along the gradient

One sun, one moon, and the two solstices make the count the same sky on the mainland. People live it differently as the wood thins, from a country where Turning-Week is an institution to the one country that has no Trees.

On **Maiethorn**, where the reach is full, Turning-Week is a near-certainty and a vast institution. The Trees stand dense and healthy, the core runs higher on the Given than the world as a whole, and the pilgrimage to Thaeloren crests at the High Solstice. When that walk was required, it was the Walking Years. The Near Mile is the inland trip on Maiethorn, days to a couple of weeks. The Ages chapter tells those years.

On **Strandoren**, where the reach is high, the solstice is kept, and it is as much a commercial hinge as a sacred one. The fairs, the contract-terms, and the deathless houses' own reckoning all turn on it. The Salt Walk was the berth that made Eolthael. Miss the last crossing, and you missed the year.

On **Heskoren**, where the reach is thin, Turning-Week is a hard-won event of a decade. A child may travel days to the nearest Turning Tree, or wait years for a graft to take. The solstice still arrives, and the gift may not fall. Whole districts are still on the Long Mile, weeks or months to a Tree that can Turn them.

On **Kumbaan**, where there is no reach, the Yumboes keep the moon and not the solstice. Their reckoning is nocturnal and lunar, and it owes the Trees nothing, because they have none. They are the one people in the world whose calendar has no Leaf-Fall in it. A mainlander who lines a moon up with a Maiethren month is wrong in the same direction every time, and the hall will not correct the chart.

### What the year feels like

The year is kept by stance and argued by faith, and the two do different work. A place sets how long people talk and what they repeat. A person's faith sets which words they will not say. That is why someone of the Watching can eat on a dock, and why a follower of the Fair Hand can stand in a pious square. Each street chooses the words. Each street also keeps one habit, and the people who keep it will not explain it.

In a devout town, and in any square that puts the name back when a sentence dropped it, the year people feel begins in Eolthael, whatever a clerk's tablet started earlier. On an ordinary market morning, people carrying baskets stop when a tree-warden steps onto the Tree's roots, and they start again when the warden steps off. Someone of the Watching will not say "Maieth," and will still wait. The wood is the wood. Someone of the Fair Hand, passing through, will not wait, and the square notices without stopping them. In Maiethlir a clerk may be copying a slate of bells in the same hour, and the hymn is still first when Rithim, the Speaker, can make it first. The Core-thaw runs louder the week before Leaf-Fall than it did in Hale-month. Maiethlir will not say why.

On a dock, and on any quay that lets the *th* go, the year the account books feel begins at the Deep Solstice, when the notes are closed. Turning-month is a fair laid on top of that closing, and it is not the start of the quay's own count. All year, a **clear** is a no-colour, a child the Tree hugged and gave no Condition, and the quay says the word the way it says a berth that just opened. A crane-crew will hire a clear. Nobody on this water calls a clear a sadness. Even a house that never kept a book will count coins into the other person's hand, not onto the barrel, when the one they owe is watching. A book-hand calls that worship. The gang calls it how you fail to start a fight. They only collide when a child in the tenth year is present. In Eolthael, salt crusts the roots of the Trees, and on Orentel's rise the tide does not reach the salt. Orentel will not say why.

In a waiting town, and in any town whose sentence does not close the week, people date a child from the last summer the local wood actually spoke. The pulpit may say Eolthael. The field says the last real fall. They do not spend the good flour on a promise that the wood will speak again. A guest gets the common pot. If the wood spoke last year, Turning-Week gets the better loaf. If it did not, the week gets the pot too. Some houses walk the old doors at a flood or at the ice and leave food, and they do not call that a second Turning. A wet leaf lies on the spring, or too many, or none. Eolvaeth will not say why. At Harrow's Green, in a year when the wood will speak, people say "the wood" first, and the neighbour after that. If their own wood will not speak, the sentence in the street is "Not this week," and they name the neighbour's town when they have to send the children on.

At Ndenjoo the year is moons. Noon is when the hall is asleep, so a tool you need at noon waits, or you stay quiet enough that the sleepers stay asleep. A mainlander who lines a moon up with a Maiethren month is wrong in the same direction every time, and the hall will not correct the chart. On one night of the moon sailors call Yoltal, the hall eats the heel of the loaf first. Every other night the heel is last, or it goes to the goats. The valley says the hands like the crust when the mainland is loud. Njunda will not correct that, and will not say it is the reason. They will not say why. A guest who asks is given more bread.

Turning-Week, as a town spends it, is baskets, bells, a berth, and a loaf. The leaf-colours, and the questions a warden gets asked, belong to the rite at the Tree.

In Maiethlir the stalls go up under the town Tree on the first morning, and the hearths stand to the bell in the morning, again in the afternoon, and again at eve. The public slate names the street and the hour. The colour a child received stays off that slate. Rithim speaks the word of the fall aloud even when the square already knows the common fall, and the next errand waits until the people who stood have stepped off the root. A Watcher household stands with their child and says their own line, quietly, and Maieth is not in it. A follower of the Fair Hand stands at the edge with a book held shut. Before the fair eats, someone in from the road may set a bowl at the root and go back to a stall. The square lets the bowl sit. Setting it down is not the standing.

On Orentel's quay they say "the fall" on the crane, and they call the wood Tallo, though a charter that is open still carries the long name. The standings follow the quay-gang, the people who already know the child, and they happen at the tide that gang can spare. A clear gets the same shout the quay uses when a berth opens. Salt crusts the roots, and the quay will not explain it. In the same days the book-hands settle a year's term where the gang can see. If a Bound's oath-day falls in the week, the oath is recited and marked, and the warden still speaks. The quay calls the Mother "the old rite," and it does not put the *th* back into the word to be polite.

In Eolvaeth the town fills before the week arrives. In Hale-month the camp-streets double, and when Eolthael comes the town faces the canopy it has, which some years speaks and some years does not. If the wood will call a fall, the town stands small and the gift-hall pot is open. A hug is not dressed as a prize, and a sermon may still pity the person who received one. The pot does not wait for the sermon to finish. If the wood will not speak, people in the street say "Not this week," and a cohort may be sent toward a luckier Tree. The spring has a wet leaf, or too many, or none, and the town will not explain it. In those same days some houses walk the old doors, leave food, and do not ask for a colour.

The hill on Kumbaan keeps its moons through the week the mainland calls Turning-Week, so no child is stood up and no one asks for a colour. A guest who brought a tenth-year is fed, and nobody offers the path to a Tree, because there is no Tree in the valley. The loaf comes heel-first, and they will not explain the heel. The shore does not come up the path to make a rite, and the sand stays the sand.
